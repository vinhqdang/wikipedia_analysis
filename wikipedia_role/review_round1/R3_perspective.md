### Summary Assessment
The essay recasts the "Wikipedia as source of truth" claim as a claim about auditability (provenance, contestability, answerability) and then asks what reusers owe a practice that sustains that property and what Wikipedia owes in return. I find the reframing the most valuable move: it is consistent with feminist and social-epistemological accounts of why Wikipedia's authority is procedural, and the reflexive Section 5, which refuses to treat a claim on others as independent of the community's own fairness, is rare in this literature. The limits are stated honestly. My concerns are about whose practice, whose duty and by what route. "The community" is treated as a single carrier of the practice, so Foundation, affiliates, non-English editions and volunteers with different interests are not distinguished. The duty is placed on "AI developers" and "reusers" while Google and search, the largest and longest-standing reuser and the one the Foundation itself blames for the traffic decline, appears only in one objection paragraph. Licensing alternatives are not weighed. Section 5's recommendations are directionally sound but lack actors and costs, and one of them would load more work onto the volunteers whose labour the essay calls the binding constraint. The philosophical apparatus is borrowed from political obligation where commons governance and epistemic-injustice scholarship would fit better. I recommend major revision, mainly rewriting and re-scoping and not new empirical work.

### Strengths
1. **S1 Auditability as the defended property**: The shift from truth to provenance, contestability and answerability matches Wikipedia's own policy and links to feminist critique of the project's epistemology. Evidence Anchor: text: §2 "what deserves defence is a practice of verification carried by a community" (Abstract, §2 passim).
2. **S2 Section 5 makes the claim on others conditional on the practice's own fairness**: This internalises the critique and avoids a one-way "AI firms must pay" argument. Evidence Anchor: text: §5 "The claim ... is only as strong as the practice is open, contestable and fair to those who join it."
3. **S3 Uses the feminist and indigenous-knowledge critiques of the notability regime structurally, not decoratively**: Tripodi, Ford and Wajcman, Gallert and van der Velden, and Menking and Rosenberg are tied to the argument that the legitimating rules produce the gaps. Evidence Anchor: text: §2 "These gaps follow from the rules that give the bastion its legitimacy".
4. **S4 Disciplined empirical modesty and disclosure of own-pull data**: Evidence Anchor: absence: none; section-level anchor §1 and §6 — limits stated in "The essay does not claim that Wikipedia is failing" and the "Three limits" paragraph.
5. **S5 Concrete, checkable observation about Enterprise's data model (provenance travels, contest does not)**: A practical, underused point. Evidence Anchor: text: §5.1 "the data dictionary lists no field for talk-page discussion or dispute history".

### Weaknesses

### W1: Reusers other than AI developers, and the search-engine relationship, are sidelined
**Problem**: The duty is built around language-model developers, but the strain the essay documents (traffic loss) is attributed by the Foundation to search engines and answer boxes, and the essay's own §6 concedes the principle generalises. Google and other search firms are the oldest and largest reusers, were among the first Enterprise customers (to my recollection Google and the Internet Archive in 2022; please verify), and are the route by which most readers meet Wikipedia (McMahon et al. 2017 is cited but used only to defend generality). Also missing: voice assistants, non-commercial and open-weight model builders (Common Crawl, academic corpora), Wikidata/knowledge-graph reusers, and the Commons image corpus, whose licensing and scraping dynamics differ from text. A duty on open-source AI builders could harm the open ecosystem Wikimedia values.
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §6 "If reliance and strain ground a duty, search engines, teachers and readers owe something too, in proportion."
**Why it matters**: The framing is at once too narrow (one class of reuser) and, for the same reason, too lenient on the actor whose behaviour most plausibly causes the measured harm. Readers will ask why LLM firms are singled out when the Foundation's own diagnosis points at search.
**Suggestion**: Add a short typology of reusers (search/answer engines; commercial model developers; open and academic model builders; downstream data reusers; mirrors and apps) with reliance, capacity and strain for each, using Vincent, Johnson and Hecht's work on Wikipedia's value to search as a starting point. State explicitly whether the proposed requests (credit, terms for heavy use, disclosure, participation support) differ by type, and how a non-commercial builder is treated.
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 4 — core expertise: Wikipedia/search interdependence

### W2: "The community" is treated as a single unit and the Foundation-volunteer-affiliate structure is under-analysed
**Problem**: The essay uses "the community" as carrier of the practice, as decision-maker ("Only the community and its stewards can decide them"), and as beneficiary of reuser support. In Wikimedia governance these are separate: English Wikipedia's editors, hundreds of other language communities, Wikimedia Commons and Wikidata communities, affiliates and chapters (some of which hold their own funds and data partnerships, e.g. Wikimedia Deutschland), the Foundation staff and Board, and a large population of readers and unregistered editors. Their interests diverge on AI: English Wikipedia voted to restrict LLM content, while Foundation product work and some smaller communities see AI as a way to fill gaps. The essay notes that money to the Foundation does not reach editors (§4) but then recommends the community "decide" without saying through which body. The established critique that peer-production communities develop oligarchic tendencies (Shaw and Hill) is not engaged, though §5.2's gatekeeping argument is a version of it.
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §5 "Only the community and its stewards can decide them."
**Why it matters**: A recommendation addressed to an undifferentiated community cannot be actioned, and the essay's legitimacy test (a practice "open, contestable and fair") applies to who speaks for the community as much as to newcomer treatment.
**Suggestion**: Add a paragraph mapping actors and decision channels (English Wikipedia RfC, Foundation Board and annual plan, Movement Charter bodies, affiliates, individual language editions) and indicate for each recommendation which channel could carry it. Acknowledge the heterogeneity of editor attitudes to AI and that the six-expert and news-outlet evidence in §3.3 is an English-dominant and partly Foundation-adjacent sample.
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 4 — core expertise: Wikimedia governance

### W3: Global South and smaller editions appear as a late case study, and the remedy risks re-inscribing the rule that creates the gap
**Problem**: The scope is English Wikipedia, with Vietnamese brought in at the end of §5.3 as a measurement exercise. Three things are missing. First, LLM quality for low-resource languages depends disproportionately on small editions, many of them heavily bot-generated (the essay's own Cebuano and Waray figures), so the "reliance on curation and quality" argument of §3.2 inverts: models may be trained on the least curated editions. Second, the harms of reuse are likely distributed unequally: small communities have fewer people to police machine text and less leverage to demand terms. Third, §5.3 proposes that community-held knowledge enter "by procedure", for example "through published research by those who record it", which still routes oral and indigenous knowledge through the published-source requirement that Gallert and van der Velden identify as the problem. Indigenous data sovereignty (CARE principles) holds that some knowledge should not be openly reusable at all, which conflicts with the CC BY-SA openness the essay takes as given and with the idea that widening the commons is uniformly good. The essay is also written from, and about, a Western liberal conception of a "commons".
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §1 "The focus is the English-language edition."
**Why it matters**: The essay's own diagnosis (gaps follow from the legitimating rules) is a Global South argument; leaving it as a coda weakens it and leaves open the charge that the paper reproduces the centre it criticises.
**Suggestion**: Either (a) re-scope the title and abstract to "English Wikipedia" explicitly, or (b) add a subsection on smaller editions and AI: who bears the cost, who is trained on, whose consent counts. Engage CARE and indigenous data sovereignty, and discuss whether the "published research" route is adequate or whether community-held knowledge needs a governance route that does not depend on print, and whether some of it should be able to opt out of open reuse.
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 4 — core expertise: Wikipedia geography and knowledge equity

### W4: Licensing alternatives to the status quo are not weighed
**Problem**: §3.1 shows convincingly that CC BY-SA is silent on training, then §4 treats this as fixed and moves to voluntary requests. It does not discuss what the movement could do on the licence and terms side: share-alike or copyleft conditions for model weights or outputs; behavioural-use or "responsible AI" licences; non-commercial or dual-licensing options for future contributions (existing contributions are irrevocably CC BY-SA, so a change would apply only to new content and would need consent, which may be practically unattainable); technical access conditions (identification, rate limits, authenticated APIs) under the Foundation's terms of use, which do not depend on copyright; or opt-out signals beyond CC Signals. It also does not explain why the movement might reject these (free-knowledge values, the free/open definition, the risk of licence fragmentation, enforcement cost after *Doe v. GitHub*). Without this, the policy menu appears to be limited to requests that bind no one.
**Evidence Anchor**: absence: §3.1 and §4 — expected discussion of licence-design and terms-of-use options (copyleft for models, behavioural-use licences, future-content relicensing, authenticated access); checked §3.1, §4, §5.4, §6
**Why it matters**: The question "what should AI developers owe" is partly a question about instruments. Reviewers from the open-licensing and Wikimedia communities will expect the essay to say why a licence change is or is not a realistic lever.
**Suggestion**: Add a short subsection comparing instruments (copyright licence, terms of use and access control, technical signals, contract via Enterprise, regulation) on enforceability, fit with free-knowledge values, community consent required and effect on non-commercial users. Even a conclusion that licence change is unrealistic would strengthen the case for the soft instruments the essay prefers.
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 3 — adjacent field: licensing law and policy, strong on Wikimedia licensing debates

### W5: Wikimedia Enterprise is treated as a funding channel, not as a contested political object
**Problem**: Enterprise appears as revenue ($8.3M, 4.0%) and as a data model, and the 30% cap and "forever free" promise are reported from the Foundation's own blog. The essay does not address the long-standing community concerns about Enterprise (creation of a commercial arm and a two-tier access structure, limited community consultation at launch, the relation to the Foundation's earlier unpopular projects, and whether payment for priority access rewards the largest reusers who already take the content at scale). It also uses Foundation communications as the main source for traffic and bot figures without noting the Foundation's institutional interest in the narrative of "AI harm" that supports its fundraising and Enterprise offer.
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §3.2 "named further partners without terms"
**Why it matters**: Section 5.4 asks the Foundation to open its books, which is appropriate, but leaves the reader unaware that this is a live dispute and that the Foundation is a party with its own incentives.
**Suggestion**: Add one paragraph on Enterprise's reception inside the movement (Meta-Wiki discussion, Signpost coverage) and flag the Foundation-as-interested-source point where Foundation figures are used. Consider asking whether a pooled arrangement under community governance (§4) is compatible with an Enterprise run by the Foundation.
**Severity**: Minor
**Confidence**: 3 — core expertise: Wikimedia politics; I have not verified current Enterprise terms

### W6: Section 5 is not yet actionable or realistic for the movement
**Problem**: The recommendations (publish how notability and deletion fall across subject categories; publish newcomer survival alongside machine-text measures; pair rejection with a named human response; carry contest history in Enterprise feeds; publish reuse-agreement terms) are plausible, but: (a) none names the actor or process that could adopt it; (b) none is costed or sequenced; (c) "pair rejection with a named human response" would add labour to the same small group whose capacity the essay calls the binding constraint, and the Teahouse evidence shows an invited human host helps but the trial counts invitations and not participation (331 participants), so scaling is unknown; (d) categorising deletion outcomes by subject gender or ethnicity requires inferring demographics of living biography subjects, which raises privacy and community-acceptability concerns the essay does not mention; (e) the newcomer-survival and retention metrics partly already exist in Foundation tooling, so the recommendation should say what is new; (f) carrying the dispute history into reuse feeds raises questions of privacy of editors and of whether talk-page content, which is volunteer-authored and sometimes harassing or personal, should be redistributed by default. The essay also treats 5.3's "explicit, documented procedures" as achievable without noting that the community's revisions to core policy are slow and contested (Menking and Rosenberg themselves report punishment of challengers).
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §5.2 "pair rejection with a named human response, as the Teahouse did"
**Why it matters**: The section is the essay's contribution to practice. As written it reads as a statement of aims, which Wikimedians will find easy to agree with and impossible to act on.
**Suggestion**: For each recommendation add a short table: proposer/venue, first step achievable in six months, resource cost and who bears it, main risk, and what would count as success. Distinguish what the Foundation can do alone (Enterprise data fields, publishing terms), what needs community consensus, and what needs affiliates. Address editor privacy for talk-page redistribution. Propose a small pilot for the newcomer-response idea, with a measure that includes volunteer load.
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 4 — core expertise: Wikimedia policy process

### W7: The theoretical borrowing is imported from political obligation and leaves out the literatures built for this object
**Problem**: The duty argument draws on Hart, Rawls, Nozick, Simmons, Singer and Kant, and the essay itself calls the extension to an epistemic commons "its own" step. The step is made against an individualist, state-oriented tradition (obligation of persons to institutions, beneficence between persons) and applied to corporate reusers and a voluntary association. Commons governance (Ostrom's design principles: monitoring, graduated sanctions, nested governance, recognition of the right to organise; Benkler on commons-based peer production) already supplies conditions for sustained provision, treats rules and not licences as the safeguard (the essay cites Hess and Ostrom once), and speaks directly to who must be inside the monitoring and sanction structure. Feminist and social epistemology (Longino's transformative criticism, Haraway's situated knowledge, Fricker's epistemic injustice) give the essay's own §2 and §5.3 their theory: contestability is Longino's "criticism" norm, and the rule that excludes oral knowledge is a testimonial and hermeneutical injustice. Care ethics and relational accounts would handle "a duty to a practice" better than Singer's drowning-child analogy, which presumes an isolated rescuer and a clear victim. Cicero's lamp and Mauss's gift are used as illustration; Mauss's gift is among groups with ongoing relations and is the weakest fit. The tangent on technology panics (Orben, Sparrow et al.) is psychology of child screen time and a failed replication of one memory effect, and bears little on the credibility argument.
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §3.3 "The step from these accounts to an epistemic commons is the essay's own and is open to the weak points above"
**Why it matters**: The weakest part of the argument, by the author's admission, is also where a better-fitting literature exists. This is a borrowing opportunity and a risk of "borrowing badly".
**Suggestion**: Keep a compressed version of the fair-play argument (its mapping to "participants accept restrictions" is apt) but reconstruct the duty through commons governance and epistemic-injustice scholarship, or at least show they converge. Reduce or cut the technology-panics and Cicero detours. See reading list.
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 4 — core expertise: commons and feminist STS; adjacent: moral philosophy

### W8: A moral-duty framing omits structural and policy routes, and may be too lenient on the actors with most leverage
**Problem**: The essay asks for "light, proportionate upkeep" in forms "of their choosing" and calls the harm modest. Several things sit outside this: (a) regulation (transparency and copyright-policy obligations on general-purpose model providers, text-and-data-mining opt-out regimes in the EU, and similar debates elsewhere [UNVERIFIED search leads: EU AI Act Article 53 and the training-content summary template; DSM Directive Article 4]); (b) public or philanthropic funding of the commons as infrastructure (public-library analogy; digital public goods); (c) critical data studies framings of extraction, data colonialism and "free labour" under which volunteer production under an open licence is a political economy question and not only a fairness one; (d) data-leverage tactics available to contributors and communities (collective bargaining, data strikes, restrictions) which the essay's "light duty" framing neither endorses nor rules out. By stipulating that proportionate, self-chosen contribution is the answer, the essay may lend moral cover to minimal gestures by firms whose capacity is vast, while the sources it cites (Longpre et al.) show the commons closing in the absence of enforceable terms.
**Evidence Anchor**: absence: §4 and §6 — expected public-policy, regulatory and collective-leverage routes beside voluntary contribution; checked §3.1, §4, §5, §6
**Why it matters**: Whether the duty framing is judged lenient or realistic depends on the alternatives, which are not shown. The conditional, modest principle is defensible but should be placed within the wider set of levers.
**Suggestion**: Add a short section positioning the moral argument as one lever among regulatory, contractual, technical and collective ones, and say why the essay focuses on the moral. Consider whether "light" is a conclusion of the argument or an assumption about acceptable demands, and say what would increase the duty (for example, evidence of a measurable decline in newcomers attributable to reuse).
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 3 — adjacent field: policy and law; core: critical data studies

### Detailed Comments

#### Assumption Audit
- **Explicit assumptions**: Practice, not truth, is what is worth defending; reliance plus strain can ground a light duty. Both are stated and argued. The conditional form in §3.3 is careful.
- **Implicit assumptions**: (1) That "the community" is a coherent bearer of the practice and of any decision (W2). (2) That openness and CC BY-SA are values shared across all contributor communities, including indigenous and small-language ones (W3). (3) That the traffic decline is the relevant cost to the practice, whereas the essay's own §5 says the binding constraint is newcomers; the link between traffic, newcomers and reuse is asserted more than shown (§3.2 "the people who might have joined the practice never arrive"). (4) That a generated answer lacks a route to contest by default; some assistants cite sources and can be corrected by users, and the essay concedes this only in §6. (5) That the Foundation's diagnosis is neutral evidence.
- **Paradigmatic assumptions**: A liberal, individual-duty frame for a commons problem (W7, W8); an English-language, text-centred conception of Wikipedia.

#### Cross-Disciplinary Connections
- **Parallel research**: Peer-production and commons governance (Ostrom; Benkler; Shaw and Hill on oligarchy); Wikipedia-search interdependence (Vincent and colleagues); infrastructure and bots as governance (Geiger and Ribes); data leverage; indigenous data sovereignty; open-source sustainability debates after the Heartbleed and log4j episodes, an obvious parallel for "who pays for infrastructure everyone uses" [UNVERIFIED as a cited literature; the episodes themselves are well documented].
- **Borrowing opportunities**: Longino's criteria for objectivity map onto the three auditability properties and give them a pedigree; Fricker's epistemic injustice for §5.3; Haraway for the "last bastion of human knowledge" rhetoric the essay already questions; Ford's work on how facts survive in Wikipedia's infrastructure.
- **Methodological borrowing**: Interviews and surveys with editors across editions (the essay notes none exist); document analysis of Meta-Wiki and RfC discussions on AI and Enterprise as a cheap evidence source on community attitudes that would replace the second-hand expert reports; trace-data analysis of newcomer survival by edition.

#### Practical Impact
- **Real-world application**: The most usable ideas are (i) shipping dispute history in Enterprise feeds and (ii) publishing reuse terms. Both are within the Foundation's power. The newcomer and notability recommendations need community processes.
- **Implementation feasibility**: See W6. Risks include volunteer load, editor privacy, demographic inference about subjects, and fragmentation across editions.
- **Stakeholders**: Missing voices are small-edition editors, readers in the Global South who meet Wikipedia mainly through search and zero-rating, affiliates, the Foundation as party, and non-commercial reusers.

#### Broader Implications
- **Ethical dimensions**: Consent of contributors who wrote under a licence before LLMs existed; differential harm across editions; privacy in redistributing talk-page and edit histories; the essay's own point that duty runs to the practice and not to editors is defensible legally but ethically contentious and should be argued against the "unpaid labour" view some editors hold.
- **Social impact**: If reuse thins the newcomer pipeline, the gaps the essay documents (gender, geography, oral knowledge) may widen, since the editors most needed to close them are the least secure in the community.
- **Future directions**: Cross-edition studies of AI and newcomer entry; ethnography of how small communities negotiate AI content; evaluation of a pilot linking generated answers back to talk-page history; analysis of whether Enterprise fees alter reuser behaviour.

### Questions for Authors
1. Whom do you take "the community" to be when you say only it can decide? Which decision channel (RfC on English Wikipedia, Foundation Board and plan, Movement Charter bodies, affiliates) should carry each recommendation, and what if English Wikipedia and a smaller edition disagree about AI reuse?
2. If the Foundation's own account attributes traffic loss to search engines, why is the duty built around language-model developers, and would your four requests apply unchanged to Google, to an open-weight academic model and to a mirror site?
3. Why is changing the terms of use, access controls or the licence for future contributions left out of the options, and would you accept that a movement might reasonably choose it?
4. Your §5.2 asks for a named human response to rejected newcomer edits. Who supplies the labour, and what evidence would show the pilot did not worsen the load it aims to relieve?
5. Does the argument for light duty change if the evidence shows reuse measurably reduces newcomer entry, and what would the essay then recommend?

### Minor Issues
- The title and Abstract say "Wikipedia" while the argument and data are English Wikipedia; either rename or signpost earlier.
- "The last bastion of human knowledge" is quoted in §2 without a source; give the origin or reframe as the essay's own foil.
- §3.3 mixes the "reuse strains the practice" premise with evidence from §3.2 that is explicitly mixed; consider stating what magnitude of strain would trigger the duty.
- The Vietnam analysis (§5.3) is long, dense with figures and personal-query provenance, and sits oddly inside a recommendations section; consider an appendix with the query text.
- Several Foundation blog posts are the only support for central empirical claims; label them as institutional sources in the text.
- §4's "pooled arrangement run by the commons' own governance" needs an example or comparator (for instance open-source foundations, collective management organisations, or the Wikimedia Endowment) to be credible.
- Reference list: Gallert and van der Velden is flagged as an unedited preliminary version; replace with the published version or state clearly in the text.
