## Summary Assessment

The essay argues that Wikipedia's standing is relational rather than a matter of truth, that what deserves defence is a community practice of verification whose distinctive property is auditability (provenance, contestability, answerability), and that language-model developers who rely on this practice have a light, conditional reason to contribute to its upkeep. It closes with four requests to reusers and four recommendations to Wikipedia itself.

The topic fits *Philosophy & Technology* and is timely, and the reframing from "source of truth" to "auditable practice" is the most useful move in the paper. The prose is generally clear, and the essay is unusually frank about what its evidence cannot show. The weaknesses lie in the middle of the paper. The duty argument (Section 3.3) rests on a "strain" premise that the essay itself calls modest and contested, and it lands on a principle so conditional that it is hard to disagree with and hard to use. The auditability thesis is asserted with a coined vocabulary but not tested against retrieval-with-citation systems, or against the essay's own evidence that contestability is weak in practice. Sections 4-5 turn into policy advice drawn from a self-described partial reading. Process notes remain in the text. I recommend major revision: the core is salvageable, but the paper needs one argument, not three.

## Strengths

### S1: A well-chosen reframing of the central question
Moving from "is Wikipedia true?" to "what does Wikipedia let a reader check?" gives a defensible, non-obvious thesis, and it explains why traffic is the wrong health measure for the commons.
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §2 "auditability, not truth, is what makes Wikipedia worth defending, and that it lives in a practice and its people"`

### S2: The "relational credibility" point is clearly argued
The three-place account of credibility (source, purpose, comparison class) is persuasive, and the essay is honest that it is illustrated and not proven. This is the strongest piece of conceptual work in the paper.
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §2 "Credibility is a three-place relation, a source credible for some purpose relative to the alternatives open to the reader"`

### S3: Candour about limits and refusal to overclaim
The essay disclaims claims of failure, illegality or betrayal, and gives the reader the premises it cannot support. For a normative essay on a charged topic this restraint is a real virtue and keeps the argument from becoming advocacy.
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §1 "The essay does not claim that Wikipedia is failing, that reuse of it is unlawful, that AI developers have betrayed its contributors"`

### S4: The comparison with Stack Overflow and Reddit disciplines the "dying commons" narrative
Separating contributors, readers and business model is a useful analytic step, and it sets up the later claim about what a practice needs.
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §1 "'Dying' needs a measure, because contributors, readers and business model do not move together."`

### S5: The licence section correctly separates "silent" from "violated"
The treatment of the CC licence, Creative Commons' own narrow reading and *Doe v. GitHub* is careful and does not lean on a legal claim it cannot support.
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §3.1 "Nothing found here establishes that reuse of Wikipedia text breaches its licence, and the essay does not assume that it does."`

## Weaknesses

### W1: The duty argument depends on a premise the essay itself weakens
**Problem**: The conditional principle requires that reuse "strains the conditions of its upkeep". Section 3.2 calls the cost "modest", "uneven" and "partly contested"; Section 3.3 concedes that the harm is "mild" so the beneficence principle "does not clearly apply"; and Section 6 admits no study shows whether editors expect anything in return or whether the dependence on Wikipedia survives synthetic-data work. What is left is a principle ("have a reason to contribute... proportioned to their reliance and capacity... in forms of their choosing within limits") with almost no determinate content.
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §3.3 "The harm is mild, so the weaker principle does not clearly apply and the duty is correspondingly light."`
**Why it matters**: This is the normative core of the paper and the reason the title question gets an answer. If the antecedent is unestablished and the consequent is "a reason, of some weight, in some form", the argument cannot support Sections 4-5, which read as if the duty were settled.
**Suggestion**: Either (a) strengthen the premise with an explicit account of what counts as "strain" and what evidence would show it, and state plainly what follows if the strain is not present; or (b) recast the claim as a conditional ("if reuse strains the practice, then...") and give the contrapositive its own subsection. Say which developers, doing what, are covered. State which of the three arguments does the work and which is merely supportive; at present the three are stacked rather than integrated, and each is conceded to have a known weak point.
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 4 — core expertise: normative argument in information ethics

### W2: Three papers in one, and Sections 4-5 drift out of philosophical scope
**Problem**: The paper moves from epistemology (§2), to moral philosophy (§3.3), to platform-economics description (§3.2), to institutional recommendations for Wikipedia and the Foundation (§§4-5). Section 5 is a policy brief to a third party, with its own empirical analysis (a Wikidata remeasurement of Vietnamese-edition cultural content and per-editor workload ratios in §5.3), while the Introduction says "The focus is the English-language edition." The paper's own framing concedes the mismatch: the recommendations are "limited by how much of that literature this essay has reviewed" and "Only the community and its stewards can decide them."
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §5 "each is limited by how much of that literature this essay has reviewed. Only the community and its stewards can decide them."`
**Why it matters**: Readers of this journal will expect Section 5 to be argued from the philosophical thesis; instead it is partly a set of reform proposals that do not follow from auditability, and partly an unmotivated empirical aside. The Vietnamese data paragraph (one paragraph of roughly 450 words) is the clearest example, and it uses an ad hoc lower-bound proxy that the text itself says is "not the 2018 measure".
**Suggestion**: Cut §5.3's Vietnamese remeasurement or move it to a separate empirical note. Reduce Section 5 to what follows directly from the auditability thesis (§5.1, parts of §5.2 and §5.4) and show the entailment. If the fair-play argument is meant to make Wikipedia's own openness a precondition of its claim on others, develop that point philosophically, and let it carry §5.
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 4 — core expertise: scope and coherence of journal essays

### W3: Originality and positioning are not established against the relevant literatures
**Problem**: The essay says its three properties "are a working vocabulary for this essay, not terms from the literature", but never shows how auditability differs from transparency, accountability, or the verifiability the Wikipedia policy already names. It cites Fallis (2008) on Wikipedia's epistemology and Hardwig (1985) on epistemic dependence, but the reference list shows no engagement with: the wider social-epistemology and testimony literature on trust in online sources; epistemic injustice (directly relevant to the §2 gaps by gender, geography and oral knowledge); the philosophy of AI-generated text and epistemic trust; commons and peer-production theory beyond Hess and Ostrom (2007) and Huang and Siddarth (2023); or the debate on data/labour compensation for training data. The same holds for the neighbouring *AI & Society* and *Ethics and Information Technology* discussions, from which the manuscript cites only two *AI & Society* items (Vetter et al.; Froneman).
**Evidence Anchor**: `absence: References and §§2-3 — expected engagement with epistemic injustice, AI-and-trust epistemology, commons governance theory and training-data compensation debates; checked reference list, §2, §3.3, §6`
**Why it matters**: Without positioning, an editor cannot tell what is new. The conclusion that Wikipedia "matters as an auditable practice... not as a source of truth" may be a restatement of existing positions.
**Suggestion**: Add a short literature-positioning passage after the Introduction that states, against two or three named positions, what the auditability account adds. Engage epistemic injustice in §2's gaps paragraph and commons governance theory in §4's institutional proposal. Check recent journal literature on generative AI and Wikipedia and on data stewardship before resubmission.
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 3 — adjacent field; judged from the reference list, not a literature search

### W4: Key philosophical sources are used at second hand, and the manuscript says so
**Problem**: Rawls (1999), Kant, and Nozick/Simmons/Klosko are cited or quoted through the *Stanford Encyclopedia* entries (e.g. "as quoted in Dagger & Lefkowitz, 2021, section 4.4"; "Johnson & Cureton, 2022, section 5"). One citation reads "Rawls, 1999, section 19, as summarised in the sources noted in the bibliography", which refers to a bibliography the reader does not have. Mauss is handled "in its standard reading". The conclusion admits "many sources used at the level of abstracts".
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §6 "the evidence base is thin on the essay's central questions, with many sources used at the level of abstracts."`
**Why it matters**: For a paper whose contribution is normative argument, building on encyclopedia summaries invites the objection that the weak points were not tested against the primary texts. The admission about abstracts will undermine reviewer trust in every empirical citation too.
**Suggestion**: Read and cite the primary texts for every argument the paper relies on (Rawls on fair play and natural duty; Simmons; Klosko; Singer's objections and responses). Remove the "sources noted in the bibliography" cross-reference. Delete the abstracts admission once the reading has been done; if some empirical sources cannot be read in full, drop them or flag individual cases.
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 4 — core expertise: scholarly norms in the field

### W5: The auditability thesis is not tested against its obvious counter-cases or against the essay's own evidence
**Problem**: The paper's differentiator, that generated answers lack "by default, a visible route to question" a claim, is stated, not argued. Section 6 allows in one clause that "some systems now cite sources", and nothing further is said about citation-grounded retrieval systems that provide provenance. Meanwhile Section 2 uses Menking and Rosenberg (2021) for the point that authority "can always be questioned" while Section 5.3 reports from the same source that "editors who challenged the pillars have been punished", and Section 2 reports newcomer rejection by quality-control machinery. If contestability is thin in practice, the claim that it is what makes the practice worth defending needs the distinction between formal and effective contestability.
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §2 "A generated answer lacks, by default, a visible route to question it."`
**Why it matters**: This is the single claim on which the essay's answer to its title question rests.
**Suggestion**: Add a section or subsection that sets auditability against (i) retrieval-with-citation systems and (ii) Wikipedia's actual record on contestability, and says what degree of the property is needed for the argument. Define the three properties more sharply, and say how each could be measured; the claim that traffic is "a poor measure" and newcomer entry "better" currently has no support beyond assertion.
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 4 — core expertise: epistemology of information systems

### W6: Repetition, density and unrevised working-process residue
**Problem**: Whole findings are restated nearly verbatim in different sections: Halfaker et al. (§2 and §5.2); Tripodi (2023) and Gallert and van der Velden (§2 and §5.3); the Menking and Rosenberg quotation about "a deeper connection and more transparency" (§2 and §5.1); the Vietnamese 2.5% figure (§2 and §5.3); the Shi et al. diversity point (§2 and §5.3). The Abstract, Introduction (para. 4), §3.3 end, and Conclusion each restate the same summary sentence. There are also process residues: "own pull", "own queries", "read 4 October 2026", "when read", "five editions timed out and are omitted", and the §3.3 reference to the bibliography. Paragraphs are very long (§2 "The walls have gaps"; §5.3 final paragraph).
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §5.3 "five editions timed out and are omitted"`
**Why it matters**: Residue of this kind signals an unfinished manuscript, and the repetition pads the paper while thinning each argument. I estimate the paper at roughly 9,000 words excluding references, which may sit near or over the journal's limit once W2 and W3 are addressed with additions; please check against the current author guidelines.
**Suggestion**: State each finding once, in the section where it does work, and cross-refer elsewhere. Move data-pull details to a short methods note or appendix with reproducible queries, written in the manuscript's register, and remove working notes. Break the longest paragraphs.
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 4 — core expertise: editorial standards

### W7: Abstract and title overstate or misstate what the body establishes
**Problem**: The Abstract says the firms "owe nothing under its licence by way of return"; the body says only that the licence is "silent" and that "nothing found here establishes" breach, which is a weaker, evidential claim. The title's metaphor of a "last bastion" is not used again except to say "'The last bastion of human knowledge' overstates the case", and the essay never says who says it; a straw target in the title is a poor start. The Introduction's question ("what role Wikipedia can and should play") is answered in the conclusion largely through a duty on reusers, which is a different question.
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: Abstract "the firms that train language models on its text owe nothing under its licence by way of return"`
**Why it matters**: A reader of the abstract alone would take away a legal claim the paper declines to make, and the framing question does not match the argument delivered.
**Suggestion**: Reword the abstract to "the licence does not address". Attribute the "last bastion" phrase to a source or drop it as a foil. Make the research question match what is argued (for example: "what, if anything, do those who rely on Wikipedia's practice owe it, and what does it owe in return").
**Severity**: Minor
**Confidence**: 5 — direct textual comparison

### W8: Rhetorical padding in the moral argument
**Problem**: The Cicero lamp passage and the Hawley note on betrayal are elegant but do little work. The lamp argument is used to say the duty attaches to the practice and not the text, which §3.2 had already said. The betrayal note rebuts a claim the essay says no one needs to make.
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §3.3 "A note on betrayal. Reaction to this reuse is often phrased as betrayal, a word the argument does not need."`
**Why it matters**: Space is spent on side arguments while the central argument (W1) is left thin.
**Suggestion**: Keep the non-rival/rival distinction (which is useful) and fold it into §3.2; either source the claim that "reaction... is often phrased as betrayal" or cut the note.
**Severity**: Minor
**Confidence**: 3 — judgement of editorial economy

## Detailed Comments

### Journal Fit
- Scope: good. Knowledge commons, epistemic dependence, and obligations towards digital infrastructure are staple *Philosophy & Technology* concerns, and the essay would also be defensible at *Ethics and Information Technology*. *AI & Society* would take the empirical and policy material more readily, which is a point against the current mix.
- Genre: *Philosophy & Technology* publishes conceptual essays, but expects a philosophical argument at the centre. At present the centre is a conditional principle assembled from three accounts, each flagged as flawed (see W1). The empirical sections are not the problem in themselves, but they cannot stand in for argument.
- Length and style: dense, clear, and with a house style of heavy qualification. Check word limits (W6). The reference list is mostly appropriate, but is weighted to grey literature (Foundation blog posts, news, SEC filings) in the sections that make factual claims, and to encyclopedia entries in the sections that make normative ones.
- Readership: the topic will interest the journal's readers, who will also want engagement with their own literature (W3).

### Originality
- Reframing credibility as relational and then as auditability: partial novelty. Applying fair play, natural duty and beneficence to AI reuse of a commons: a reasonable but expected move, since similar applications exist for open-source and data-sharing. The conclusion that the duty is "light" and the form "of their choosing" leaves little distinctive content. Positioning is needed (W3).

### Significance
- Timely, with a likely audience beyond philosophy. The significance in the philosophical sense depends on whether the paper gives a transferable account of obligations to epistemic commons; the essay explicitly disclaims this as "its own step" open to objections. If W1 and W5 are repaired the paper could say something general; as written it is a case study with a hedged moral.

### Structural Coherence
- Title, Abstract and Introduction promise a role question; Sections 2-3 answer a nature-of-standing question and a duty question; Sections 4-5 answer a policy question. The order is logical, but each stage is thinner than the preceding framing suggests. Sections 2 and 5 repeat each other (W6). The Conclusion's objections section is a good device, but two of the objections ("proves too much", "who bears the duty") are answered in a sentence and deserve the weight of a section, since they bear on W1.

### Title & Abstract
- Title is memorable but the metaphor is not developed (W7). Abstract is a single dense paragraph; it includes a sentence on method that is helpful ("notes where the evidence is thin"). It should not state a legal claim that the body avoids.

### Conclusion
- The conclusion is honest about limits. It should not close on the admission that sources are used at the level of abstracts (W4); that is a problem to fix, not a limitation to report. The third limit also contradicts the framing that the paper is a conceptual essay with a thin empirical base: it should simply be corrected.

## Questions for Authors

1. Which of the three duty arguments (fair play, natural duty, beneficence) is meant to carry the conclusion, and what would the essay conclude if the empirical "strain" turned out to be nil?
2. How does auditability differ in kind from the transparency and accountability that citation-grounded retrieval systems can offer, and what would a retrieval system have to expose to match Wikipedia's contestability?
3. Given the evidence cited that contestability is penalised (Menking and Rosenberg) and that newcomers are rejected (Halfaker et al.), in what sense does the practice presently provide contestability, as opposed to the formal possibility of it?
4. Why does Section 5.3 focus on the Vietnamese edition when the essay states that its focus is the English edition, and what does the remeasurement add to the argument rather than to the Wikipedia literature?
