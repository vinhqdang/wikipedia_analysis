# Lit thread 2: Technological succession and moral panic about knowledge tools

Verification key: "Crossref" = api.crossref.org record for the DOI opened; "abstract only" = only an abstract or summary was seen, not the full text. Annotations are limited to what was seen. Books were verified through catalogue records (Open Library) only, so their annotations are flagged as standard characterisations and not as readings.

## A. Writing, media theory, technopoly

**1. Werner (2012), Myth and Philosophy in Plato's Phaedrus, ch. "Theuth, Thamus, and the Critique of Writing"**
- Citation: Werner, D. S. (2012). *Myth and philosophy in Plato's Phaedrus*. Cambridge University Press. (Chapter: "Theuth, Thamus, and the Critique of Writing," pp. 181-235.) https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781139108737
- Type: book (chapter-level DOI 10.1017/cbo9781139108737.008)
- Verification: Crossref records for the book DOI (author Daniel S. Werner, CUP, 2012) and the chapter DOI (title, pages 181-235) opened.
- Annotation: Only the book abstract was seen. Werner argues that Plato criticises traditional myth while using it, so myths in the Phaedrus engage non-philosophers, mark the limits of communication and knowledge, and draw readers into active self-examination. The chapter title shows it treats the Theuth/Thamus myth, the locus classicus of the writing critique, within that framework. I did not read the chapter.
- Bears on: Supplies the "first panic" origin (writing as a threat to memory) and a reading in which Plato's critique is ambivalent and self-undermining. That complicates a simple "tool blamed, then rehabilitated" arc, because the critique is itself written and the myth is part of its method.
- Keywords: Phaedrus, writing, memory, Theuth, myth, Plato

**2. Derrida (1981), Dissemination ("Plato's Pharmacy")**
- Citation: Derrida, J. (1981). *Dissemination* (B. Johnson, Trans.). University of Chicago Press. (Original work published 1972.)
- Type: book
- Verification: Open Library catalogue record opened (Dissemination, Derrida, 1981, Chicago/Athlone). No publisher page opened. The year and translator were not read from the record (the catalogue gave 1981 and 1983 editions); check the edition before final citation.
- Annotation: Catalogue record only. Dissemination is standardly cited for "Plato's Pharmacy," which reads the Phaedrus myth of writing through the double meaning of *pharmakon* (remedy and poison). Search-result summaries I saw describe the essay as turning Plato's oppositions (living speech against dead writing, internal against external memory) back on the critique itself.
- Bears on: Argues that the remedy/poison ambivalence is built into the writing critique, so "blame then rehabilitation" is a structural feature of any memory technology and not a historical sequence.
- Keywords: pharmakon, writing, deconstruction, Plato, memory

**3. McLuhan & McLuhan (1988), Laws of Media**
- Citation: McLuhan, M., & McLuhan, E. (1988). *Laws of media: The new science*. University of Toronto Press.
- Type: book
- Verification: Open Library record opened (title, University of Toronto Press, 1988). It lists Marshall McLuhan as author. The co-author Eric McLuhan comes from my own knowledge and is not confirmed by the record I opened; confirm before citing.
- Annotation: Catalogue record only. The book is the standard source for the tetrad: any medium enhances, obsolesces, retrieves and reverses. Annotation is a standard characterisation, not a reading.
- Bears on: "Retrieval" gives a vocabulary for the "older tool rehabilitated" pattern: an obsolesced form returns in a new role. It also suggests Wikipedia as a retrieved encyclopaedic form.
- Keywords: tetrad, retrieval, obsolescence, media ecology

**4. Postman (1992), Technopoly**
- Citation: Postman, N. (1992). *Technopoly: The surrender of culture to technology*. Knopf.
- Type: book
- Verification: Open Library record opened (Technopoly, Postman, first published 1992, Knopf/Vintage). The subtitle comes from my own knowledge and is not in the record.
- Annotation: Catalogue record only. It is standardly characterised as arguing that a culture can come to take its authority and meaning from technology and technique. Annotation is a standard characterisation, not a reading.
- Bears on: Provides the critical pole of the panic narrative, to be set against Orben's critique of such narratives.
- Keywords: technopoly, technological determinism, culture

## B. Cognitive offloading and "Google effects"

**5. Sparrow, Liu & Wegner (2011)**
- Citation: Sparrow, B., Liu, J., & Wegner, D. M. (2011). Google effects on memory: Cognitive consequences of having information at our fingertips. *Science, 333*(6043), 776-778. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1207745
- Type: peer-reviewed article
- Verification: Crossref record for the DOI opened (authors, year, volume, pages).
- Annotation: Only the abstract-level record was seen: "Owing to Internet search, we are more likely to encode 'where' aspects of memory rather than 'what.'" It is the foundational claim that online availability shifts memory toward knowing where information is stored.
- Bears on: Canonical source for the "only know Google" claim. Item 6 shows its first experiment did not replicate, which weakens the panic narrative's empirical base.
- Keywords: transactive memory, Internet search, Google effect

**6. Camerer et al. (2018)**
- Citation: Camerer, C. F., Dreber, A., Holzmeister, F., Ho, T.-H., Huber, J., Johannesson, M., ... Wu, H. (2018). Evaluating the replicability of social science experiments in Nature and Science between 2010 and 2015. *Nature Human Behaviour, 2*, 637-644. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-018-0399-z
- Type: peer-reviewed article
- Verification: Crossref record opened (title, authors, volume, pages). FLoRA Replication Atlas page for Sparrow et al. opened (outcome "failed", quoting p = 0.2647 in round 1 and p = 0.4493 pooled).
- Annotation: Replicated 21 social-science experiments published in Nature or Science, including Sparrow et al.'s Experiment 1. The Google effect priming result did not replicate. A web summary notes the original authors gave no materials and later pointed to design differences, so the result is not conclusive. I did not read the article text.
- Bears on: Complicates T3. The empirical basis for "Google ruined memory" is weaker than its cultural reach, so "rehabilitation" may correct an overstated panic and not an actual decline.
- Keywords: replication, Google effect, priming

**7. Marsh & Rajaram (2019)**
- Citation: Marsh, E. J., & Rajaram, S. (2019). The digital expansion of the mind: Implications of internet usage for memory and cognition. *Journal of Applied Research in Memory and Cognition, 8*(1), 1-14. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jarmac.2018.11.001
- Type: peer-reviewed article
- Verification: Crossref record opened (authors, year, volume, pages). A web summary of the abstract was also seen. No full text.
- Annotation: Reviews cognitive-science research on whether the internet encourages superficial processing, serves as a source of misinformation, inflates beliefs about what we know, and changes personal remembering. It treats cognitive offloading as one effect among several, not a simple loss.
- Bears on: A balanced review for the T3 offloading claim, and a source for the point that the internet can inflate confidence in what one knows.
- Keywords: cognitive offloading, internet, memory, metacognition

**8. Kosmyna et al. (2025), preprint**
- Citation: Kosmyna, N., Hauptmann, E., Yuan, Y. T., Situ, J., Liao, X.-H., Beresnitzky, A. V., Braunstein, I., & Maes, P. (2025). *Your brain on ChatGPT: Accumulation of cognitive debt when using an AI assistant for essay writing task* [Preprint]. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872
- Type: preprint (not peer reviewed as seen)
- Verification: arXiv abstract page opened (submitted 10 June 2025, revised 31 December 2025; 54 participants in sessions 1-3, 18 in session 4).
- Annotation: EEG study comparing essay writers using an LLM, a search engine, or no tool. LLM users showed the weakest brain connectivity, less ownership of their essays, and under-engagement when later working without the tool. The authors conclude prolonged LLM dependence correlates with poorer neural, linguistic and behavioural outcomes. Abstract only.
- Bears on: The current "only know AI" evidence. It is a small, preprint-stage study, so it fits the panic pattern as much as it counters it.
- Keywords: ChatGPT, EEG, cognitive debt, education

**9. Gerlich (2025), preprint**
- Citation: Gerlich, M. (2025). *AI tools in society: Impacts on cognitive offloading and the future of critical thinking* [Preprint]. SSRN. https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.5082524
- Type: preprint (SSRN). The author also published a journal version (*Societies*, 2025). I could not open it (HTTP 403), so it is not cited here.
- Verification: Crossref record for the SSRN DOI opened (title, author, year 2025, abstract summary).
- Annotation: Survey and interview study (666 participants) reports a significant negative correlation between frequent AI-tool use and critical thinking, mediated by cognitive offloading. Younger users show more dependence and lower scores, and higher education is protective. Abstract-level summary only.
- Bears on: Illustrates the correlational evidence behind current claims. Correlation cannot separate tool effects from selection.
- Keywords: AI tools, cognitive offloading, critical thinking

## C. Panics, their cycles, and critiques

**10. Orben (2020)**
- Citation: Orben, A. (2020). The Sisyphean cycle of technology panics. *Perspectives on Psychological Science, 15*(5), 1143-1157. https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691620919372
- Type: peer-reviewed article
- Verification: Crossref record opened (title, author, year, volume, pages, abstract summary). The SAGE page returned 403.
- Annotation: Argues that public anxiety about each new technology recurs and that psychologists are drawn into studying each one to calm worried publics. The research stays ineffective because findings and theory do not carry over from one technology generation to the next. She identifies political, demographic and academic factors that sustain the cycle. Abstract-level summary.
- Bears on: Strongest direct theoretical support for T3's panic cycle. It also complicates it: the cycle is sustained by research incentives, so "rehabilitation" may be part of the same cycle and not a final verdict.
- Keywords: technology panic, youth, research cycle
- **Full-text update (4 Oct 2026, copy supplied by the author):** read in full (15 pages; PPS 15(5), 1143-1157). The article opens with Preston's 1941 study of children and radio and movie crime dramas, then analyses how political, population and academic factors sustain a cycle in which psychologists are drawn to study each new technology and its effects on children, while the lack of a theoretical baseline means research restarts each time. On p. 1144 she notes that concerns about radio have practically disappeared and that many parents today would welcome radio dramas if they replaced phone use. Scope: young people and psychology; it does not mention Wikipedia or search engines. The earlier abstract-level annotation is superseded.

**11. Bennett, Maton & Kervin (2008)**
- Citation: Bennett, S., Maton, K., & Kervin, L. (2008). The 'digital natives' debate: A critical review of the evidence. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 39*(5), 775-786. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8535.2007.00793.x
- Type: peer-reviewed article
- Verification: Crossref record opened (authors, year, volume, issue, pages). Abstract-level only.
- Annotation: The record states the article examines the digital-native discourse through an evidence-based lens and questions common assumptions about this generation. I saw no further detail.
- Bears on: Complicates the "students only know Google/AI" frame. The claim about what students can or cannot do rested on generational assumptions with thin evidence, a critique of the panic and not of the tool.
- Keywords: digital natives, generation, education

**12. Ellington (2003)**
- Citation: Ellington, A. J. (2003). A meta-analysis of the effects of calculators on students' achievement and attitude levels in precollege mathematics classes. *Journal for Research in Mathematics Education, 34*(5), 433-463. https://doi.org/10.2307/30034795
- Type: peer-reviewed article
- Verification: Crossref record opened (author spelling Ellington, 2003, vol. 34(5)). Findings are from a web-search summary, not the article. The end page 463 comes from my own knowledge and is not in the record I opened; confirm before citing.
- Annotation: Per the summary, a meta-analysis of 54 studies (1983-2002) found operational and problem-solving skills improved when calculators were part of testing and instruction, with better attitudes toward mathematics.
- Bears on: Standard example of a panic (calculators erode arithmetic) followed by evidence that the effect is neutral or positive. It supports the "rehabilitation" pattern but through evidence, not through the collapse of alternatives.
- Keywords: calculators, mathematics education, meta-analysis

## D. Epistemic infrastructure and Wikipedia's place in it

**13. Star & Ruhleder (1996)**
- Citation: Star, S. L., & Ruhleder, K. (1996). Steps toward an ecology of infrastructure: Design and access for large information spaces. *Information Systems Research, 7*(1), 111-134. https://doi.org/10.1287/isre.7.1.111
- Type: peer-reviewed article
- Verification: Crossref record opened (authors, year, volume, pages, abstract).
- Annotation: Studies the Worm Community System, a collaborative platform for dispersed geneticists, where users had access difficulties despite positive feedback and a thorough needs assessment. Applies Bateson's learning levels to analyse infrastructural complexity, access, and designer-user communication. Abstract only.
- Bears on: Source for treating infrastructure as relational and tied to practice. It lets the essay ask for whom Wikipedia has become infrastructure, and what breaks if others stop maintaining it.
- Keywords: infrastructure, ecology, access, STS

**14. McMahon, Johnson & Hecht (2017)**
- Citation: McMahon, C., Johnson, I., & Hecht, B. (2017). The substantial interdependence of Wikipedia and Google: A case study on the relationship between peer production communities and information technologies. *Proceedings of the International AAAI Conference on Web and Social Media, 11*(1), 142-151. https://doi.org/10.1609/icwsm.v11i1.14883
- Type: peer-reviewed conference paper
- Verification: AAAI OJS article page opened (authors, ICWSM 2017, abstract).
- Annotation: Removing Wikipedia from Google results in deception studies lowered click-through and made Google a worse search engine for many queries. Google also supplies much of Wikipedia's traffic, but Google's direct display of Wikipedia content in results reduces visits to Wikipedia itself. The authors argue for studying interdependence between peer-production communities and distributing technologies.
- Bears on: Strong evidence for T2/T3: Wikipedia is infrastructure for search, and its feeding of platforms predates the AI reuse dispute. It names the "knowledge panel" erosion problem.
- Keywords: Wikipedia, Google, search, peer production, traffic

**15. Piccardi, Redi, Colavizza & West (2021), WWW 2021**
- Citation: Piccardi, T., Redi, M., Colavizza, G., & West, R. (2021). On the value of Wikipedia as a gateway to the Web. In *Proceedings of the Web Conference 2021* (WWW '21). ACM. https://doi.org/10.1145/3442381.3450136
- Type: peer-reviewed conference paper (arXiv version opened: 2102.07385)
- Verification: arXiv abstract page opened (authors, submission date 15 February 2021, WWW 2021). The ACM DOI comes from a search-result URL and was not opened.
- Annotation: Clickstream analysis finds English Wikipedia sent about 43 million clicks to external sites in a month. Infobox official links had the highest click-through (2.47%). Wikipedia often sits between search engines and third-party sites, and the traffic would cost site owners an estimated $7-13 million a month in sponsored search. Abstract only.
- Bears on: Shows Wikipedia as infrastructure beyond its own readership. It supports a commons argument that value flows out of Wikipedia to other platforms.
- Keywords: Wikipedia, clickstream, referral traffic, infrastructure

**16. Miller (2025), Wikimedia Diff**
- Citation: Miller, M. (2025, October 17). New user trends on Wikipedia. *Diff* (Wikimedia Foundation). https://diff.wikimedia.org/2025/10/17/new-user-trends-on-wikipedia/
- Type: news-commentary / institutional blog (not peer reviewed)
- Verification: Diff post opened (author, date).
- Annotation: Reports that human pageviews fell about 8% compared with 2024 after bot traffic from Brazil was reclassified in May 2025. The Foundation attributes the fall to generative AI and social platforms answering users directly instead of linking to Wikipedia. It says Wikipedia content remains foundational to AI and search, and describes responses including enforcement policies and a new reader-experience team.
- Bears on: Central to T2 and a complication of T3. Wikipedia is rehabilitated as an input to AI while losing its direct audience, so legitimacy and sustaining attention are separating.
- Keywords: pageviews, generative AI, Wikimedia, search summaries

## E. Wikipedia disparaged, then used

**17. Jaschik (2007), Inside Higher Ed**
- Citation: Jaschik, S. (2007, January 26). A stand against Wikipedia. *Inside Higher Ed*. https://www.insidehighered.com/news/2007/01/26/stand-against-wikipedia
- Type: news-commentary
- Verification: Inside Higher Ed page opened (author and date as returned by fetch; the fetch tool gave January 25, other results January 26; use the page's own date).
- Annotation: Reports Middlebury College's history department voting to bar students from citing Wikipedia in papers and exams. The chair said it may help students find credible sources but lacks editorial oversight. Some voices backed the rule, while others said teaching information literacy beats bans.
- Bears on: Documents the academic disparagement phase T3 refers to, with a built-in counter-position favouring literacy over bans.
- Keywords: Wikipedia ban, citation, higher education, 2007

**18. Head & Eisenberg (2010)**
- Citation: Head, A. J., & Eisenberg, M. B. (2010). How today's college students use Wikipedia for course-related research. *First Monday, 15*(3). https://doi.org/10.5210/fm.v15i3.2830
- Type: peer-reviewed article
- Verification: First Monday article opened (authors, 2010, abstract); Crossref DOI record opened.
- Annotation: Focus groups and surveys across six US colleges show students mostly use Wikipedia for background information, less often than course materials and Google. Use is higher in architecture, engineering and science. Wikipedia is used in combination with other resources because of its "coverage, currency, convenience, and comprehensibility."
- Bears on: Complicates T3's "disparaged" story. Students were already treating Wikipedia as a starting point, so later reputation follows practice more than it reverses it.
- Keywords: Wikipedia, students, information seeking

**19. Minguillón et al. (2018), preprint**
- Citation: Minguillón, J., Aibar, E., Lerga, M., Lladós, J., & Meseguer-Artola, A. (2018). *Wikipedia in academia as a teaching tool: From averse to proactive faculty profiles* [Preprint]. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.07138
- Type: preprint
- Verification: arXiv abstract page opened (authors, submitted 22 January 2018).
- Annotation: Surveyed instructors at two Barcelona universities and sorted them into four groups by willingness to use Wikipedia in teaching. Clusters with a positive view and some use clearly outnumber strictly negative ones. Resistance was concentrated among humanities, full-time female instructors, and perceived usefulness and quality mattered more than demographics. Abstract only.
- Bears on: Disparagement was uneven within academia, and attitudes depended on institutional culture and disciplines. That complicates a single "academia disparaged it" story.
- Keywords: Wikipedia, faculty attitudes, teaching

**20. Okoli et al. (2014)**
- Citation: Okoli, C., Mehdi, M., Mesgari, M., Nielsen, F. Å., & Lanamäki, A. (2014). Wikipedia in the eyes of its beholders: A systematic review of scholarly research on Wikipedia readers and readership. *Journal of the Association for Information Science and Technology, 65*(12), 2381-2403. https://doi.org/10.1002/asi.23162
- Type: peer-reviewed article (systematic review)
- Verification: Crossref record opened; search results from RePEc and Wiley abstract pages seen (not opened).
- Annotation: Reviews 99 scholarly studies on Wikipedia readers and readership and analyses their methods. As summarised, Wikipedia is popular for both light and serious topics, including health and legal information, with scholars, librarians and students among its users. Abstract-level only.
- Bears on: Reference survey of the 2000s-2010s reader-credibility literature that T3 can use to date the disparagement phase.
- Keywords: Wikipedia, readers, credibility, systematic review

## Gaps and disputes
No peer-reviewed study found directly compares Wikipedia's credibility against successive rival media over time, which is T3's core claim. The Sparrow effect is disputed: failed priming replication, but cognitive offloading itself is not refuted. Evidence on AI and cognition is mostly preprints or correlational. I found no empirical source on the "implicit commons social contract" in T2 in this thread. Book entries (Derrida, McLuhan, Postman) need primary-source page checks. Education-panic literature on generative AI specifically (as a moral-panic critique) is thin in what I could open.
