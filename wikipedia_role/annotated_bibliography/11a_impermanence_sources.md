## A. Core entries (read in full text, quotes verified)

### A1. Siderits, "Buddha", SEP
- Reference: Siderits, Mark. "Buddha." *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, first published 17 Feb 2011; substantive revision 6 Mar 2023. https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/buddha/
- Read: full entry text (sections 1-5), searched for the relevant passages.
- Quotes:
  1. (Sec. 2, Core Teachings) "the realization that all (oneself included) is impermanent can undermine a precondition for real enjoyment of the events in a life: that such events are meaningful by virtue of their having a place in an open-ended narrative."
  2. (Sec. 3, Non-Self) "This also demonstrates that the Buddha did not mean by ‘impermanent’ what some later Buddhist philosophers meant, viz., existing for just a moment; the Buddhist doctrine of momentariness represents a later development." (Momentariness vs ordinary impermanence: the source draws exactly this distinction. Here "permanent" means "continued existence over at least several lives".)
  3. (Sec. 2) "The extremes are eternalism, the view that persons are eternal, and annihilationism, the view that persons go utterly out of existence (usually understood to mean at death, though a term still shorter than one lifetime is not ruled out)."
  4. (Sec. 2) "The idea of the ‘middle path’ is that all of life’s continuities can be explained in terms of facts about a causal series of psychophysical elements."
- Bearing: Ordinary impermanence means things last a while and then go; this is the sense relevant to a norm or institution, and the entry shows it is the early, non-momentary one. (Sec. 2 also gives the "ignorance concerning suffering, impermanence and non-self" formulation of dependent origination's role.)

### A2. Vélez de Cea, "Buddha", IEP
- Reference: Vélez de Cea, Abraham. "Buddha (c. 500s B.C.E.)." *Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy*. https://iep.utm.edu/buddha/ (author line on page: Abraham Velez, Eastern Kentucky University; entry date not captured).
- Read: full entry text; sections 3.d, 3.f, 4.b, 5 checked.
- Quotes:
  1. (3.f) "The general principle of dependent arising states that “when this exists, that comes to be; with the arising of this, that arises. When this does not exist, that does not come to be; with the cessation of this, that ceases” (M.II.32; S.II.28)."
  2. (3.f) "The point behind dependent arising is that things are dependent on specific conditions (paṭicca), and that they arise together with other things (samuppāda)."
  3. (3.f) "This relativity, however, does not mean that for the Buddha of the Pāli Nikāyas everything is interdependent or that something is related to everything else. This is a later development of Buddhist thought, not a characteristic of early Indian Buddhism." (Caution for any use of Thich Nhat Hanh's "interbeing": the strong interdependence reading is, per this entry, a later development.)
  4. (3.d) "whatever becomes, that is conditioned, volitionally formed, dependently arisen, that is impermanent." (quoting A.V.188)
  5. (4.b) "dependent arising as a middle way between the extremes of eternalism and annihilationism"
- Bearing: gives the early canonical formula: a thing exists while its conditions hold and ceases when they cease, which is the structure of a norm that depends on supporting practices.

### A3. Arnold, "Madhyamaka Buddhist Philosophy", IEP
- Reference: Arnold, Dan. "Madhyamaka Buddhist Philosophy." *Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy*. https://iep.utm.edu/madhyamaka-buddhist-philosophy/ (entry date not captured).
- Read: full text of sections 2.a-2.c (the relevant core); rest not read closely.
- Quotes:
  1. (2.a) "Philosophically, the relevant extremes between which any Buddhist account of the person must steer are “eternalism” and “nihilism.” Eternalism (śāśvatavāda) is the view that there are enduring existents of which the self is an example. Nihilism (ucchedavāda) might be termed “eliminativism,” and denotes, for Buddhists, the view that actions (karma) have no ethical consequences, insofar as the agents of actions cannot be said to endure as the subjects who will experience their effects."
  2. (2.b) "phenomena are dependently originated all the way down, and it is therefore impossible to specify precisely what it is upon which anything finally depends."
  3. (2.b) MMK 24.19 as quoted: “Since there is no dharma whatsoever that is not dependently originated, therefore there is no dharma whatsoever that is not empty.”
  4. (2.c) "there is nothing “more real” than the world as conventionally described – then the world is finally to be accepted as irreducibly “conventional,” and the persons therein can hence be regarded as ethical agents"
- Bearing: the best open statement that conventions are not a lesser layer hiding a "real" one, so a norm is real as a convention and still contingent.

### A4. Hayes, "Madhyamaka", SEP
- Reference: Hayes, Richard. "Madhyamaka." *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, first published 6 Nov 2010; substantive revision 18 Aug 2023. https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/madhyamaka/
- Read: full entry text; introduction, sec. 1, sec. 5.2 and sec. 6.2 passages checked. (Authorship from the page metadata.)
- Quotes:
  1. (Intro) "a middle path (madhyamā pratipad) that avoids the two extremes of eternalism—the doctrine that all things exist because of an eternal essence—and annihilationism—the doctrine that things have essences while they exist but that these essences are annihilated just when the things themselves go out of existence."
  2. (Intro) "All things are, in other words, empty of inherent natures."
  3. (Sec. 1) "there is a level of understanding that consists of an accurate account of the world as it is experienced in everyday life and another level of understanding that is conducive to reaching the ultimate goal (paramārtha) of Buddhist practice"
  4. (Sec. 5.2, Candrakīrti) "The ultimate truth—that is, truth concerning the highest goal—is that phenomena do not come into being; the conventional transactional truth, on the other hand, is that things do come into being and that their arising is conditioned."
  5. (Sec. 6.2, Śāntideva) "The principal delusion of those who rely on conventional truths is that they mistakenly believe that propositions conventionally accepted as truth are grounded in the natures of things."
- Bearing: this entry's version of the two extremes is about essences; the point for norms is that treating a convention as grounded in a fixed nature (eternalism) is the error, and so is concluding it is nothing (annihilationism). Note this entry's Candrakīrti reading treats conventional truth as a "screen"; A5 and B1 give the more rehabilitating reading.

### A5. Westerhoff, "Nāgārjuna", SEP
- Reference: Westerhoff, Jan Christoph. "Nāgārjuna." *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, first published 10 Feb 2010; substantive revision 31 May 2026. https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nagarjuna/
- Read: full entry text; sec. 2, 3.1 and 3.5 passages checked.
- Quotes:
  1. (Sec. 3.1) "the Mādhyamikas themselves are very clear that their position avoids both of the extreme views, the view that believes in the existence of svabhāva as well as its nihilistic opposite."
  2. (Sec. 3.5, Language and truth) "According to the Madhyamaka view of truth there can be no such thing as ultimate truth, a theory describing how things really are, independent of our interests and conceptual resources employed in describing it. All one is left with is conventional truth, truth which consists in agreement with commonly accepted practices and conventions."
  3. (Sec. 3.5) "the Mādhyamika might want to make the point that it is at least sometimes advantageous to treat truths as if they had a more than conventional grounding ... This is precisely because such practices need improvement from time to time"
  4. (Sec. 2) "Emptiness is of course always the emptiness of something, and the something Nāgārjuna has in mind here is svabhāva."
- Bearing: the most directly usable passage for norm change: conventions are the only truth available, are real as practices and agreements, and are open to improvement. Westerhoff presents this as one reading (anti-realist), not the only one.

### A6. Khawaja, "Sarvāstivāda Buddhism", IEP (momentariness context only)
- Reference: Khawaja, Zishan. "Sarvāstivāda Buddhism." *Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy*. https://iep.utm.edu/sarvastivada-buddhism/
- Read: sec. 3.d and 5.a passages only.
- Quotes:
  1. (3.d) "In order to support the Buddha’s claim that reality is characterised by impermanence, meaning that all entities arise and cease, the Sarvāstivāda also included four types of dharma"
  2. (5.a) "The Sautrāntika approach to the Buddhist reductive project differs and argues that reality consists of momentarily existing and causally interacting basic elements"
- Bearing: shows the later scholastic move from "all things arise and cease" to momentary elements; supports keeping ordinary impermanence (A1) separate from momentariness when discussing slow institutional change.

- Reference: Garfield, Jay L. 2010. "Taking Conventional Truth Seriously: Authority Regarding Deceptive Reality." *Philosophy East and West* 60, 341-354. DOI 10.1353/pew.0.0113 (Crossref: exists; issued July 2010; publisher Project MUSE).
- Read: abstract only, taken from the Crossref record. Full text not read.
- Quotes (abstract):
  1. "Mādhyamika philosophers in India and Tibet distinguish between two truths: the conventional and the ultimate."
  2. "It is difficult, however, to say in what sense conventional truth is indeed a truth, as opposed to falsehood."
