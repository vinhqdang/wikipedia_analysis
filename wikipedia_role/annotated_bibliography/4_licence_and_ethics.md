Section A: Licence texts and organisational positions

## 1. CC BY-SA 4.0 legal code
- Citation: Creative Commons. (2013). *Attribution-ShareAlike 4.0 International (CC BY-SA 4.0) legal code*. https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/legalcode.en
- Type: legal-or-policy document (licence text).
- Verification: opened the legal-code page on creativecommons.org.
- Annotation: Quoted provisions: Adapted Material is "material subject to Copyright and Similar Rights that is derived from or based upon the Licensed Material" (s.1); the grant is to "reproduce and Share the Licensed Material, in whole or in part; and produce, reproduce, and Share Adapted Material" (s.2(a)(1)); attribution duty is to "retain ... identification of the creator(s)" and notices (s.3(a)(1)); ShareAlike applies "If You Share Adapted Material You produce" (s.3(b)); the no-endorsement clause bars implying the Licensor "sponsored, endorsed" the user (s.2(a)(6)). Note the conditions bite on Sharing, not on private use or training as such. Section numbers as reported by the fetch tool; check against the page before quoting in print.
- Bears on: T2. Defines what the licence promises: no reciprocity of funds, traffic or participation, only attribution and share-alike on shared adaptations. Sets the baseline against which "betrayal" must be tested.
- Keywords: CC BY-SA, adapted material, attribution, ShareAlike, commercial use.

## 11. The Open Source Definition (v1.9)
- Citation: Open Source Initiative. (2007). *The open source definition* (v1.9, 22 March 2007). https://opensource.org/osd
- Type: legal-or-policy document.
- Verification: opened the page.
- Annotation: Clause 6: "The license must not restrict anyone from making use of the program in a specific field of endeavor. For example, it may not restrict the program from being used in a business, or from being used for genetic research." Free software and open licensing norms deliberately refuse use-based conditions, so a reuser who complies owes nothing further by design. Software-specific; CC BY-SA is not OSI-approved, so this is an analogy for open-licensing culture, not the CC text.
- Bears on: T2. The best-documented statement that open licences waive claims about who uses the work and for what; the "no discrimination" principle cuts against reading AI use as a breach.
- Keywords: open source, field of endeavour, non-discrimination, free redistribution.

Section C: Moral-philosophy tools

## 12. Trust and Antitrust
- Citation: Baier, A. (1986). Trust and antitrust. *Ethics, 96*(2), 231-260. https://doi.org/10.1086/292745
- Type: peer-reviewed article.
- Verification: Crossref record and PhilPapers/Semantic Scholar listing (via search) confirm author, year, volume, pages, DOI; full text not read.
- Annotation: Per the description found, Baier treats trust as accepting vulnerability to another's possible ill will and criticises contractarian models of morality for neglecting trust, especially under unequal power; she offers a moral test for trust relations. Not read in full here.
- Bears on: T2. Supplies the contrast between contract (what a licence is) and trust (accepted vulnerability to discretionary goodwill): betrayal needs trust, not a contract.
- Keywords: trust, vulnerability, contract, moral test.
- **Full-text update (3 Oct 2026, copy supplied by the author):** read in full (Ethics 96(2), 231-260). Trust, "on this first approximation", is "accepted vulnerability to another's possible but not expected ill will (or lack of good will) toward one" (p. 235). Mere dependence differs from trust: Kant's neighbours who counted on his walk to set clocks might be disappointed but not let down or betrayed. Trust extends to strangers and enemies and may be unconscious or unwanted; plausible conditions for proper trust are that it survives the consciousness of both parties and that the trusted has had an opportunity to signify acceptance or rejection and to warn the trusting. The contract model is strained for relations between unequals (infant and parent). The earlier record-only annotation is superseded.

## 13. Trust, Distrust and Commitment
- Citation: Hawley, K. (2014). Trust, distrust and commitment. *Noûs, 48*(1), 1-20. https://doi.org/10.1111/nous.12000
- Type: peer-reviewed article.
- Verification: Crossref record (online 2012, issue 2014) and St Andrews repository/Wiley listings via search; full text not read.
- Annotation: Per the summary found, to trust someone is to rely on them to fulfil a commitment, and distrust is the expectation of an unfulfilled commitment. This ties betrayal to commitments, which may be explicit or implicit.
- Bears on: T2. Test for "betrayal": did AI firms or the licence incur any commitment (e.g. through Wikimedia's own attribution request, or assurances) that editors relied on? If not, disappointment is not betrayal.
- Keywords: trust, commitment, betrayal, reliance.
- **Full-text update (3 Oct 2026, copy supplied by the author):** read in full. Hawley defines trust as believing that someone has a commitment to do something and relying on them to meet it, and distrust as believing they have the commitment and not relying on them. Commitments may be implicit or explicit; "mutual expectation and convention give rise to commitment unless we take steps to disown these". Betrayal does not require that the victim trusted; the wrong lies in failing to fulfil a commitment. Expecting what nobody committed to gives a feeling of betrayal without a betrayal. When a commitment is made to a third party, the person betrayed is the one to whom it was made. The earlier annotation above (record-only) is superseded by this one. The paper does not discuss open licences or digital commons.

## 14. Deciding to Trust, Coming to Believe
- Citation: Holton, R. (1994). Deciding to trust, coming to believe. *Australasian Journal of Philosophy, 72*(1), 63-76. https://doi.org/10.1080/00048409412345881
- Type: peer-reviewed article.
- Verification: Crossref record; abstract wording from a search description; publisher page 403.
- Annotation: Distinguishes trust from mere reliance by the participant stance and reactive attitudes: trusting makes one liable to feel betrayed rather than merely let down. Not read in full.
- Bears on: T2. Marks the line the essay needs: editors who merely relied on reusers feel disappointed; only those who trusted can claim betrayal.
- Keywords: trust, reliance, betrayal, reactive attitudes.
- **Full-text update (3 Oct 2026, copy supplied by the author; manuscript pagination, so no page numbers are given here):** read in full. Holton argues that one can decide to trust without believing the other will deliver; that trust differs from reliance by a participant stance (after Strawson), i.e. a readiness to feel betrayal if let down and gratitude if the reliance is upheld, whereas a machine's failure gives annoyance but not resentment; and that trust is a three-place relation (one person trusts another to do certain things). He says he does not address when and to whom trust is appropriate. The earlier record-only annotation is superseded.

## 17. The Gift

## 18. Are There Any Natural Rights?
- Citation: Hart, H. L. A. (1955). Are there any natural rights? *The Philosophical Review, 64*(2), 175-191. https://doi.org/10.2307/2182586
- Type: peer-reviewed article.
- Verification: Crossref record confirms author, year, volume, issue, start page 175; end page 191 from my own knowledge, confirm before print. Text not read.
- Annotation: Record only. Hart's passage on mutual restriction (those who restrict liberty for mutual benefit have a right to similar submission from those who benefited) is the source of fair-play arguments; not verified in text here.
- Bears on: T2. Fair play grounds a duty on beneficiaries of a cooperative scheme; whether anonymous open licensing is such a scheme is disputed (Rawls item below).
- Keywords: fair play, mutual restriction, free riding.

## 19. Legal Obligation and the Duty of Fair Play
- Citation: Rawls, J. (1964). Legal obligation and the duty of fair play. In S. Hook (Ed.), *Law and philosophy* (pp. 3-18). New York University Press.
- Type: chapter.
- Verification: contemporarythinkers.org page confirms bibliographic details; search description gave the principle: a person who has accepted the benefits of a mutually advantageous cooperative scheme has a duty to do their share. Text not read.
- Annotation: Fair play requires acceptance of benefits within a scheme with restrictions borne by participants. AI firms did not bear Wikipedia's restrictions or opt in to its cooperation; whether they "accepted" benefits is the question.
- Bears on: T2. Possible ground for a non-licence obligation of contribution; also a limit, as fair-play duties usually presuppose a cooperative scheme the beneficiary joined.
- Keywords: fairness, free-riding, cooperative schemes.

## 20. Exploitation (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy)
- Citation: Zwolinski, M., Ferguson, B., & Wertheimer, A. (2022). Exploitation. In E. N. Zalta & U. Nodelman (Eds.), *The Stanford encyclopedia of philosophy* (Winter 2022 ed.). https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/exploitation/
- Type: reference work (peer-reviewed encyclopedia entry).
- Verification: opened entry (first published 20 Dec 2001; revision date shown as 3 Oct 2022); author list from SEP archive listing via search.
- Annotation: "To exploit someone is to take unfair advantage of them. It is to use another person's vulnerability for one's own benefit." Also "not all exploitation is harmful"; mutually beneficial, consensual exchanges may be exploitative because unfair. Open licensing is non-transactional and consensual, so the exploitation charge turns on fairness of distribution, not consent.
- Bears on: T2. Gives a test for "betrayal vs exploitation": consent to the licence does not by itself dispose of an unfairness claim.
- Keywords: exploitation, unfair advantage, consent, mutual benefit.

## 24. Consent in Crisis: The Rapid Decline of the AI Data Commons
- Citation: Longpre, S., Mahari, R., Lee, A., Lund, C., Oderinwale, H., Brannon, W., ... Pentland, S. (2024). *Consent in crisis: The rapid decline of the AI data commons* [Preprint]. arXiv:2407.14933. https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.14933 (49 authors; full list on page)

- Keywords: consent, robots.txt, data commons, terms of service.

## Gaps and disputes

Sources arguing that licence compliance suffices are mainly institutional (CC, OSI, Wikimedia terms) and fair-use scholarship; I found no philosophy paper arguing it directly for Wikipedia. Philosophy items (Baier, Hawley, Holton, Hart, Rawls, Mauss, Scott) were verified only as records; the argument must be read before quoting. No litigation concerns Wikipedia text directly; Doe v. GitHub is the nearest analogue and its contract claims are pending. Disputed: whether fair-play and gift obligations survive deliberate unreciprocated open licensing, and whether Wikimedia's attribution/payment requests assert a breach or only a hope.
