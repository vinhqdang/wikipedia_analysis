Compiled 2026-10-05. Method: each source was fetched from an open location and the quoted strings were checked against the retrieved text (HTML converted to plain text, or PDF text extraction). Quotes are exact except that line breaks and hyphenation from PDF extraction were normalised. "PDF p." means the page of the PDF file, with the printed page given where it could be derived.

Status labels used below:
- READ-FULL: full text read and searched.
- READ-SEP: relevant sections of a Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry read.
- READ-ABSTRACT: abstract or publisher blurb read, not the full text.
- RECORD-ONLY: bibliographic record confirmed via Crossref (https://api.crossref.org/works/...), nothing of the content read.
- UNVERIFIED: remembered or seen only in a search-result snippet or a secondary summary; do not quote in the essay without checking the primary text.

## 1. Social norms and conventions

### 1a. Bicchieri, Muldoon and Sontuoso, "Social Norms", Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy
- Reference: Bicchieri, Cristina, Ryan Muldoon and Alessandro Sontuoso. "Social Norms." *The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, ed. Edward N. Zalta. First published 1 March 2011; substantive revision 19 December 2023.
- URL: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/social-norms/
- Read: READ-SEP. Sections 1 and 5 read closely; the other sections were searched. Raw page fetched and text-searched.
- Quotes:
  1. (Sec. 1, General Issues) "In a nutshell, norms refer to actions over which people have control, and are supported by shared expectations about what should or should not be done in different types of social situations."
  2. (Sec. 1) "In particular, according to Bicchieri's (2006) account, preferences for conformity to social norms are conditional on "empirical expectations" (i.e., first-order beliefs that a certain behavior will be followed) as well as "normative expectations" (i.e., second-order beliefs that a certain behavior ought to be followed). Thus, norm compliance results from the joint presence of a conditional preference for conformity and the belief that other people will conform as well as approve of conformity."
  3. (Sec. 5, Game-Theoretic Accounts) "The combined force of empirical and normative expectations makes norm conformity a compelling choice, be it because punishment may follow or just because one recognizes the legitimacy of others' expectations (Sugden 2000)."
  4. (Sec. 5) "Having conditional preferences means that one may follow a norm in the presence of the relevant expectations, but disregard it in its absence."
  5. (Sec. 1, on lapse) "A poorly understood phenomenon is the sudden and unexpected change of well-established patterns of behavior." And: "However, Bicchieri (2016) points out that inefficiency is not a sufficient condition for a norm's demise."
  6. (Sec. 1, on Ullmann-Margalit and Lewis) "norms are either Nash equilibria of coordination games or cooperative equilibria of prisoner's dilemma-type games (Lewis 1969; Ullmann-Margalit 1977), and as such they solve collective action problems."
  7. (Sec. 1, on the Brennan et al. account) "Norms function to hold us accountable to each other for adherence to the principles that they cover."
- Bearing on norm change: a norm lasts only while the two kinds of expectation are mutually held, so it can lapse without anyone changing their values when the expectations about others shift. This is the mechanism for the argument that a norm of contributing to Wikipedia can erode.
- Caution: the quoted wording in 1.2 uses straight quotation marks here; the SEP uses curly ones.

### 1b. Rescorla, "Convention", Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (secondary source for Lewis 1969)
- Reference: Rescorla, Michael. "Convention." *The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*. First published 6 September 2007; substantive revision 22 February 2024.
- URL: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/convention/
- Read: READ-SEP. Sec. 1.1 and sec. 3.2 (Lewis).
- Quotes:
  1. (Sec. 3.2) "Lewis analyzes convention as an arbitrary, self-perpetuating solution to a recurring coordination problem. It is self-perpetuating because no one has reason to deviate from it, given that others conform."
  2. (Sec. 3.2, quoting Lewis 1969, p. 76) "A regularity R in the behavior of members of a population P when they are agents in a recurrent situation S is a convention if and only if it is true that, and it is common knowledge in P that, in any instance of S among members of P, (1) everyone conforms to R; (2) everyone expects everyone else to conform to R;"
  3. (Sec. 3.2) "Notably, Lewis introduces the concept of common knowledge."
- Bearing on norm change: Lewisian conventions are arbitrary (some alternative R' would also do) and self-sustaining, so they persist by expectation alone and can be displaced by coordinated shift to an alternative.

### 1d. Ullmann-Margalit, *The Emergence of Norms* (1977)
- Reference: Ullmann-Margalit, Edna. 1977. *The Emergence of Norms*. Oxford: Clarendon Press.
- Read: RECORD-ONLY for the book itself (Crossref returned only reviews, for example Hardin, *Ethics* 90(4), 1980, https://doi.org/10.1086/292187). Content known only through the SEP sentence in 1a, quote 6.
- Bearing on norm change: treats norms as solutions to prisoner's-dilemma, coordination and inequality problems, which implies a norm can be displaced if the underlying problem structure changes. UNVERIFIED as a reading of the book.

### 1e. Bicchieri, *The Grammar of Society* (2006)
- Reference: Bicchieri, Cristina. 2006. *The Grammar of Society: The Nature and Dynamics of Social Norms*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511616037
- Read: READ-ABSTRACT (publisher blurb via Crossref).
- Quote (publisher blurb): "In The Grammar of Society, first published in 2006, Cristina Bicchieri examines social norms, such as fairness, cooperation, and reciprocity, in an effort to understand their nature and dynamics, the expectations that they generate, and how they evolve and change."

## 2. How norms change and lapse

### 2a. Bicchieri and Mercier, "Norms and Beliefs: How Change Occurs" (2014)
- Reference: Bicchieri, Cristina and Hugo Mercier. 2014. "Norms and Beliefs: How Change Occurs." In *The Complexity of Social Norms*, ed. Maciej Xenitidou and Bruce Edmonds, 37-54. Cham: Springer. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-05308-0_3 (Crossref record confirmed).
- Open copy read: author-posted manuscript at https://web.sas.upenn.edu/behav-ethics/files/2017/05/norms_and_beliefs-_how_change_occurs_copy-1dywraz.pdf (25 pages). The manuscript header says "To appear in in B. Edmonds (ed.) The Dynamic View of Norms, Cambridge University Press", so its wording and pagination may differ from the Springer chapter. Springer's page returned a bot challenge, so the published version was not checked.
- Read: READ-FULL of the manuscript. Locators below are manuscript PDF pages.
- Quotes:
  1. (PDF p. 1) "Often norms support or embed certain practices, so that eliminating the latter involves changing the former. Sometimes, however, norms have to be created in order to eliminate a negative practice and support a new one"
  2. (PDF p. 2-3) "Social norms, it has been argued (Bicchieri 2006), are behavioral rules supported by a combination of empirical and normative expectations. Individuals have a conditional preference for obeying social norms, provided they hold the right expectations."
  3. (PDF p. 5) "It has been shown (Bicchieri and Fukui 1999) that it may take a small number of 'trendsetters' who question the standing norm and start behaving differently to effect a major change."
  4. (PDF p. 21, Conclusion) "To abandon negative norms, we need to change people's empirical and normative expectations."
- Bearing on norm change: change works by altering expectations, and a few visible deviators can start it; this is the vocabulary for how editors' expectations of each other might shift.

### 2b. Centola, Becker, Brackbill and Baronchelli, "Experimental evidence for tipping points in social convention" (2018)
- Reference: Centola, Damon, Joshua Becker, Devon Brackbill and Andrea Baronchelli. 2018. "Experimental evidence for tipping points in social convention." *Science* 360(6393): 1116-1119. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aas8827
- Open copy read: https://www.asc.upenn.edu/sites/default/files/2021-03/Experimental%20evidence%20for%20tipping%20points%20in%20social%20convention.pdf (5 pages; this is a copy hosted by the authors' department).
- Read: READ-FULL (text searched; the lead parts and figure legends read closely).
- Quotes:
  1. (PDF p. 1 = p. 1116, abstract) "The findings provide direct empirical demonstration of the existence of a tipping point in the dynamics of changing social conventions. When minority groups reached the critical mass—that is, the critical group size for initiating social change—they were consistently able to overturn the established behavior."
  2. (PDF p. 1, introduction) "Observational accounts of rapid changes in social conventions have suggested that apparently stable societal norms can be effectively overturned by the efforts of small but committed minorities"
  3. (PDF p. 1, Fig. 1 legend) "showing the largest unsuccessful minority (21%) and the smallest successful minority (25%)."
  4. (Science editor's summary, via Crossref) "When the number of confederates was roughly 25% of the group, the opinion of the majority could be tipped to that of the minority."
- Bearing on norm change: a convention held only by mutual expectation can be reversed by a committed minority above a threshold; it shows how a norm can flip quickly. Caution: the setting is a coordination game with only a coordination incentive, so it models conventions, not sanction-backed norms.

### 2c. Young, "The Evolution of Social Norms" (2015)
- Reference: Young, H. Peyton. 2015. "The Evolution of Social Norms." *Annual Review of Economics* 7: 359-387. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-economics-080614-115322
- Open copy read: http://www.econ2.jhu.edu/people/young/SocialNormsFinal.pdf (29 pages; PDF p. 1 = printed p. 359).
- Read: READ-FULL of abstract and sections 3-3.4; the remainder searched.
- Quotes:
  1. (p. 359, abstract) "Social norms are patterns of behavior that are self-enforcing within a group: Everyone conforms, everyone is expected to conform, and everyone wants to conform when they expect everyone else to conform."
  2. (p. 363, sec. 3.2) "When norm shifts do occur, the transition tends to be sudden rather than incremental (Schelling 1960, 1978)."
  3. (p. 363, sec. 3.3) "There are long periods of no change punctuated by occasional bursts of activity in which an old norm is rapidly displaced by a new one."
  4. (p. 361) "Norms typically evolve without top-down direction through a process of trial and error, experimentation, and adaptation."
- Bearing on norm change: gives the "long stasis, then rapid displacement" pattern, which supports the claim that a norm can appear stable until shortly before it lapses.

## 3. Reciprocity

### 3a. Gouldner, "The Norm of Reciprocity: A Preliminary Statement" (1960)
- Reference: Gouldner, Alvin W. 1960. "The Norm of Reciprocity: A Preliminary Statement." *American Sociological Review* 25(2): 161-178. DOI: https://doi.org/10.2307/2092623 (Crossref record confirmed: vol. 25, issue 2, April 1960, starting page 161).
- Read: RECORD-ONLY. JSTOR returned a bot challenge. The only HTML copy turned up by search (a course page at media.pfeiffer.edu) could not be resolved from this environment, and Scribd was not opened. I did not read any primary text.
- What is UNVERIFIED (seen only in search snippets and in a Wikipedia article that attributes the claim to a secondary paper, not to Gouldner directly): that Gouldner proposes a universal norm with two minimal demands (help those who have helped you; do not injure those who have helped you), and that reciprocity works "as starting mechanisms as well as stabilizing functions". The Wikipedia article ("Norm of reciprocity", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norm_of_reciprocity) quotes this wording as from Chen, Chen and Portnoy (2009), *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology* 45(1), not from Gouldner. Do not quote Gouldner for these claims until the 1960 text is checked (a library copy is needed).
- Bearing on norm change (if confirmed): a reciprocity norm that both starts and stabilises exchange is exactly what fails when contributions are no longer reciprocated by readers.

## 4. Epistemic norms as historically contingent social products

### 4a. Craig, *Knowledge and the State of Nature* (1990)
- Reference: Craig, Edward. 1990. *Knowledge and the State of Nature: An Essay in Conceptual Synthesis*. Oxford: Clarendon Press. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1093/0198238797.001.0001
- Read: READ-SEP (Ichikawa and Steup, "The Analysis of Knowledge", SEP, first published 6 February 2001, substantive revision 21 January 2026, https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/knowledge-analysis/, sec. 9 Methodological Options) plus READ-ABSTRACT (OUP abstract via Crossref). The book was not read.
- Quotes:
  1. (SEP, sec. 9) "In particular, Craig suggested that the point of using the category of knowledge was for people to flag reliable informants—to help people know whom to trust in matters epistemic."
  2. (SEP, sec. 9) "Craig's entry-point into the analysis of knowledge was not intuitions about cases, but rather a focus on the role that the concept of knowledge plays for humans."
  3. (OUP abstract) "Craig develops an alternative approach, akin to the 'state of nature' method in political theory, which builds up the concept from a hypothesis about the social function of knowledge and the needs that it fulfils."
- Bearing on norm change: if knowing is a status conferred on good informants, what counts as a good informant is a social standard that can shift when the sources people rely on change.
- Also confirmed: SEP "Social Epistemology" (O'Connor, Goldberg and Goldman, https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/epistemology-social/) lists Craig (1990) among works that made testimony central: "Testimony emerged as a central topic for social epistemology owing to works such as Elizabeth Fricker (1987), Edward Craig (1990), and C.A.J. Coady (1992)."

### 4c. Williams, *Truth and Truthfulness: An Essay in Genealogy* (2002)
- Reference: Williams, Bernard. 2002. *Truth and Truthfulness: An Essay in Genealogy*. Princeton: Princeton University Press. DOI (2010 e-edition): https://doi.org/10.1515/9781400825141
- Read: READ-ABSTRACT (Princeton University Press page, https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691271767/truth-and-truthfulness) plus two secondary summaries read in full: the SEP entry on Bernard Williams (Chappell and Smyth, first published 1 February 2006, revised 28 January 2023, https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/williams-bernard/, sec. 1 Biography) and the NDPR review by Clancy W. - Quotes:
  1. (Princeton UP) "Without denying that we should worry about the contingency of much that we take for granted, he defends truth as an intellectual objective and a cultural value.   2. ## 5. Reactive attitudes and norm violation

### 5a. Strawson, "Freedom and Resentment" (1962)
- Reference: Strawson, P. F. 1962. "Freedom and Resentment." *Proceedings of the British Academy* 48: 187-211. Reprinted in *Freedom and Resentment and Other Essays* (London: Routledge, 2008), DOI https://doi.org/10.4324/9780203882566. Crossref record confirmed for the 2008 volume and for a 2020 reprint chapter (https://doi.org/10.1515/9780691200972-010); the original 1962 record was not checked.
- Read: READ-SEP via Talbert, "Moral Responsibility", SEP (first published 16 October 2019, substantive revision 3 June 2024, https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-responsibility/, secs. 2.2 and 2.2.3). Strawson's essay was not read. The quotations of Strawson below are as the SEP reproduces them, with the SEP's pagination ([1962] 1993).
- Quotes:
  1. (SEP sec. 2.2, quoting Strawson [1962] 1993, 48) The SEP notes Strawson's emphasis on the "importance that we attach to the attitudes and intentions towards us of other human beings".
  2. (SEP sec. 2.2, quoting Strawson 56) "our practices of holding others responsible are largely responses to these things: that is, "to the quality of others' wills towards us""
  3. (SEP sec. 2.2) "According to Strawson, the tendency to respond with reactive attitudes to another's display of good or ill will involves imposing on the other a demand for moral respect and due regard ([1962]1993, 63)."
  4. (SEP sec. 2.2.3) "Due to Strawson's influence, philosophers often now think of blameworthiness as centrally involving an agent's being an appropriate object of certain emotions, particularly resentment."
- Bearing on norm change: resentment is the emotional form of an unmet normative expectation, so it explains how violations register in a community; and where resentment is absent (the person is treated as outside the demand), the norm no longer binds that person. Link to betrayal is my inference, not in the SEP text I read.

## 6. Wikipedia's own norms, commons governance, and AI

### 6a. Butler, Joyce and Pike, "Don't look now, but we've created a bureaucracy" (CHI 2008)
- Reference: Butler, Brian, Elisabeth Joyce and Jacqueline Pike. 2008. "Don't Look Now, But We've Created a Bureaucracy: The Nature and Roles of Policies and Rules in Wikipedia." *Proceedings of the SIGCHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (CHI '08)*, 1101-1110. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/1357054.1357227
- Open copy read: https://www.kostakos.org/courses/socialweb10F/reading_material/5/butler08.pdf (a course-hosted copy, 10 pages; printed page = PDF page + 1100). The ACM page returned HTTP 403.
- Read: READ-FULL (text searched).
- Quotes:
  1. (PDF p. 1, p. 1101, abstract) "Wikis are sites that support the development of emergent, collective infrastructures that are highly flexible and open, suggesting that the systems that use them will be egalitarian, free, and unstructured. Yet it is apparent that the flexible infrastructure of wikis allows the development and deployment of a wide range of structures."
  2. (PDF p. 1) "While the "Ignore all rules" policy itself is only sixteen words long, the page explaining what the policy means contains over 500 words, refers readers to seven other documents, has generated over 8,000 words of discussion, and has been changed over 100 times in less than a year."

### 6c. Ostrom, design principles for commons
- Reference (the book in the brief): Ostrom, Elinor. 1990. *Governing the Commons: The Evolution of Institutions for Collective Action*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press. ### 6e. Open-source and commons contribution norms under AI (arXiv abstracts, read at arxiv.org/abs/)
All four are preprints dated 2026 and were read at abstract level only; I did not read their methods, and none has been peer reviewed to my knowledge.

- Robles, Gregorio and Daniel M. German. 2026. "You can contribute if you... An Empirical Framework of AI Contribution Policies in OSS." arXiv:2609.07919 [cs.SE], 7 September 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.07919. Quote: "AI is reshaping open source software (OSS) contribution by lowering the cost of producing code, documentation, issue reports, and review interactions. This creates opportunities for broader participation, but also disrupts how maintainers assess contributor effort, competence, and accountability." (The abstract's first word is "Artificial intelligence".)
- Hora, Andre and Romain Robbes. 2026. "AI Policy, Disclosure, and Human in the Loop: How Are Contribution Guidelines Adapting to GenAI?" arXiv:2605.16706 [cs.SE]; comment: accepted to ICSME 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.16706. Quote: "We analyzed 1,000 popular GitHub repositories and identified 118 AI policies for contributors. Our results show that (1) 78% of the AI policies allow AI-assisted contributions, while 22% explicitly discourage AI use. (2) 51% of the AI policies require the disclosure of AI-assisted contributions; and (3) 74% of the AI policies require a human in the loop during contribution."
- Afroz, Sadia, Courtney Miller, Tyler Menezes, Edward Gilmour, Anita Sarma and Zixuan Feng. 2026. ""AI Slop is DDoSing Open Source": Understanding the Impact of AI-Generated Contributions on Open Source Sustainability." arXiv:2607.04003 [cs.SE], 4 July 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.04003. Quote: "while PR volume increased in 2025, merge rates declined, with one-time contributors experiencing an 18.18% drop in PR merge rates relative to the counterfactual."
- Robles, Gregorio and Daniel M. German. 2026. "Open Source Stewardship Communities: "We need you, but not your pull request"." arXiv:2609.12236 [cs.SE], 10 September 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.12236. Quote: "AI can replace implementation labor while weakening how OSS communities renew themselves."
- Bearing on norm change: these show commons communities responding to a cheaper, less costly form of contribution by writing new rules (disclosure, human-in-the-loop) or by restricting who may contribute, which is explicit norm revision under pressure. Authors' names were taken from the arXiv metadata of each abstract page.
