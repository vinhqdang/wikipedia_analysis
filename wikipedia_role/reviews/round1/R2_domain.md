### Summary Assessment
The essay asks what role Wikipedia should play once language models reuse its text. Its answer is that Wikipedia should be defended as an auditable, community-held practice of verification and not as a source of truth. It argues that reliance plus strain creates a light duty of upkeep, owed by reusers, and that this duty is conditional on Wikipedia being open and fair. The essay is unusually candid about its limits. The provenance-not-truth reframing, the comparison-class account of credibility, and the point that Section 5 turns the fairness condition back on Wikipedia are real contributions. The philosophical core (Section 3.3) is the weakest part, however. The three families of argument are summarised mostly through encyclopedia entries, and each is used at the point where its own conditions fail. The "conditional principle" is then presented as the common product of three theories that do not support it separately. The step to an epistemic commons is declared to be "the essay's own" without being argued. The central notion, auditability, is stipulated, and its relation to the epistemic dependence the essay itself cites (Hardwig) is not examined. Important literatures are missing: epistemic injustice and Longino-style critical norms, trust and testimony, commons and infrastructure, exploitation, collective responsibility, and data governance. The essay should be publishable as a conceptual essay once the normative argument is rebuilt and the literature base is deepened.

## Strengths

### S1: Provenance-not-truth reframing with a relational account of credibility
The three-place account (a source is credible for a purpose, relative to the alternatives open to the reader) is clear and usefully reframes the "scorned then rehabilitated" story. It is honestly labelled a conceptual claim that cases illustrate and do not prove.
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §2 "Credibility is a three-place relation, a source credible for some purpose relative to the alternatives open to the reader"

### S2: The text/practice distinction is the right place to look
Separating a non-rival text from a rival, labour-dependent practice is the move that makes any reciprocity claim intelligible. It also explains why free reuse and a duty of upkeep can coexist. The use of Cicero's lamp to mark the limit of the analogy is apt.
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §3.3 "Wikipedia's text is non-rival: copying it does not diminish it. The practice that produces and maintains the text is not"

### S3: Section 5 turns the fairness condition on Wikipedia
Noting that Rawls's principle of fairness binds only where the institution is "just (or fair)" is faithful to the source. It also gives the essay's recommendations a principled link to its obligation argument, and few essays of this type make that link.
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §5 "Rawls's principle of fairness binds only where the institution is itself 'just (or fair)'"

### S4: Candour about weak points, and refusal of the betrayal and illegality framings
The essay states Nozick's and Simmons's objections, the analogical status of the natural-duty extension, the contested dependence on Wikipedia, and the lack of editor evidence. It denies any legal duty or claim by individual editors. This restraint is a model for writing in this area.
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §6 "The step from fair play, natural duty and beneficence to an epistemic commons is this essay's own and is open to the weak points"

## Weaknesses

### W1: The "conditional principle" is claimed as the joint output of three arguments that do not support it, singly or together
**Problem**: Section 3.3 says each argument "has a known weak point" and that "together they support a modest answer". The conditions of the final principle are: knowing dependence, strain from reuse, and small cost relative to means. These are not drawn from any one of the three families. Fair play (Hart, Rawls) needs acceptance of benefits within a scheme that is itself fair, with mutual restriction. Natural duty needs a just institution in Rawls's full sense. Beneficence (Singer) needs only the capacity to prevent something bad at small cost, and has no reliance or strain condition at all. The principle is a conjunction of conditions drawn from different theories, and no theory yields that conjunction. Weak arguments do not accumulate unless they are independent routes to the same conclusion. Here they are not: they ground different duties, with different bearers, objects and defeaters. The essay also states the principle only after conceding that fair play is doubtful (the restrictions do not fall on developers), that natural duty is an analogy, and that the weaker Singer principle "does not clearly apply". It is left unclear what, if anything, survives.
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §3.3 "Taken together, the three arguments support a conditional principle: those who knowingly depend on a shared practice that others sustain"
**Why it matters**: This is the normative thesis of the essay, and Sections 4 and 5 depend on it.
**Suggestion**: Either (a) choose the strongest single ground, which I think is a modest Samaritan or fair-play route, and defend it against its best objections; or (b) say explicitly that the principle is a pluralist, reflective-equilibrium proposal and show how reliance and strain enter (for example as the relevance conditions that make a Samaritan duty particular to Wikipedia). If the duty is a Samaritan duty (Wellman), the essay should address the particularity problem it raises. State the principle as a numbered premise and test each premise.
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 5 - core expertise: political obligation

### W2: Fair play is applied while its decisive conditions go unexamined, and the directedness of the duty is not addressed
**Problem**: (i) Hart's principle gives a right to those who have submitted to mutual restriction, against those who benefit from that submission. It is a directed, relational duty owed to cooperators. The essay then says that "the duty runs to the practice" and that editors have no claim. A practice is not a right-holder. The essay needs to say who can claim performance, who can waive, and whether the irrevocable licence is itself a waiver by the cooperators of any fair-play claim. (ii) Klosko's conditions are summarised but never applied. As Klosko states them, the "presumptively beneficial" goods are those "indispensable for satisfactory lives", which is a demanding condition that a source of training text is unlikely to meet. The essay uses only the acceptance route. (iii) The classic free-rider literature (Arneson 1982) requires that the scheme's survival depends on enough contributions. The essay's own figures (editors are about 0.02% of readers; text is already produced and irrevocably licensed; the main finding is mixed or no overall decline in activity) suggest that the marginal reuser's non-contribution makes no one worse off. That bears directly on whether anyone is being treated unfairly. (iv) The reply to "proves too much" accepts that all readers, search engines and teachers owe something, and calls this "a feature". For fair play it is the standard reductio from the free-rider problem and needs an answer, not acceptance. (v) Rawls distinguishes obligations, which arise from voluntary acts under fairness, from natural duties, which do not. The text slides between "duty of fair play", "obligation" and "duty".
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §3.3 "The duty runs to the practice" and "That the principle generalises is a feature" (§6)
**Why it matters**: The problem of who the duty is owed to, and what grounds it for each class of bearer, determines whether the "light, proportionate upkeep" of Section 4 is owed to anyone.
**Suggestion**: Distinguish directed duties (owed to editors, to the community as a plural subject, to readers) from undirected duties to sustain a good. Say which of these the essay defends. Apply Klosko's three conditions to Wikipedia explicitly, and address indispensability. Engage Arneson on the threshold condition and use the figures the essay already holds. Treat the licence as a possible waiver and argue why it does not cover reciprocity of upkeep, since share-alike and attribution show editors did want some return.
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 5 - core expertise: fair play and political obligation

### W3: The natural-duty and beneficence arguments are used where they do not apply, and sourced second-hand
**Problem**: (i) Rawls's natural duty of justice is a duty to support just institutions of the basic structure. Its "excessive cost" wording belongs to the mutual-aid duty (jeopardy or need), while the further-just-arrangements clause says "too much cost". The essay says "excessive cost" for the former. The cited supporting passage is "as summarised in the sources noted in the bibliography", with no such source listed. (ii) Rawls's mutual-aid duty applies to a person in need or jeopardy. The essay concedes the harm is mild, so it does not apply either. This should be said, since the essay lists it. (iii) Singer's principle is not reliance-based: it applies to anyone with the capacity, whether or not they have used or strained the practice. Used as the essay uses it, it also implies that a developer should give to better causes than Wikipedia, so it gives no reason to support this practice in particular (Simmons's particularity objection returns). The "weaker version" is conceded away and the stronger one is notoriously overdemanding; the cost-condition (small cost against means) is met by "the largest firms" only if one ignores the clause "comparable moral importance". (iv) Kant is cited only for beneficence, via SEP. Kant's duty of gratitude (Metaphysics of Morals, Doctrine of Virtue §§32-34) and his publicity principle are more apt and unused. Direct citation to the Akademie pages is needed. Kant's beneficence concerns others' ends and happiness, and the essay does not argue that upholding a knowledge practice falls under it. (v) Nozick, Simmons and Klosko are presented through Dagger & Lefkowitz, and Rawls, Kant and Singer partly through quotation chains. The essay says "many sources used at the level of abstracts". For a philosophical essay, the primary texts of Hart, Rawls, Nozick, Simmons and Klosko should be read directly.
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §3.3 "Rawls's (1999, p. 99) natural duty of justice ... (as quoted in Dagger & Lefkowitz, 2021, section 4.4)" and "as summarised in the sources noted in the bibliography"
**Why it matters**: A specialist reviewer will test the quotations and applications against the primary texts first.
**Suggestion**: Cite primary pages. Correct the "too much cost" and "excessive" wording and cite where each appears. Either drop mutual aid explicitly or justify it. Put Singer in the context of the literature that adapts it to cooperative or collective settings (Murphy 2000, Cullity 2004) [UNVERIFIED as to exact titles]. Rework the Kant paragraph around gratitude and publicity if it is to be retained.
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 4 - core expertise; Klosko "indispensable" wording checked against SEP, other page details from memory

### W4: The extension to an epistemic commons is declared, not argued
**Problem**: The essay says three times that the step to an epistemic commons "is the essay's own" and open to the weak points above. Candour is welcome, but the essay offers no argument for why a knowledge practice is the kind of thing these principles apply to. In particular: (i) the premise that the practice is "just or fair" in Rawls's sense is never assessed in Section 3, while Section 5 itself lists evidence that it may fail the condition (gender and geographic gaps, punishment of dissent). By the essay's own test, the claim on reusers may currently be defeated or weakened, and the essay never says so. (ii) The strain premise rests on four weak links: crawler load (documented, and not all AI-related); attention diverted (mixed evidence); a recruitment funnel from readers to editors (the essay admits no study); and contamination of the text (conceded to "lie beside the argument"). (iii) The "practice" combines at least three things: the editing community, the Foundation's infrastructure, and the rule system. The duty is said to run to the practice, yet the means in Section 4 (Enterprise payments) go to the Foundation, and the essay notes this does not reach editors. This needs a theory of the steward or trustee as an intermediary. (iv) There is no engagement with the closest existing analogues for non-rival goods and reciprocity: copyleft's reciprocity design and its failure on network use (the AGPL response), the Titmuss gift-relationship literature on anonymous gifts to strangers, and Sahlins's generalised reciprocity.
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §3.3 "The extension is the essay's own step and is offered as an argument by analogy"
**Why it matters**: "Conceptual" does not excuse the essay from giving reasons. The recommendations in Sections 4 and 5 are only as strong as this step.
**Suggestion**: Give the analogy its own subsection: list the relevant features of a political institution (coercive, comprehensive, non-exit) and of a voluntary open practice, and argue which are relevant to the duty. Say plainly whether the justice condition is currently satisfied. Add a short account of the intermediary role of the Foundation. Engage the copyleft/AGPL story as the nearest institutional analogue.
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 4 - core expertise in obligation; adjacent knowledge of commons and licensing

### W5: "Auditability" is stipulated, and its epistemic value and fit with the cited social epistemology are not shown
**Problem**: (i) The essay says provenance, contestability and answerability are "a working vocabulary for this essay, not terms from the literature". Yet auditability has a literature (accountability and audit studies; O'Neill on intelligent accountability and trust; algorithmic auditing), and the notions the essay wants are central to social epistemology: Longino's norms of critical discourse (public venues, uptake, shared standards, equality of intellectual authority) are an almost exact fit and are absent. (ii) Hardwig's argument is that lay people and even experts cannot audit most of what they rely on. Epistemic dependence is dependence without checking. The essay never says who audits in practice. If few readers follow citations or talk pages (evidence exists on reference click-through [UNVERIFIED: Piccardi et al., WWW 2020]), auditability is mainly a potential held by a small group, and its value to readers must be explained through a division of epistemic labour (Kitcher, Goldman) and through trust in the processes. That is a different claim from "readers can check". (iii) "Auditability, not truth" is presented as a contrast. Auditability matters epistemically because it supports error correction and calibrated trust. A highly auditable practice with poor accuracy would not be worth defending. The essay's own comparison-class argument (Wikipedia is reliable beside a model that fabricates) relies on reliability. The dichotomy should be recast as an instrumental or constitutive relation. (iv) Hardwig is used for "knowledge known by the community". Wikipedia disclaims knowing: it compiles what published sources say (the essay's own provenance point). The knowers are in the sources, so dependence on Wikipedia is dependence on dependence, a second-order relation. Hardwig's own trust condition (the character and competence of experts) is also not what Wikipedia offers; Hardwig 1991, "The role of trust in knowledge", is the relevant companion. (v) Provenance is no longer a distinctive property: retrieval-augmented systems cite sources, as the essay says in one clause. Only contestability and answerability remain distinctive, and these are the least developed. "A community with rules answers for what stands" is asserted without any discussion of answerability to whom, with what sanction, and with what failures (capture cases such as the Croatian edition [UNVERIFIED: Foundation's 2021 assessment] and the Holocaust-distortion case in the Polish-language material [UNVERIFIED: Grabowski & Klein 2023]).
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §2 "They are a working vocabulary for this essay, not terms from the literature" and "auditability, not truth, is what makes Wikipedia worth defending"
**Why it matters**: Auditability is the property the essay says deserves defence and is its central thesis. A reader will ask why it, and not reliability, carries the value.
**Suggestion**: Introduce auditability as a stipulated notion explicitly connected to Longino and to accountability theory. Add a paragraph on who exercises audits and how audit value reaches non-auditors (calibrated trust, Anderson on lay assessment of experts). Reframe Hardwig honestly as second-order dependence. Replace "not truth" with a statement that auditability is valuable for its connection to error correction. State what audit failure looks like (capture, edit warring, systemic bias) and explain why the community can still be said to "answer".
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 4 - core expertise in social epistemology; Wikipedia capture cases need checking

### W6: The treatment of trust and betrayal is selective, and trust is otherwise missing from a social-epistemology essay
**Problem**: The "note on betrayal" uses Hawley's commitment account to conclude that nobody was betrayed, because nobody committed. This is one account among several. Baier's account places trust in reliance on goodwill and discretion, and betrayal arises without an explicit commitment. Holton's participant-stance account ties betrayal to the reactive attitudes and so to the feelings the essay sets aside (it says "we may feel betrayed, but we have not been betrayed"). The essay picks the narrowest account and uses it to define the question away; it should say why. The Stack Overflow case also shows that expectations were formed by community norms and the platform's own terms, which fits the broader accounts. More broadly, there is no discussion of epistemic trust in a pseudonymous crowd, though the literature on this is where the essay's central claim would be tested (see Missing References). I could not verify the exact Hawley sentence quoted.
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §3.3 "On the commitment account (Hawley, 2014), betrayal requires a commitment that was broken"
**Why it matters**: The essay uses betrayal to set aside one ground of grievance, and its conclusion depends on one contested account.
**Suggestion**: Present at least Baier and Holton alongside Hawley and say what follows on each. A short section on trust in testimony from anonymous or collective sources would situate the "relational standing" claim.
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 4 - core expertise in trust; Hawley quotation unverified

### W7: The gift argument via Mauss is thin, and gratitude and exploitation are absent
**Problem**: Mauss is cited "in its standard reading" in one sentence to say that gifts create reciprocity but that no claim arises here. Mauss describes reciprocal gift exchange among identified parties in archaic societies. The relevant questions for an anonymous gift to all (generalised reciprocity in Sahlins; Titmuss on blood donation; Parry's critique of the "pure gift" ideology) are not touched. The 2024 Routledge edition is cited without a page. Gratitude is the most obvious source of an obligation to return, and the essay does not discuss it (Kant's duty of gratitude, Card, Manela, Berger [UNVERIFIED as to exact titles]). Exploitation, which concerns taking advantage of cooperative labour on unfair terms, is the nearest relative of "reliance without return" and is not mentioned (Wertheimer 1996; Goodin 1987; Sample 2003; the digital-labour literature). Even if the essay rejects these routes, a specialist will expect to see why.
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §3.3 "Mauss (2024), in its standard reading, treats gifts as creating obligations of reciprocity"
**Why it matters**: The essay's answer on "what is owed" would be sharper if it explained why gratitude and exploitation do not carry the claim.
**Suggestion**: Add a short paragraph on each, saying whether it supports or defeats the principle. Cite Mauss by page and use Sahlins and Titmuss for anonymous gifts.
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 4 - core expertise in gratitude and exploitation

### W8: The "who bears the duty" objection is answered by assertion
**Problem**: The essay raises the problem that reliance varies across thousands of reusers and a duty without an assignable bearer is easily ignored. It answers with "pooled arrangements with published terms". That answers a design question, not the philosophical one of how a shared responsibility is distributed. The collective-responsibility and shared-duty literature (Young's social connection model; Kutz on complicity; Murphy's cooperative principle; Nefsky on the difference-making problem; Parfit's mistakes in moral mathematics) addresses exactly this and is not cited. It would also clarify why proportionality to "reliance and capacity" is the right distribution.
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §6 "Reliance varies across thousands of reusers, so a duty with no assignable bearer is easily ignored"
**Why it matters**: The recommendation of pooled arrangements follows from this objection.
**Suggestion**: Add a paragraph on how duties in collective settings are discharged and distributed, then derive the pooled-arrangement recommendation from it.
**Severity**: Minor
**Confidence**: 4 - adjacent expertise: collective responsibility

### W9: Literature base is thin for the commons, infrastructure, epistemic injustice and data-ethics strands
**Problem**: The commons strand rests on one 2007 edited volume and one 2023 preprint. Ostrom's design principles (boundaries, appropriation and provision rules, monitoring, graduated sanctions, nested enterprises), Frischmann's work on infrastructure and the demand-side of shared resources, and Benkler and Nissenbaum on commons-based peer production are absent, though they bear directly on the text/practice distinction and on open-access versus commons regimes. The social-epistemology strand cites Hardwig and Fallis. It omits Goldman, Kitcher, Longino, Fricker (epistemic injustice, directly relevant to Section 2 and 5.3 on Tripodi and Gallert & van der Velden), Coady and Lackey on testimony, and the philosophical papers on Wikipedia and trust (Tollefsen; Magnus [UNVERIFIED as to exact titles]). Infrastructure studies (Star & Ruhleder; Bowker & Star; Edwards) are needed to support the claim that facts are "black-boxed" in Ford and Wajcman. The data-ethics strand lacks relational and stewardship accounts of data governance (Viljoen; Delacroix & Lawrence; Jernite et al.). See Missing Key References.
**Evidence Anchor**: absence: References and §3.3-§5 — expected the Ostrom/Frischmann commons literature, Longino/Fricker/Goldman/Kitcher in social epistemology, and a data-governance source; checked the reference list, §2, §3.3, §4, §5
**Why it matters**: The essay claims a place in social epistemology and the ethics of commons; a specialist reader will expect those conversations to be acknowledged and engaged.
**Suggestion**: Add and engage the references below as part of rewriting W1, W4 and W5, not as a bibliography patch.

### W10: Section 5 mixes the normative and the empirical, and its "ought" is hypothetical
**Problem**: The argument is that Wikipedia's claim on others is "only as strong as the practice is open, contestable and fair". This makes Section 5 a set of conditions for making a claim, not duties of Wikipedia, and the essay later says the recommendations "can be pursued whether or not reusers help". Two different grounds are therefore at work (strategic or conditional, and intrinsic), and the essay does not say which it relies on. Section 5.3 also carries a long original empirical analysis (Wikidata query results and edit counts) whose relation to the philosophical argument is not drawn. It reads as a different paper. These parts are for Reviewer 1 on method, but for the domain argument the problem is a loss of focus in a section that should be argued from the justice condition.
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §5 "The claim that those who rely on Wikipedia should help keep it going is only as strong as the practice is open, contestable and fair"
**Why it matters**: A reader cannot tell whether Section 5 says "reform or lose your claim" or "reform because it is right".
**Suggestion**: State the grounding of each recommendation. Move the Wikidata analysis to an appendix or a companion paper, or tie it to the justice condition in a sentence.
**Severity**: Minor
**Confidence**: 4 - core expertise

### W11: Cicero's passage supports the giver's duty, not the recipient's
**Problem**: The Ennius lamp passage (De officiis I.51-52) is used to say that it "supports the duty, since if nothing is lost there is no reason to refuse". In Cicero the duty is on the holder not to refuse a costless service to a stranger (and it comes from his account of justice and of common ownership). It points at the giver, not at a return from the recipient. As used, it supports free use more than any duty of upkeep. The better reading for the essay's purpose is that Cicero shows why non-rival goods carry no claim of return, which makes the move to the rival practice the whole argument (see S2). Jefferson's taper letter is the usual modern statement of the same image and would help.
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §3.3 "it supports the duty, since if nothing is lost there is no reason to refuse"
**Why it matters**: A specialist reader will catch the direction of the duty.
**Suggestion**: State whose duty the passage supports, and use it for the point it fits.
**Severity**: Minor
**Confidence**: 4 - core expertise in classical sources and sharing ideals

## Detailed Comments

### Title & Abstract
The title ("last bastion") sets up the essay's own correction in Section 2 and works well. The abstract states a conclusion ("light, proportionate upkeep") whose normative grounding the body does not carry (W1, W4).

### Literature Review / Theoretical Framework
- Coverage: the Wikipedia-studies literature is good. The moral and political philosophy is represented by the right names (Hart, Rawls, Nozick, Simmons, Klosko, Singer, Kant, Cicero, Hawley, Hardwig, Mauss), but mostly through secondary sources (W3). The social-epistemology and commons literatures are thin (W9).
- Application: Hart and Rawls on fair play are quoted accurately, as I recall them, and the essay is right that the principle requires a just institution. The Nozick example is correctly attributed. Simmons's acceptance requirement and particularity objection are fairly stated. Klosko's conditions are incomplete (indispensability missing) and unused (W2). Hardwig is quoted accurately but stretched (W5).
- Research gap: the gap is real (no philosophical account of what AI developers owe a knowledge commons), but the essay's first move should be to show the gap against the existing data-governance, commons and exploitation literatures, not only Wikipedia studies.

### Academic Argument Quality
- Factual accuracy: the main philosophical misstatements are small (Rawls cost wording; "excessive" versus "too much"; missing Klosko indispensability; Cicero direction).
- Argument logic: the chief gaps are the cumulative-support move (W1), the unargued extension (W4), and the contrast of auditability with truth (W5).
- Terminology: obligation, duty and claim are used interchangeably. "Practice", "commons", "community" and "Wikipedia" shift reference. "Epistemic commons" is used in the conclusion without being defined; the introduction speaks of a knowledge commons.

### Contribution to the Field
The contribution is a conceptual reframing (provenance and audit, not truth), plus a modest conditional principle and a set of reforms. It is real and incremental. The overclaiming risk is moderate: "the conditional principle", "what is owed", and "should" in Section 5 outrun the argument given.

## Questions for Authors
1. Who holds the claim that the principle generates? If editors have waived any claim by the licence, who can demand upkeep, and does the duty exist without a claimant? Is it a directed or an undirected duty?
2. Does Wikipedia, as it stands, satisfy the justice or fairness condition you invoke in Section 5? If not, is the claim on reusers currently defeated, and what follows for Section 4?
3. If few readers audit, what does auditability do for non-auditors? How does your account of auditability differ from the reliability that the comparison-class argument depends on?
4. Which of the three arguments would you retain if you had to defend only one, and which of the principle's three conditions would that argument leave out?
5. What would count as evidence against the strain premise (reliance plus strain), given the mixed empirical findings you cite?
