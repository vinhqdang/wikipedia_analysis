## Overall Recommendation
**Major Revision.** The Buddhist material is mostly quoted accurately, and the manuscript is honest about its limits. But the lens is thinner than the title and abstract promise. Its central move, applying the "two extremes" to obligations, rests on a conflation the tradition would not accept. The conventional half of the two truths is borrowed, and the ultimate half and the soteriological purpose are left out. The paper's real contribution survives if the lens is demoted and re-described accurately.

## Weaknesses

### W1: The two extremes are mapped onto a different dichotomy from the one the tradition names
**Severity**: Major | **Confidence**: 4 (core expertise: Madhyamaka and early Buddhist views)
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §5.1 "In the original context the extremes concern persons and selves. Applying them to norms is an analogy"; §5.2 "The eternalist stance about reuse holds that the obligation ... is part of the nature of the situation"

**Problem.** The paper's own quotation from Hayes defines the extremes in terms of essences (svabhāva) of things. The next sentence then says they "concern persons and selves". These are two different historical layers.
- In the early texts, sassatavāda and ucchedavāda concern the fate of the self after death. Eternalism says the self persists. Annihilationism says it is cut off at death, with no fruit of action.
- In Madhyamaka they concern svabhāva. To say "it exists" is eternalism, and to say "it does not exist" is annihilationism (MMK 15.10-11, from memory).

The paper's "eternalism" is something else: the view that obligations have timeless, condition-independent grounds. That is closer to moral realism or non-conditionality. Its "annihilationism" is the view that nothing is owed. It is closer to moral nihilism or contractualist voluntarism.

The two dichotomies are not isomorphic:
- The eternalist's error in Buddhism is attributing an unconditioned essence. The paper's "eternalists" (Rawls, Hart, Singer) do not attribute essences to Wikipedia's norms. They attribute binding force from general principles. Rawlsian fair play is explicitly conditional on benefits accepted, institutional justice, and so on (as the paper itself shows in §5.2).
- The tradition's annihilationism (ucchedavāda) is notable because it denies the fruit of action: the Ajita Kesakambalī view in the Sāmaññaphala Sutta (DN 2, from memory), "there is no gift, no offering". That is much closer to "nothing is owed" than the paper realises, and the tradition's refutation is the point of dependent arising: actions have conditioned consequences. The paper never draws on this.

**Why it matters.** An informed reviewer will say the "three stances" are a rhetorical tripartition (strong duty, no duty, weak conventional reason) with Buddhist labels attached. The paper's §5.2 conclusion, that each "eternalist" account "fails at a different premise", already shows these accounts are conditional. That undercuts calling them eternalist.

**Fix.** Either (a) drop "eternalism" and "annihilationism" as labels for the obligation views and call them unconditional-ground and no-ground views, using the Buddhist pair only as an analogy in one paragraph; or (b) rebuild the mapping around the tradition's own link between annihilationism and the denial of karmic consequence, and argue that "nothing is owed" is the structural analogue of denying fruit. Option (b) is stronger and more faithful, because it connects to what Buddhist ethics actually says about conditioned consequences of giving and receiving.

### W2: The two truths are used halfway
**Severity**: Major | **Confidence**: 4
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §5.4 "all one is left with is conventional truth, truth which consists in agreement with commonly accepted practices and conventions"; §7 "the two truths and the middle path supply the conventional standing of norms"

**Problem.**
1. §7 claims the two truths are used. §5.4 uses only the conventional half. There is no ultimate truth in the essay's account, and no relation between the two.
2. Saṃvṛti, the Sanskrit term behind "conventional", has a second meaning in Candrakīrti: "concealing" or "obscuring". The classical position is that the conventional is real for ordinary beings, and also a veil for the ignorant. On the Prāsaṅgika reading, conventional truth is soteriologically defective, and the point of seeing emptiness is to stop reifying it. The paper uses the "conventionalist" reading, that conventional truth is agreement with practice. That is a respectable modern reading (Garfield, Westerhoff, Siderits), but it is not neutral. The paper says "one reading and not the only one", which is helpful. It does not say which alternatives exist, so the reader cannot see the cost of the choice.
3. In the tradition the two truths are not a theory of social obligation. Nāgārjuna's concern is the cessation of suffering through understanding emptiness (MMK 24.8-10, from memory). The Arnold passage the paper uses is about the compatibility of emptiness with ethics, i.e. that agents are not eliminable. It does not say that conventions bind.
4. "Agreement with commonly accepted practices" is a gloss. The Buddhist notion of conventional truth is not equivalent to Lewis-style convention or Bicchieri-style norms. It is about the ordinary level of description (persons, tables, selves) rather than about regulative rules.

**Fix.** Rename the §5.4 section to say the paper borrows a conventionalist reading of conventional truth, and in one sentence note the saṃvṛti = "concealing" sense and the Prāsaṅgika/Svātantrika divergence (Tillemans; Cowherds). Say plainly that the paper does not use the ultimate truth and the claim in §7 is only about the conventional truth. Or delete the claim that "the two truths" are used.

### W3: The "improvement" sentence is stretched from truths to norms
**Severity**: Major | **Confidence**: 4
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §5.4 "since 'it is at least sometimes advantageous to treat truths as if they had a more than conventional grounding', the point is that 'such practices need improvement from time to time'"

**Problem.** In Westerhoff's s. 3.5 the sentence concerns the epistemic practices by which we form and revise beliefs about the world, including science. The paper turns "such practices need improvement" into a licence for deliberate reform of social obligations, and then (in the third part of the view) into the claim that "a convention is better or worse according to whether it serves the function that gave it point". That second step is the paper's own teleological claim. It is plausible, but it is a Lewisian and Aristotelian functionalism and not a Madhyamaka thesis. I see no Madhyamaka warrant for "function that gave it point". Madhyamaka is hostile to essentialist teleology, and the tradition would more naturally frame improvement as upāya (skilful means) judged by liberative effect.

**Fix.** Mark the step from epistemic practices to norms as the paper's own, which it partly does at the end of §5.4. Move the functionalist criterion out of Buddhist attribution. If a Buddhist anchor is wanted for "judge conventions by what they are for", use upāya or the raft simile (see W5).

### W4: Dispensability: the lens adds little beyond norm theory, and the paper nearly concedes it
**Severity**: Major | **Confidence**: 4 (STS/peer-production side: adjacent)
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §7 "If those distinctions could be drawn without the vocabulary, the vocabulary would be dispensable, and the essay would stand."

**What the lens does contribute.**
- It supplies a disciplined question ("which specific conditions?") and a caution against reifying (§2.5).
- The "do not reify, do not nihilate" shape gives the section on obligations a memorable structure.

**What it does not contribute.** Bicchieri, Lewis and Young already say norms are conditional on mutual expectations, can lapse without change in values, and can be re-instituted (§2.1, which the paper itself sets out). That is the entire analytic content of "conditioned, real while conditions hold, defeasible, open to reform". Sections 3-4 and 6 are independent of §5's Buddhist labels. The actual novelty lies in the auditability function (§2.4) and the dispute-record proposal (§6.1). Neither depends on Buddhism. The title, abstract and keyword "impermanence" therefore over-advertise the lens. A reader who comes for Buddhist philosophy will find three paragraphs and a few quotations; a reader who comes for norm theory will find the Buddhist frame unnecessary.

My verdict is that the paper could drop the lens without loss to its argument, but would lose some rhetorical coherence. This is worse than neutral: the lens carries risk (W1-W3) with little payoff. The honest options are (a) shrink the lens to a short framing device in §1 and a one-paragraph analogy in §5, and revise the title and abstract; or (b) extend it substantively so it earns its place, using the resources below that the lens could add which norm theory lacks:
- **Non-attachment to form** (the raft, MN 22) as a principled stance for "hold the function, release the form". This is the paper's best fit and is not explicitly used.
- **Consequences of giving** (dāna and kataññutā, gratitude) as a tradition-internal account of non-contractual reciprocity (see Section 4 below). This would speak directly to "resentment without breach".
- **Sammuti / Aggañña Sutta** as a Buddhist account of social conventions and their origin in agreement (see Section 4).

Option (b) turns a decoration into an argument. It needs the Buddhist-ethics literature below.

### W7: Wikipedia and peer-production claims: accuracy and blind spots
**Severity**: Major | **Confidence**: 3 (adjacent field; I could not verify the 2026 sources)
**Evidence Anchor**: absence: §2.2 and §3.1 — expected mention of the ShareAlike condition of CC BY-SA and of Foundation-versus-community tension; checked §2.2, §3.1, §5, §6.4, References

I split this into points. Items marked (memory) are from my own knowledge and not verified here.

1. **ShareAlike is missing.** §2.2 quotes the licence as "royalty-free, non-sublicensable, non-exclusive, irrevocable" and says the attribution duty is "the rest was expectation". The licence cited is CC BY-SA 4.0. Its ShareAlike condition is itself a reciprocity norm encoded in the licence (adaptations must be released under a compatible licence). Whether model weights or outputs are adaptations is the contested question in the licensing debate. The claim "nothing is encoded about return" in §3.1 needs qualifying: ShareAlike encodes a return, and the dispute is whether it is triggered. This is a substantive accuracy gap in the third strand. Fix: add a paragraph on ShareAlike and say it is untested for model training.
2. **Foundation versus volunteers.** The paper treats "the Foundation" as steward and often as a proxy for "the community". Its §6.4 correctly asks the Foundation for transparency, but the essay does not discuss the historical friction between the two (e.g., the 2014-15 Superprotect and media viewer disputes; the June 2025 pause of the AI-summaries pilot after editor objection) (memory, UNVERIFIED). 3. **"Mostly anonymous" volunteers (§2.2)** are mostly pseudonymous. This matters for the answerability claim, since pseudonymous accounts with histories are accountable in a way anonymous contributors are not. Fix: change the wording.
4. **Reference form.** "Dang Nguyen, Dejean, & Jullien (2017)" lacks initials and may mis-parse the surname. Also check the final author list for this paper. Fix: verify the author list and format.
5. **Sources I could not verify.** The following are dated 2026 or later and lie beyond what I could check here: the 44-2 vote of March 2026, the *Doe v. GitHub* Ninth Circuit decision (Sept. 16, 2026), the Enterprise 30% cap (Becker 2026), and several preprints. I treat them as the author's responsibility. The paper does flag that Foundation figures come from an interested party.
6. **Global South and smaller editions.** The scope statement says "The focus is the English-language edition" and then Appendix A gives a single quantitative check on Vietnamese. The conclusions on norms (credit, route back, dispute record) are English-edition conclusions. The paper's own evidence (Bhattacharya 2026 on regional-language editors; Graham et al. 2014; Gallert & van der Velden 2014) suggests the conditions of §2.5 (scarcity of free reference, reader-to-editor path, cost of reuse) differ greatly. In small editions the path from reader to editor is thin, bot-created stock dominates, and LLM quality for those languages is lower, so a dispute record may not exist to travel. §6.1 should say what the proposal means where little dispute exists. 7. **Peer-production literature.** Benkler's commons-based peer production and Yochai Benkler / Shaw & Hill (2014) on the "iron law of oligarchy" in Wikipedia would strengthen §2.3. ### W8: The "function" claim has a Buddhist analogue the paper could use, and an important mismatch it ignores
**Severity**: Minor | **Confidence**: 3
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §6 "The function is auditable testimony. The form includes the institutional centrality of one encyclopedia"

"Holding function, releasing form" is closer to the Buddhist idea of using a convention as a means (raft, finger and moon) than to anything the paper attributes to Nāgārjuna. But the Buddhist version has a different telos (liberation from suffering), and the function it protects is not "auditable testimony". The paper should avoid implying that Buddhism endorses the function it picks. Say that the function is chosen by the essay on epistemic grounds.

## Detailed Comments

### Assumption audit
- **Explicit**: Norms are expectation-dependent and can lapse (§2.1); the authority of Wikipedia is a complex of three strands (§2.2). Both are stated and defended.
- **Implicit**: (i) That "obligation" is the correct category for what reusers owe, rather than gratitude, reciprocity or care. Buddhist ethics tends to frame these as virtues and dispositions (kataññutā) and not as duties. This is the largest implicit assumption and the lens could have challenged it. (ii) That the Foundation's requests express the contributors' expectation. (iii) That the reader-to-editor path is the key reciprocity mechanism. For many contributors the motive is not return visits (the paper's own §2.2 motives are mixed).
- **Paradigmatic**: The deontic and contractual vocabulary (debt, owed, commitment, betrayal) is Western analytic. A Buddhist framing would ask about intention (cetanā), the effect on the community, and the cultivation of generosity. That alternative would change what counts as "betrayal" in §4.

### Cross-disciplinary connections
- Parallel research: Buddhist ethics of conventions and gift (Section 4). Hybrids of Buddhism and technology ethics (e.g., Vallor 2016 on technomoral virtues includes cross-cultural virtue ethics, but it is not specifically Buddhist; UNVERIFIED for Buddhist content).
- Borrowing: sammuti (agreed convention) in the Aggañña Sutta; Vinaya procedure (the Sangha's power to amend minor rules).
- Method: none needed for a conceptual essay; interviews with Vietnamese-language editors would answer the paper's own §4.2 gap.

### Practical impact
The dispute-record proposal (§6.1) is concrete and useful. It is feasible for the paid Enterprise feed, though adoption by reusers is uncertain and the paper says so. In small editions, volumes of dispute are low (see W7.6).

### Broader implications
The paper takes care not to overclaim harm. An ethical risk in the Buddhist framing is that "impermanence" can be read to console acceptance of loss that falls on volunteers. A Buddhist-studies reader would note that the tradition insists impermanence is not a reason for indifference (compassion and skilful action follow from seeing conditions). The paper should state that "lapsing" does not imply "acceptable".

### How these would strengthen or correct the argument
1. **Sammuti and the Aggañña Sutta** show that early Buddhism itself treats social order and property as products of agreement within conditions, which is a tradition-internal source for "norms are conventional, real, and revisable". It also shows that conventions are not morally empty, because the sutta ties them to the consequences of greed. This supports §5.4 better than the Westerhoff passage does, and avoids the stretch from truth to norm (W3).
2. **Vinaya revisability.** The Sangha's rules were adjustable: in DN 16 the Buddha says the Sangha may abolish minor rules after his death (from memory, DN 16 5.? needs checking). That is a Buddhist institution designed for "release the form, keep the function", and it offers a precedent for §6's recommendation and for the succession planning of §6.4.
3. **Annihilationism as denial of consequence** (W1).
4. **Gratitude as a virtue, not a debt** (kataññutā). This directly addresses §4's puzzle of resentment without breach, since the tradition values acknowledgement of benefit without grounding it in a contract. It also corrects the paper's deontic framing.
5. **Vietnamese Thiền and engaged Buddhism** for a regional voice, used with care. Note that "interbeing" is a modern reading, not early Buddhism, which the paper already has the machinery to say.

## Questions for Authors
1. Is the paper's "eternalism" the view that obligations are unconditioned, or that they are grounded in essences? The paper's own §5.2 shows the "eternalist" accounts are conditional. What exactly makes them eternalist rather than merely non-contingent?
2. If annihilationism in the tradition denies the fruit of action, what would the paper's argument look like if "nothing is owed" were treated as the denial that giving and using have conditioned consequences?
3. Which reading of saṃvṛti does the paper adopt, and would the argument survive the Prāsaṅgika "concealing" reading?
4. Is the title's "impermanence" needed? Which sentence in §§3-6 would fail without it?
5. The licence is CC BY-SA. What follows for the third strand from ShareAlike, and from the unresolved question whether model training creates an adaptation?
6. Do the Foundation's requests for credit and payment represent editors? What would count as evidence either way?
7. What does the dispute-record proposal mean in editions where there is little dispute to record?

## Recommendation signal
**MAJOR REVISION.** The paper is honest and its Buddhist quotations check out, but the central analogy (W1), the two-truths claim (W2) and the dispensability of the lens (W4) need rework, and the ShareAlike and Foundation-versus-community gaps (W7) affect the third strand. The core contribution (auditability and the portable dispute record) stands independently and can be preserved.
