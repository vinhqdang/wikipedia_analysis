## 1. Introduction

In 2007 the history department at Middlebury College voted to bar its students from citing Wikipedia, after a historian traced near-identical errors in a set of examinations to Wikipedia entries (Jaschik, 2007; Waters, 2007). Two years earlier a *Nature* exercise had found that the encyclopedia's science entries were not much less accurate than Britannica's (Giles, 2005). Students were already using it, and so were some scientists (Head & Eisenberg, 2010; Giles, 2005).

In 2025 the Wikimedia Foundation published a statement titled "In the AI era, Wikipedia has never been more valuable". It says that almost all large language models train on Wikipedia datasets, and it asks their makers to credit the human contributors and to pay for access (Miller, 2025; Wikimedia Foundation, 2025d). In the same period human readership fell by roughly 8%, a fall the Foundation attributes in part to search engines and chatbots that answer directly, often from Wikipedia's own content (Miller, 2025). A source once scorned is now relied on, and is losing readers to the systems that rely on it.

It is tempting to tell this as a story of decline, or as a story of betrayal. This essay tells it as a lesson in how norms change. Wikipedia's authority, its model of contribution and its expectations of those who use it were never fixed properties. They were a set of social norms that held for about two decades under particular conditions, and several of those conditions have now shifted. The question is what that shows about the status of the norms, about what the people who rely on Wikipedia owe it, and about what is worth keeping.

The essay's resource is the idea of impermanence as it appears in Buddhist philosophy: that what is conditioned arises in dependence on conditions and passes when they change, and that the right response lies between treating such things as eternal and treating them as nothing. The idea is used as an interpretive lens, with the limits set out in Section 5, and each concept it supplies is tied to a specific claim. The argument runs as follows. Wikipedia is a complex of credibility, contribution and reciprocity norms, whose function is to make testimony auditable (Section 2). The conditions that sustained the complex are changing, and the norms are lapsing in some places and being rewritten in others (Section 3). The feeling often described as betrayal registers lapsing expectations and does not by itself show a broken commitment (Section 4). Between the view that the obligations of reuse are timeless and the view that nothing is owed lies a view on which they are conventional, real while their conditions hold, and open to deliberate reform (Section 5). The task that follows is to hold the function and release the form, and Section 6 says what that requires.

The essay does not claim that Wikipedia is failing, that reuse of it is unlawful, or that the harm of reuse is large. The argument is conceptual and rests on public documents and the scholarly literature. Empirical premises carry their dates and sources, and the text says where the evidence is thin or comes from an interested party. The focus is the English-language edition.

## 2. Wikipedia as a conditioned complex of norms

### 2.1 What a norm is, and what keeps one in place

On Bicchieri's account, summarised in the *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, a social norm is a behavioural rule that people follow because of conditional preferences: they prefer to conform "conditional on 'empirical expectations' (i.e., first-order beliefs that a certain behavior will be followed) as well as 'normative expectations' (i.e., second-order beliefs that a certain behavior ought to be followed)" (Bicchieri et al., 2023, section 1). The entry adds that "having conditional preferences means that one may follow a norm in the presence of the relevant expectations, but disregard it in its absence" (section 5). Lewis's conventions have the same structure: they are arbitrary, self-perpetuating solutions to recurring coordination problems, sustained because "no one has reason to deviate from it, given that others conform" (Rescorla, 2024, section 3.2). Two consequences matter here. A norm persists only while the expectations that support it are mutually held, so it can lapse without anyone changing their values, when expectations about others shift. And norm change is typically not smooth: "there are long periods of no change punctuated by occasional bursts of activity in which an old norm is rapidly displaced by a new one" (Young, 2015, p. 363).

### 2.2 Three strands

Wikipedia's authority rests on three strands of norms, each with a history.

*Credibility.* The policy asks editors to base articles on "reliable, independent, published sources with a reputation for fact-checking and accuracy", and says that content "is determined by published information rather than editors' beliefs, experiences, or previously unpublished ideas" (Wikipedia contributors, n.d.). What it promises is provenance, that a reader can check where a statement came from, and not truth. When Middlebury barred citation, the historian who proposed the rule reported that a Foundation representative agreed, since Wikipedia is, like a print encyclopedia, a tertiary source (Waters, 2007). The standing of the encyclopedia has moved with the set of alternatives. Fallis (2008) defended it comparatively, as comparable in accuracy to traditional encyclopedias and better than other free sources, and a scientist quoted in the *Nature* report observed that print encyclopaedias are "set up as the gold standards" against which "faster or cheaper resources" are judged (Giles, 2005, p. 901). Credibility is a three-place relation, a source credible for some purpose relative to the alternatives open to the reader, so a change in the alternatives changes the standing of the source without any change in the source. Craig's (1990) account of the concept of knowledge makes the point generally. On the reading given in the *Stanford Encyclopedia*, "the point of using the category of knowledge was for people to flag reliable informants—to help people know whom to trust in matters epistemic" (Ichikawa & Steup, 2026, section 9). What counts as a good informant is then a social standard answering to a social need, and it can shift when the available informants do.

*Contribution.* Wikipedia is written by volunteers, mostly anonymous, under a licence that grants a "worldwide, royalty-free, non-sublicensable, non-exclusive, irrevocable" right to reproduce and share (Creative Commons, 2013, section 2(a)(1)). The norm is that the work is given, and the Foundation's terms say that the licences "do allow commercial uses of your contributions, as long as such uses are compliant with the terms of the respective licenses" (Wikimedia Foundation, 2023). The motives behind it are mixed. In experiments with Wikipedia contributors, reciprocity and social image were strong motives for sustaining cooperation and altruism was not (Algan et al., 2016); among superstar contributors only social image concerns continued to predict contribution levels (Hergueux et al., 2024). A survey experiment found that most respondents, including users who did not edit, chose an equal split, which the authors read as adherence to a social norm of sharing (Dang Nguyen et al., 2017). All three are abstract-level readings, but they agree that contribution rested on norms of return and recognition and not on pure gift.

*Reciprocity in use.* The remaining strand concerns readers and reusers: that people come to the site, that those who copy credit the source, that those who benefit do not hollow it out. The Foundation states it as the hope of "a virtuous cycle that continues those human contributions that create the training data that these new technologies rely on" (Wikimedia Foundation, 2025d). The licence supplies an attribution duty that applies when material is shared (Creative Commons, 2013, section 3(a)(1)); the rest was expectation.

### 2.3 Norms that revise themselves

The norms were never static. Governance in Wikipedia has been described as "less akin to rule imposition by external authority than to constant reform and refinement of social norms within the community" (Forte & Bruckman, 2008, p. 2), and an early study found that the anti-bureaucratic norm "Ignore all rules", sixteen words long, had generated a page of over 500 words, referred readers to seven other documents and changed over a hundred times in less than a year (Butler et al., 2008, p. 1101). Halfaker et al. (2013) report that active English editors peaked at 56,400 in March 2007, and trace the decline to quality-control machinery, including tools that reject contributions, which treats good-faith newcomers badly, and to norm-articulation mechanisms that calcified against changes proposed by newer editors. So the norms of the complex were already impermanent from within: the machinery that made the practice reliable thinned the community that carried it.

### 2.4 The function: auditable testimony

If the complex is conditioned, the question is what it is for. The proposal here is that it makes testimony auditable. Three properties describe what the community provides. They are a working vocabulary for this essay and not terms from the literature: *provenance* (a claim points to a published source), *contestability* (any claim can be challenged through a recorded process), and *answerability* (a community with rules answers for what stands). Together they are auditability.

Auditability is not a rival to truth. It is valued in the way social epistemology values practices generally, by their tendency to promote true belief (Goldman, 1999), and it serves truth-tracking in two ways: it lets errors be found and corrected, and it lets readers calibrate how far to trust a claim. Hardwig (1985) argued that a chain of appeals to authority "must end somewhere", with "someone who possesses the necessary evidence" (p. 337), and that where no individual has all the evidence one may have to accept "knowledge that is known by the community, not by any individual knower" (p. 349). Most readers cannot audit most claims, and Wikipedia compiles sources and does not produce the knowledge in them, so the dependence is second-order. The audit is divided: editors check the link from claim to source, the fields that produced the source answer for its content, and the reader can verify that a source exists, sample the check, and see whether a claim has been disputed. What the reader gets is not an audit but the assurance that a check is possible and has been attempted by people who answer for it. Menking and Rosenberg (2021), drawing on Code's epistemic responsibility and Longino's procedural objectivity, argue that the project's pillars privilege the product over the process, and that authority can assert itself through accuracy and reliability "precisely because authority can always be questioned" (p. 469). Magnus (2009) describes the reader's side: Wikipedia varies a great deal in quality, so the reader must judge where it is likely to be reliable, and he argues that it frustrates, to a greater or lesser degree, the strategies readers use with other sources. Provenance and a record of dispute are the cues that judgement needs. Tollefsen (2009) asks whether the answerable party is the individual editors or the community as a source of group testimony, and the essay leaves that open.

Systems that retrieve and cite sources now offer a link from claim to source, so provenance in the narrow sense no longer separates Wikipedia from a generated answer. Whether the cited sources support the claims they accompany is an empirical question this essay does not settle. What a citation link does not carry are the other two properties, the record of dispute and a community with rules that answers for the result (Section 6.1).

### 2.5 Conditions of arising

On the early Buddhist formula of dependent arising, "when this exists, that comes to be; with the arising of this, that arises. When this does not exist, that does not come to be; with the cessation of this, that ceases" (Vélez de Cea, n.d., section 3.f). The point is that "things are dependent on specific conditions", and the strong reading that everything depends on everything is a later development (section 3.f). Applied to the norm-complex, the formula asks which specific conditions held it up. Drawing on the sources above, four can be named: scarcity of trusted free reference, so that Wikipedia stood well in the relevant comparison; a path from reader to editor, which the Foundation's own call to "elevate opportunities to visit and participate in those sources" (Miller, 2025) presupposes; a cost of reuse high enough that those who copied also credited and visited; and a licence culture in which giving freely was met by a shared expectation of acknowledgement. These conditions are the essay's reconstruction and not an established causal history. Each has now changed.

## 3. The conditions change

### 3.1 The licence is silent

The licence says nothing about training a model and nothing about returning anything to the source. The steward of the licences reads their reach narrowly: "AI training is often permitted by copyright", so that "the CC license conditions have limited application to machine reuse" (Pearson, 2025), and its guidance "was developed for a world of reuse by people, a premise that doesn't fit as neatly in a world of widespread machine use" (Creative Commons, 2026). Courts have not filled the gap. *Doe v. GitHub* concerned code under open-source licences, not Wikipedia text: the Ninth Circuit affirmed dismissal of a Digital Millennium Copyright Act claim on the theory that Copilot's outputs remove copyright management information, treated the training-stage theory as forfeited, and left two contract claims pending (*Doe v. GitHub, Inc.*, 2026). Lemley and Casey (2021) argue that training should generally be fair use, in which case licence conditions are not reached at all. Nothing found here establishes that reuse of Wikipedia text breaches its licence, and the essay does not assume it does. The gap is not a violation: it is the point at which the third strand of norms, which the licence never encoded, was left to expectation alone.

### 3.2 Reuse at scale, and the readers who no longer arrive

Much of the evidence in this subsection comes from the Foundation, which is an interested party, and is marked as such. Models draw on Wikipedia's text: the Foundation says almost all large language models train on its datasets (Miller, 2025), though its share is small in volume, since Wikipedia and Wikibooks together, in English and Simple editions, are about 0.14% of the tokens in one open corpus, and Wikipedia is 4.5% of the sampling mixture in another (Soldaini et al., 2024; Touvron et al., 2023). Any reliance is on curation and quality and not on bulk, and it is contested whether it can be replaced (Shumailov et al., 2024; Gerstgrasser et al., 2024).

Two kinds of cost must be kept apart. The first falls on infrastructure. At least 65% of the most resource-consuming traffic to the Foundation's data centres comes from bots, which account for about 35% of pageviews, and bandwidth used for multimedia has grown by 50% since January 2024, "largely" from scrapers feeding images to AI models (Mueller et al., 2025); not all bot traffic is linked to AI developers. Wikimedia Enterprise, the paid-access service, earned $8.3 million in fiscal 2024-25, 4.0% of Foundation revenue, from 13 commercial customers (Wikimedia Foundation, 2025a), and in January 2026 named further partners without terms (Wikimedia Enterprise, 2026). The second falls on the path from reader to editor. The Foundation reports human pageviews down roughly 8% against 2024 and attributes this to search engines "providing answers directly to searchers, often based on Wikipedia content" (Miller, 2025). The academic evidence is mixed: in twelve editions through early 2024 views and visitors did not fall and rose less where ChatGPT was available, while edits and editor numbers showed no fall (Reeves et al., 2025), against larger declines in editing and viewing for new, popular articles overlapping with ChatGPT's output (Lyu et al., 2025). The first cost is the better evidenced. Whether the second erodes the supply of editors is not established.

### 3.3 Two other norm-complexes

The comparison with Stack Overflow and Reddit shows that norm-complexes of this kind change in different ways. Stack Overflow lost its contributors: monthly new questions that are still visible fell from 207,171 in March 2014 to 5,262 in October 2025 (author's retrieval from the Stack Exchange data interface, October 2026), and activity fell by 25% within six months of ChatGPT's release relative to comparison forums where it was less available, which the authors treat as a lower bound (del Rio-Chanona et al., 2024). Its owner had earlier announced a partnership under which OpenAI would "provide attribution to the Stack Overflow community within ChatGPT" (Stack Overflow, 2024). Reddit did not shrink: its 2025 filings report revenue of $2.2 billion, up 69%, and 121.4 million daily active users in the fourth quarter, up 19% (Reddit, Inc., 2026a, 2026b). Wikipedia lost a modest share of attention, with falls of 6.7% and 7.9% in English-language views in 2025 and 2026 (author's retrieval from the public pageviews interface, October 2026), while keeping its scale and its finances. A loss of norms need not be a loss of the institution, and the reverse.

### 3.4 The community rewrites its own norms

The community has responded. It has run a cleanup project against machine-generated text, adopted a narrow speedy-deletion criterion, and in March 2026 voted 44 to 2 to prohibit LLM-generated or rewritten article content (Bansal, 2026; Froneman, 2026). In interviews with 16 editors who had used language models, Zhou et al. (2026) report that newcomers used the tools to fill gaps in knowledge, that fitting the output to community norms took tacit knowledge they lacked, and that other editors accepted such edits from experienced editors and called out and rejected them from newcomers. The same thing is happening elsewhere. In a study of 1,000 popular repositories, 118 had AI policies for contributors, over half requiring disclosure of AI-assisted contributions and about three-quarters a human in the loop (Hora & Robbes, 2026), and open-source maintainers report that AI-assisted contributions raise volume while merge rates fall, with one-time contributors hit hardest (Afroz et al., 2026); both are preprints read at abstract level. Communities are writing down norms that were once tacit because a cheaper form of contribution has broken the assumptions behind them.

## 4. What lapsing norms feel like

### 4.1 Resentment without breach

The reaction to this reuse is often called betrayal. Strawson's account of reactive attitudes, as summarised in the *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, holds that "our practices of holding others responsible are largely responses to" the "quality of others' wills towards us", and that responding with a reactive attitude "involves imposing on the other a demand for moral respect and due regard" (Talbert, 2024, section 2.2). Resentment is the emotional form of an unmet normative expectation. Holton (1994) locates the difference between trust and mere reliance in a participant stance, a readiness to feel betrayal if reliance fails, as against the objective stance in which another's failure produces annoyance and not resentment. Hawley (2014) puts commitment at the centre of betrayal. To trust is to believe that someone has a commitment and to rely on them to meet it; commitments may be implicit, since "mutual expectation and convention give rise to commitment unless we take steps to disown these". But expecting what nobody committed to produces a feeling and not a fact: "we may feel betrayed, but we have not been betrayed".

The norm account explains how the feeling can arise without a breach. While the empirical and normative expectations of the third strand were mutually held, a contributor had reason to expect acknowledgement and a visit in return. Where reuse at scale no longer needs the visit, the empirical expectation fails first, and the normative expectation may persist as a sense that it ought to hold. The result is resentment, which reports the expectation, and is not yet a finding of wrongdoing. Real commitments exist, but they run to platforms and not to editors, as in the Stack Overflow partnership above, and the Foundation's requests create an expectation on one side and not a commitment on the other.

### 4.2 What editors think

What editors themselves think of this reuse has not been measured systematically, and the argument does not assume an answer. The nearest evidence is indirect. In expert interviews one participant reported that the objection heard most often from Wikipedians is that "the situation doesn't seem fundamentally fair", since editors work unpaid under an open licence while large firms profit (Vetter et al., 2025); this is a second-hand report from six experts and not an account from editors. Editors from regional-language communities quoted by a news outlet spoke of wanting models trained on quality content and of fixing knowledge gaps, and none of those named reported payment or credit (Bhattacharya, 2026). A survey of editors on the future of the project was being prepared by volunteer researchers and had no results on its project page (Gelauff, 2026). A norm that is lapsing should show up exactly so: some members still hold the expectation and feel its breach, and others have already stopped holding it.

### 4.3 Tacit norms become explicit

A mark of lapse is codification. Where a norm was tacit while conditions held, it is written down once it is threatened: the Foundation's requests for credit and for access through its paid service (Wikimedia Foundation, 2025d), Creative Commons' signals, described as "a flexible, commons-friendly framework for communicating expectations around AI use of content or data" and not called binding (Creative Commons, n.d.), and the contribution policies above. Longpre et al. (2024) document the other response, in which data holders without a channel for stating terms close their data: a growth of crawl restrictions in 2023-24. Writing a norm down turns a shared expectation into a request and a request into a candidate rule, which is how norms are re-instituted. Bicchieri and Mercier (2014) note that "it may take a small number of 'trendsetters' who question the standing norm and start behaving differently to effect a major change" (manuscript p. 5), and Centola et al. (2018) found in experiments that when a committed minority reached about a quarter of the group it could overturn an established convention, though in a pure coordination setting that models conventions and not sanction-backed norms. Sunstein (1996) names the agents, norm entrepreneurs, and the process, norm cascades through shifting reputational incentives. These accounts suggest where the opportunity lies: not in appealing to a lapsed expectation, but in a few visible reusers adopting a norm of credit, route back and carried provenance that others then follow.

## 5. Three stances toward impermanence

### 5.1 The lens and its limits

In early Buddhist thought, the standard formula is that whatever is conditioned is impermanent: "whatever becomes, that is conditioned, volitionally formed, dependently arisen, that is impermanent" (Vélez de Cea, n.d., section 3.d). The sense relevant here is ordinary impermanence, things that last for a while and then go, and not the later doctrine of momentariness (Siderits, 2023, section 3). The two errors to be avoided are named in the tradition as eternalism and annihilationism. The first is "the doctrine that all things exist because of an eternal essence", the second "the doctrine that things have essences while they exist but that these essences are annihilated just when the things themselves go out of existence", and the middle path avoids both (Hayes, 2023, introduction). In the original context the extremes concern persons and selves. Applying them to norms is an analogy, and it earns its place only if it organises a real distinction, which the next two subsections try to show. The lens is an interpretive device, drawn from standard scholarly sources, and the essay does not rest any premise on Buddhist doctrine being true. No source was found that applies the idea of impermanence to social institutions or technology, so the application is the essay's own.

### 5.2 Eternalism: timeless obligations

The eternalist stance about reuse holds that the obligation of those who draw on a practice is part of the nature of the situation, whatever conditions hold. The accounts in moral and political philosophy that come closest are three, and each can be read as an attempt to find permanent ground.

*Fair play.* Hart (1955, p. 185) held that when persons conduct a joint enterprise according to rules and restrict their liberty, "those who have submitted to these restrictions when required have a right to a similar submission from those who have benefited by their submission", and Rawls (1964) made it a duty of fair play. Rawls's later statement requires that the institution be just and that one "has voluntarily accepted the benefits of the arrangement or taken advantage of the opportunities it offers to further one's interests" (Rawls, 1999, p. 96). Developers who choose Wikipedia as training material have arguably taken advantage of its opportunities. But Nozick (1974) objected that benefits nobody asked for cannot bind; Simmons (1979) replied that obligations arise only where benefits are accepted and recognised as others' cooperative product; and Klosko (1992) asks that the goods be presumptively beneficial, later "indispensable for satisfactory lives" (all as reported in Dagger & Lefkowitz, 2021, section 4.2). Developers have not joined the scheme, since its restrictions fall on editors, and Wikipedia's text is hardly indispensable to firms that could use other data.

*Natural duty.* Rawls's (1999, p. 99) natural duty of justice "requires us to support and comply with just institutions that exist and apply to us" (as quoted in Dagger & Lefkowitz, 2021, section 4.4), and rests on no voluntary act. But it concerns just institutions in a full political sense, so applying it to a voluntary association is an extension by analogy, and Simmons's particularity objection asks why one institution and not another.

*Beneficence.* Singer (1972) holds that "if it is in our power to prevent something bad from happening, without thereby sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, we ought, morally, to do it", and in a weaker version that this holds for something "very bad" and without sacrificing "anything morally significant". On Kant's account beneficence is an imperfect duty, "to sometimes and to some extent" aid others (Johnson & Cureton, 2022, section 5), which leaves latitude about form. The harm found in Section 3.2 is mild, so the weaker principle does not clearly apply. A cooperative account, on which each agent owes a fair share of what is needed to meet a problem given that others do their share (Murphy, 2000; Cullity, 2004), fits the case better.

Each account fails at a different premise: joining the scheme, the justice of the institution, the badness of the harm. They do not add up to a duty, because they ground different duties. What they share is a premise, that those who knowingly benefit from a practice that others sustain, and can help at small cost, have a reason not to leave its upkeep to others. That is a reason and not a duty, and it is a reason whose force depends on whether the practice and its expectations are in place. This is already the move to the third stance.

### 5.3 Annihilationism: nothing is owed

The opposite stance holds that because the norms are mere conventions, and no one committed to them, nothing is owed. A gift to anyone, under an irrevocable royalty-free licence, creates no claim for individuals, whatever Mauss (2024) says of reciprocity in general. The Open Source tradition's refusal of restrictions on fields of endeavour expresses the same culture. This stance is right about one thing: editors are not owed payment or credit as a debt for past gifts, and the essay makes no such claim. It goes wrong in treating the lapse of a norm as the end of any reason. Bicchieri's account says that conditional preferences depend on expectations, and expectations that are still partly held, as Section 4.2 suggests, still shape what is reasonable to expect. Nor is a conventional norm nothing. A convention such as the credibility norms of Section 2.2 was the means by which a large body of knowledge was produced and checked. And the essay does not call reuse exploitation: exploitation can be mutually beneficial and consensual yet unfair (Zwolinski et al., 2022), but whether developers take unfair advantage depends on a theory of fair division that the essay does not supply.

### 5.4 The middle way: conventional reasons within conditions

The middle position takes norms to be real as conventions, dependent on conditions, and open to improvement. Madhyamaka thought supplies the structure. On the reading of Nāgārjuna given in the *Stanford Encyclopedia*, "there can be no such thing as ultimate truth, a theory describing how things really are, independent of our interests and conceptual resources", and "all one is left with is conventional truth, truth which consists in agreement with commonly accepted practices and conventions"; and since "it is at least sometimes advantageous to treat truths as if they had a more than conventional grounding", the point is that "such practices need improvement from time to time" (Westerhoff, 2026, section 3.5). Arnold (n.d., section 2.c) puts the ethical corollary: if "there is nothing 'more real' than the world as conventionally described", the persons in it can still be regarded as ethical agents. Garfield (2010) takes up the question in what sense conventional truth is truth and not falsehood. Westerhoff presents this as one reading and not the only one, and the essay uses it as such.

The view has three parts. First, norms are real as conventions. A norm of credit and route back, so long as the relevant parties hold the expectations of Section 2.1, binds in the way conventional norms do, by making it reasonable to expect compliance and to resent its absence. Second, they bind within their conditions. When the expectations lapse, so does the standing of the reason, and the reason that remains is the thin one of Section 5.2, to not leave the upkeep of a practice one benefits from to others. Third, they can be improved. Because norms are conventional they can be criticised by what they are for, and re-instituted. A convention is better or worse according to whether it serves the function that gave it point. The function, per Section 2.4, is auditable testimony, so the test of a norm of reuse is whether it keeps testimony auditable.

This yields an account of what reusers owe that is neither a timeless duty nor nothing. They owe, in proportion to their benefit and their capacity, a conventional and defeasible reason to take part in re-instituting norms that keep the function alive. It is a weak reason, as Sections 5.2 and 5.3 would lead one to expect, and it is stronger where expectations are still held and the practice is fair to those who join it. Its form is left to the one who has it, within limits. The step from these accounts to an epistemic commons is the essay's own; the nearest work is Hess and Ostrom's (2007) point that shared resources survive through community rules and not licences alone, and Huang and Siddarth's (2023) proposal that developers contribute quality data back to the commons they draw on.

## 6. Holding the function, releasing the form

If the norms are conditioned, the practical question is which part to hold. The function is auditable testimony. The form includes the institutional centrality of one encyclopedia, particular rules, and the habits of one era of the web. The recommendations below are the essay's own, drawn from the literature, and each is limited by how much of that literature the essay has reviewed. Only the community and its stewards can decide them. They do not depend on whether reusers contribute.

### 6.1 Let the dispute record travel

What models take is the text, which is the output of the practice. They do not take the properties that make the practice auditable. Provenance travels in the text's references, but the record of dispute and the community that answers for the result stay behind. The Foundation's paid service already carries part of the trail. Its data dictionary lists parsed references and, for each revision, the editor's name, the edit comment and three machine-generated credibility signals, a revert risk, a reference risk and a reference need; for the previous version it gives only an identifier and a character count, and it lists no field for talk-page discussion or dispute history (Wikimedia Enterprise, n.d.). What travels is therefore provenance and a measure of how risky an edit looks, and not the contest itself. Revision histories and talk pages are already public on the site, so the proposal concerns what travels in structured feeds and reuse products, not disclosure. The recommendation is that Wikipedia extend what travels to the record of contest, meaning the discussion, reverts and protection history behind a claim, and ask reusers to carry it, so that a reader of a generated answer can follow a claim back to its source and to the dispute about it. This is the specific practical claim that follows from locating the function in auditability, and it is a request that a few visible reusers could adopt first.

### 6.2 Protect the way in

The practice is carried by people, and its binding constraint is who can join. Experiments give a mixed picture. In a controlled trial on the English edition in 2014-15, newcomers sent an invitation to the Teahouse, a place to ask questions, delivered by a bot and signed by a named host, were more than 10% more likely than the control group to be editing three to four weeks after registering and to have made five or more edits two to six months later (Morgan & Halfaker, 2018); the analysis counts invitation and not participation, since only 331 invitees took part, and its authors treat the result as conservative. A newcomer homepage combining suggested tasks, a mentor module and help links, tested in 2021 on 27 non-English editions with 244,060 new accounts, slightly raised early activation but had no significant effect on two-week retention, on productivity or on revert rates (Warncke-Wang et al., 2023). The two differ in design, edition and period, so the contrast is suggestive only. The defence against machine-generated text raises the risk of the old pattern, since tools for rejecting contributions applied at scale reject good-faith newcomers with the bad (Halfaker et al., 2013; Zhou et al., 2026). The recommendation is that the community measure and publish newcomer survival alongside its measures of machine-generated text, and test pairing rejection with a personal, named explanation and invitation, with the added volunteer labour counted as a cost.

### 6.3 Release what should lapse, and widen without loosening

Some of the form deserves to lapse. The same rules that give the practice its legitimacy produce its gaps. Graham et al. (2014) find that despite millions of hours of volunteer labour the encyclopedia's geography is uneven and clustered. Tripodi (2023), using 38 months of deletion data on 22,174 biographies, finds women consistently over 25% of the biographies nominated for deletion while they were under 19% of all biographies, and reads the higher rate at which such nominations were kept as evidence of mistaken nomination, an inference open to challenge. Ford and Wajcman (2017) argue that the expertise required to take part is coded as male. Gallert and van der Velden (2014) describe a catch-22 for oral knowledge: the reliable-sources rule excludes oral transmission, and keepers of knowledge are judged too close to their subjects. Fricker's (2007) framework of testimonial and hermeneutical injustice names how some knowers are discounted, and Menking and Rosenberg (2021) considered it and set it aside as less compatible with the encyclopedic project; the gaps can be read through it, which this essay does not attempt. Ostrom's design principles make a related point about survival: most individuals affected by a resource regime should be authorised to participate in making and modifying its rules (Ostrom, 2010, design principle 3). The recommendation is that the community publish how notability and deletion decisions fall across categories of subject, so that the disparity Tripodi infers can be tested, and develop documented procedures through which community-held knowledge may enter, designed with the communities that hold it, so that widening is done by procedure and not by lowering the standard of checking. A widened practice that cannot be audited would lose the function that makes it worth keeping.

### 6.4 Open the books, and plan for succession

The argument asks reusers for transparency and asks the same of the steward. The Foundation controls the budget for community programmes (Menking & Rosenberg, 2021) and announced partners for its paid service without giving terms (Wikimedia Enterprise, 2026). It states that Enterprise income is capped at 30% of annual revenue and that its content will "forever be free and open to anyone" (Becker, 2026). The recommendation is that it publish the terms of reuse agreements, or the principles that govern them, and an account of how income from reuse is divided between infrastructure and the support of participation. A published account would turn promises into commitments that others can check.

The lens adds a further requirement, which is to plan for the passing of the form. If institutions are conditioned, the function should be able to outlive any one of them. The licence is irrevocable and the content public, so the text can persist. The audit record, the discussion and the history of dispute, is the part most at risk of being left behind, and archiving it in portable form is a way of making the function survive a change of institution. Part of the recommendation in Section 6.1 is therefore also an act of succession planning. None of the proposals is a prediction that it will succeed, and none is a plan: they are the ways of holding the function that the argument allows.

## 7. Objections and conclusion

*The lens is decoration.* The test is whether each borrowed concept does analytic work. Dependent arising supplies the question of which conditions held the norms up; the two extremes organise the contrast between treating the obligations of reuse as timeless and as nothing; the two truths and the middle path supply the conventional standing of norms and their openness to reform. If those distinctions could be drawn without the vocabulary, the vocabulary would be dispensable, and the essay would stand. The conventionalist reading of Madhyamaka is also one reading among others, and no premise rests on its being true.

*Impermanence proves too much and gives no guidance.* Everything conditioned passes, so the observation does not tell anyone what to do. The guidance comes from the further steps: identify the function, ask which conditions supported it, ask what has changed, and judge reforms by whether they preserve the function.

*Conventional reasons are too weak for a philosophy of obligation.* They are weak, and this essay says so. The stronger grounds, fair play, natural duty and beneficence, were examined and each failed at a premise that would bind reusers. Where expectations are firmly held, as in an explicit partnership, a conventional reason is strong; the weakness here follows the thinness of the expectations in question.

*Wikipedia is not fading.* The essay does not say so. Changing conditions are not an ending, Wikipedia keeps its scale and its finances, and the lens applies to a healthy institution as much as a failing one.

*AI answers may serve readers better, so who is wronged?* No one need be. The claim concerns the standing of norms and the function they served. Systems that cite sources already offer a link from claim to source, and where their citations are accurate that serves readers well. What a link does not carry is the record of dispute and an answerable community, which is what Section 6.1 asks Wikipedia to make portable.

*A light reason is too lenient.* A reason of this kind is not the only route. Regulation, contract terms, public funding of the commons and collective control over data are others that this essay does not assess.

The question was what the fate of Wikipedia shows about changing norms. Its authority was a conditioned complex of credibility, contribution and reciprocity norms, whose function is to make testimony auditable. Several of the conditions have changed, and the norms are lapsing, being rewritten, or being written down for the first time. The sense of betrayal registers the expectations that those norms had made reasonable, without by itself showing a broken commitment. Between the view that the obligations of reuse are timeless and the view that nothing is owed lies a view on which they are conventional, real while their conditions hold, defeasible, and open to deliberate reform. What is worth holding is the function, and what is worth releasing includes parts of the form. The most specific thing the argument supports is that the record of dispute behind Wikipedia's claims should be able to travel with them.

Three limits should be kept in view. No study yet measures systematically whether editors hold an expectation of return from AI developers (Section 4.2 gives the indirect evidence), whether readers sent to a model fare worse than readers sent to the source, or how far the sources that Wikipedia cites are being contaminated by machine-generated text. The application of impermanence to norms is the essay's own, no source applying it to institutions was found, and the philosophical sources are used largely through encyclopedia entries and abstracts. And the empirical premises about reuse and its costs come largely from the Foundation.

## Appendix A. A check on a smaller edition

The conditions of Section 2.5 differ across editions, and the practice in smaller ones is thinner. In a comparison of 40 editions in 2018, culturally specific content averaged 23.2% of articles, and the Vietnamese edition, at 2.5%, was the third lowest after Cebuano and Waray (0.1% each), the three editions where, the authors note, bots made a major contribution to creation and translation (Miquel-Ribé & Laniado, 2018). A simpler remeasurement counts articles whose Wikidata item has a country, citizenship, origin or sport-country property pointing to the language's territory, or sits inside an administrative unit of it. It is a lower bound on cultural-context content and not the 2018 measure, and it treats articles without a Wikidata item or with sparse properties as zero, so it may penalise editions with weaker Wikidata linkage; that was not tested. Applied uniformly in October 2026 it gave 1.8% for Vietnamese (23,410 of about 1.30 million articles), against 9.7% to 19.7% for 13 other editions, with a median of 13.5%; only Cebuano (0.4%) and Waray (0.2%) were lower. Five editions (Korean, Polish, Czech, Japanese and Hindi) could not be queried. In the same month the Vietnamese edition had 4,882 users active in the past 30 days, 267 articles per active user against 27 on the English edition (7,247,997 articles, 268,352 active users), and 117 editors made 100 or more edits in July 2026 against 4,230 on the English edition. Almost all new pages in 2026 are created by human accounts, 2,037 of 2,089 in July. A small group maintains a large stock, much of it created earlier by bots. Data are the author's retrievals from the Wikidata query service and the Wikimedia statistics interfaces, with queries and results kept with the project files.
