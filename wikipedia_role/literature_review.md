# Last bastion or relative authority? A narrative review on Wikipedia, large language models and the knowledge commons

Working draft, 3 October 2026.

## 1. Purpose, scope and reading status

This review maps the literature behind an essay in philosophy and technology. The essay rests on two claims.

- **T3, relative legitimacy.** Wikipedia's standing as a "source of truth", or as the last bastion of human knowledge, in the era of large language models (LLMs) is relative. It rose because alternatives collapsed or were contaminated, and it had earlier been disparaged in academia. Each new tool is blamed for eroding skills, and the older tool is then rehabilitated.
- **T2, reuse without reciprocity.** AI firms' reuse of Wikipedia can comply with its CC BY-SA licence and still leave something owed to the people who built it. The everyday word for the unease is "betrayal"; the review treats that as a colloquial label, and tests whether it fits.

The review is narrative and broad, not systematic. Six threads were searched on 3 October 2026: social epistemology of Wikipedia; technological succession and panics about knowledge tools; knowledge commons and peer production; licensing law and the moral philosophy of reuse; empirical and policy facts on Wikipedia and LLMs for 2023-2026; and critiques of Wikipedia as an epistemic institution. About 110 distinct sources were confirmed to exist through a publisher page, a DOI registry record or a preprint server. Thirteen candidates that could not be confirmed were dropped, and a few more that were confirmed only as bare metadata were left out.

**Reading status.** Twelve academic sources were read in full from copies supplied by the author (Baier, 1986; Hardwig, 1985; Giles, 2005; Graham et al., 2014; Halfaker et al., 2013; Hawley, 2014; Holton, 1994; Ford & Wajcman, 2017; Menking & Rosenberg, 2021; Orben, 2020; Tripodi, 2023; Waters, 2007). Hardwig's copy is a scan and was read from page images. No other academic source was read in full. Academic works were assessed from abstracts, catalogue records or institutional summaries, because several publishers (Nature, Sage, Springer, MIT Press, Taylor & Francis) blocked access. Policy pages and institutional posts were read through a page-summarising tool, so quotations should be rechecked on the page. The claims here are therefore claims about what the literature says at the level of abstracts. Section 8 lists what must be read in full before the essay quotes or leans on an argument. Per-source verification notes are in `annotated_bibliography/`.

## 2. How Wikipedia came to be trusted

### 2.1 Dependence, testimony and the anonymous source

Relying on other people's knowledge is ordinary and rational (Coady, 1992), and the philosophical question is how laypeople choose whom to trust (Goldman, 2001). Hardwig (1985), read in full, argues that one can have good reasons to believe a proposition because one has good reasons to think that others have good reasons for it, and that a chain of appeals to authority, if it is to be sound, "must end somewhere" with someone who has the evidence (p. 337). He then presses the point to a conclusion that bears on the essay: if the arguments hold, one must accept "that there is knowledge that is known by the community, not by any individual knower" (p. 349), and he cites Peirce's view that the community of inquirers is the primary knower. On that view the bastion is a community of knowers and not a store of content, which is the shape of the author's own answer that the bastion is the community and its verification. Wikipedia posed that question in an unusual form, with collective and largely anonymous authorship. The main philosophical papers date from 2008-2009. Fallis (2008) argued that its epistemic effects were likely positive, because accuracy was comparable to traditional encyclopedias and better than free alternatives, with added benefits in speed and coverage. Magnus (2009) replied that the strategies people normally use to assess a source, such as credentials and track record, are frustrated by anonymity. Sanger (2009), a co-founder, argued that quality depends on voluntary deference to expertise, which anonymity and aggressive editors drive away. Tollefsen (2009) asked whether an entry is individual or collective testimony and how to judge the trustworthiness of a collective source. The search found almost nothing from this tradition after 2010, and nothing that connects it to LLM reuse of Wikipedia.

### 2.2 What Wikipedia claims to be

Wikipedia does not claim to be a source of truth. Its verifiability policy asks that articles rest on "reliable, independent, published sources" and that content be determined by published information "rather than editors' beliefs, experiences, or previously unpublished ideas". The slogan "verifiability, not truth" is described as the historical wording of the principle (Wikipedia contributors, n.d.). Garfinkel (2008) read this as an appeal to the authority of other publications. Two consequences follow for the essay. The label "source of truth" is an external projection onto a tertiary compiler. And the bastion is downstream of its sources: if the outlets and papers that Wikipedia cites become contaminated, so does its verifiability.

### 2.3 Accuracy evidence

The best-known comparison is Giles's (2005) *Nature* news report, read in full. Experts reviewed matched science entries from both encyclopaedias without being told which was which, and 42 usable reviews came back out of 50. The average science entry in Wikipedia had around four inaccuracies and Britannica's about three; reviewers found eight serious errors, four in each, and 162 versus 123 factual errors, omissions or misleading statements. The report concluded that the difference was "not particularly great", while noting that several reviewers found the Wikipedia articles poorly structured. It was an exercise run by Nature's news team, not a peer-reviewed study of the encyclopaedias. Encyclopaedia Britannica (2006) rejected its method; that rebuttal could not be read. Later work is narrower. Kräenbring et al. (2014) compared drug information in Wikipedia with pharmacology textbooks and reported 99.7% factual accuracy and lower completeness (83.8% German, 93.1% English). Mesgari et al. (2015) reviewed 110 studies of content quality. The evidence is domain-specific, mostly from 2005-2015, and the search found no recent broad comparison, including none against LLM output.

### 2.4 From prohibition to use

The "disparaged, then rehabilitated" story is more tangled than it first appears. Historians were measured (Rosenzweig, 2006) or hostile (Waters, 2007), and Middlebury's history department voted in 2007 to bar citation of Wikipedia (Jaschik, 2007). Yet students were already using it as a starting point for background reading (Head & Eisenberg, 2010), and faculty attitudes varied by discipline and institution (Konieczny, 2016; Minguillón et al., 2018). Okoli et al. (2014) found scholars and librarians among its readers, and Giles (2005) reported a survey of more than 1,000 Nature authors in which over 70% had heard of Wikipedia, 17% of those consulted it weekly, and fewer than 10% helped to update it. Practice seems to have preceded reputation. That supports T3's relational reading, but it undercuts any version of T3 that treats academic disparagement as uniform.

## 3. Succession, panic and relational legitimacy

### 3.1 The recurring pattern

Anxiety that a new technology erodes a human capacity has a long pedigree. The standard origin is the critique of writing in Plato's *Phaedrus*, which Werner (2012) reads as ambivalent and self-undermining, and which Derrida (1981) reads through the double sense of *pharmakon*, remedy and poison. McLuhan and McLuhan (1988) supply "retrieval", the idea that a new medium brings back an older form, which is a natural vocabulary for the rehabilitation of an older tool. Postman (1992) is the critical pole. Orben (2020), read in full, argues that panics over new technologies, mostly concerning children, recur in a cycle that research itself helps to sustain, because psychological research restarts for each new technology without a theoretical baseline.

The empirical base for the best-known recent panic is thinner than its reach. Sparrow et al. (2011) reported that expecting information to be available online shifts memory toward where it is stored. Their first experiment did not replicate in Camerer et al. (2018), though the original authors have pointed to design differences. Marsh and Rajaram (2019) treat offloading as one effect among several. Bennett et al. (2008) criticise the evidence behind "digital native" claims. Ellington (2003), a meta-analysis of 54 calculator studies, found neutral-to-positive effects on operational and problem-solving skills. For generative AI the evidence is early: Kosmyna et al. (2025, 54 participants in the first sessions) and Gerlich (2025, 666 participants, correlational) are both preprints.

### 3.2 What this supports and what it does not

The literature supports a recurring pattern of blaming new tools, and it shows that the panic often outruns the evidence. It does not test the stronger claim in T3, that an older source's legitimacy is a function of the class of alternatives it is compared with. The search found no empirical study of that claim for Wikipedia or any other source. Fallis's (2008) case is explicitly comparative ("better than free alternatives"), and Giles's framing was parity with Britannica, but both are arguments about a moment, not studies of change over time. T3 will have to be argued conceptually, with the succession literature as illustration.

### 3.3 Infrastructure that predates generative AI

Star and Ruhleder (1996) treat infrastructure as relational and tied to practice, which lets the essay ask for whom Wikipedia has become infrastructure. McMahon et al. (2017) showed that Wikipedia and Google depend on each other: removing Wikipedia from results made search worse, while Google's direct display of Wikipedia content reduced visits to Wikipedia. Piccardi et al. (2021) estimated that English Wikipedia sent about 43 million clicks a month to external sites, worth an estimated $7-13 million a month in sponsored search. So intermediaries capturing Wikipedia's value, and returning less attention than they take, is not new with LLMs. What LLMs change is the scale and the mode of reuse.

## 4. The present evidence, 2023-2026

### 4.1 Readership, contribution and money

The Wikimedia Foundation reported that human pageviews across all languages were down roughly 8% against the same months of 2024, after it revised bot detection, and attributed the fall to search engines answering directly with generative AI (Miller, 2025). Its 2026-27 draft plan says Wikipedia is seeing falling pageviews and Google referrals and unprecedented bot traffic, and that nearly 90% of visitors historically arrived through Google (Wikimedia Foundation, 2026b). A pull from the public Wikimedia pageviews API (agent type "user", English Wikipedia, January to September) gives 69.2 billion views in 2024, 64.6 billion in 2025 (-6.7%) and 59.5 billion in 2026 (-7.9%). That is consistent with the Foundation's figure, though the earlier years may still include bots that evaded detection.

Scale and finances look different. The Foundation reported nearly 15 billion views and 1.5 billion unique devices a month in January 2026 (Wikimedia Foundation, 2026a), 94 million edits in 2025 (Wikimedia Foundation, 2025b), and fiscal 2024-25 revenue of $208.6 million, 11% above target (Wikimedia Foundation, 2025c). Bots account for 65% of the most expensive traffic to its core data centres while generating 35% of pageviews, and multimedia bandwidth rose 50% from January 2024, mostly from scrapers collecting training data (Mueller et al., 2025).

Academic estimates of the effect of ChatGPT diverge. Reeves et al. (2024; published as Reeves et al., 2025) found no overall decline in views, visitors, edits or editors across twelve language editions, with growth that was lower where ChatGPT was available. Lyu et al. (2025) found larger declines in editing and viewership for new, popular articles whose content overlapped with ChatGPT's output. These can both hold, because they use different windows and units; read together they describe uneven substitution, not a general collapse. The scale of the decline in aggregate readership therefore needs to be stated with its definition and date.

### 4.2 Two comparison cases

*Stack Overflow* is the clear collapse. Monthly new questions, from a direct query of the Stack Exchange API (visible questions only, so deleted questions are excluded), were 207,171 in March 2014, 105,745 in October 2022, 52,337 in October 2023, 5,262 in October 2025 and 1,179 in July 2026. The decline began years before ChatGPT, but it accelerated afterwards: del Rio-Chanona et al. (2024) estimate about a 16% fall in weekly posts relative to platforms with limited ChatGPT access, and Burtch et al. (2024) found sharp falls, especially on topics where ChatGPT performs well and among newer users.

*Reddit* is not a collapse. Burtch et al. (2024) found no decline in Reddit communities through early 2023, which they tentatively link to social ties. Reddit's fiscal 2025 filings show revenue of $2.2 billion (up 69%) and 121.4 million daily active uniques in the fourth quarter (up 19%), with "other revenue" of $140 million, whose composition is not shown in the filings excerpts read, and the annual report says the company continues to explore content-licensing agreements (Reddit, Inc., 2026a, 2026b). So the premise that Stack Overflow and Reddit are both dead holds for the first and not for the second. The three platforms diverge in an informative way: a community of answers has been displaced, a community of conversation has grown and monetises its archive, and an encyclopedia has lost some attention while staying large.

### 4.3 How central is Wikipedia to model training?

It depends on the metric. In Dolma, the encyclopedic subset (Wikipedia and Wikibooks) is 4.3 billion tokens of roughly 3,059 billion, about 0.14% (Soldaini et al., 2024); in the LLaMA mix, Wikipedia has 4.5% of the sampling weight and is repeated 2.45 times (Touvron et al., 2023). A vendor analysis of about 730,000 ChatGPT conversations found Wikipedia in 5% of citations and in about 18% of conversations that cite sources (Punturo, 2026; vendor data, medium-low trust). The Foundation's claim that almost all LLMs train on Wikipedia (Miller, 2025) is compatible with a small share by volume. Wikipedia's weight is curation and upweighting, not size.

The dependence of AI on human text is contested. Shumailov et al. (2024) show that training recursively on model output causes collapse, and note that human-generated data gain value as synthetic text spreads. Gerstgrasser et al. (2024, preprint) show that collapse is avoided if real data are retained and synthetic data accumulate. Villalobos et al. (2024, preprint) forecast that training sets will match the stock of public human text between 2026 and 2032, with synthetic data and efficiency as escape routes. So the argument that AI needs a continuing supply of human-written commons holds conditionally, not as a theorem.

### 4.4 LLM text inside Wikipedia, and the community response

Brooks et al. (2024) used GPTZero and Binoculars, with thresholds set for a 1% false-positive rate on older articles, and flagged over 5% of newly created English articles as AI-generated, presenting this as a lower bound. Huang et al. (2025, preprint) estimate LLM-related change of about 1% in some categories. A sampling study of new English articles from 2018 to 2026 in this repository (`ai_text/`; working data, not peer reviewed) finds 1.6% flagged since 2024, against 1.0% expected from human text, and a rise in LLM-style wording from 2.0% to 4.4%. These figures sit between the two published estimates and depend heavily on the detector.

The community has acted. WikiProject AI Cleanup was founded in December 2023; the speedy-deletion criterion G15 covers pages with unambiguous signs of unreviewed LLM generation (messages aimed at the user, non-existent references, technical artefacts) and says other signs are insufficient; and a request for comment closed on 20 March 2026 with 44 votes to 2 in favour of prohibiting LLM-generated or rewritten article content, with exceptions for copyediting and translation (Bansal, 2026). Froneman (2026) traces the governance history and argues that consensus governance fell into a "comprehensiveness trap" before it settled on minimal rules.

### 4.5 Reciprocity in money

Wikimedia Enterprise announced Amazon, Meta, Microsoft, Mistral AI and Perplexity as paid partners in January 2026, alongside Google and others, with no contract values given (Wikimedia Enterprise, 2026). In fiscal 2024-25 Enterprise revenue was $8.3 million, 4.0% of Foundation revenue, from 13 paid customers (Wikimedia Foundation, 2025a). The Foundation says commercial revenue is capped at 30% of annual funding and that commercial reusers should help carry the burden of sustaining the commons (Becker, 2026). On the other side of the comparison, Stack Overflow's partnership with OpenAI included a commitment to attribute the community (Stack Overflow, 2024), and Reddit has licensed its content to Google, reported by a law-firm commentary at about $60 million a year (Chintalapoodi, 2024; secondary source).

## 5. Commons, reciprocity and what is owed

### 5.1 Commons theory and the AI turn

Hess and Ostrom (2007) treat knowledge as a commons sustained by community rules, not by the licence alone. Benkler (2002) grounds peer production in intrinsic and social motivation, which suggests that extraction without recognition can erode the motivational base. Boyle (2003) describes enclosure as private property fencing off a commons, a different direction of harm from extraction from an open commons. Lessig (2004) argues for more openness, so a call for permission or strict attribution against AI reuse sits awkwardly with his position.

For generative AI, Huang and Siddarth (2023, preprint) argue that foundation models trained on public data may harm the commons without compensating contributors, and that copyright and privacy approaches are ill-suited; they propose that firms contribute quality data back. Noroozian et al. (2025, preprint) list five open problems, including contribution decline, web closure and the distribution of costs. Longpre et al. (2024, preprint) audited about 14,000 web domains and found rapid growth in restrictions in 2023-24, with about 5% of C4 tokens fully restricted and about 45% restricted by terms of service. The enclosure here is by data holders reacting to AI, which complicates a story in which AI firms fence the commons. The search found no strong peer-reviewed argument that generative AI is good for the commons. The dissent is a pair of preprints (Gerstgrasser et al., 2024; Kallel & El Louadi, 2026).

### 5.2 What the licence promises

The CC BY-SA 4.0 legal code grants rights to reproduce and share the work and adapted material, with duties of attribution and share-alike that attach when material is shared (Creative Commons, 2013). The Wikimedia Terms of Use state that the licences allow commercial uses (Wikimedia Foundation, 2023). Creative Commons itself says that AI training is often permitted by copyright, so licence conditions "have limited application to machine reuse" (Pearson, 2025), that its guidance was developed for reuse by people (Creative Commons, 2026), and that its CC Signals project aims at norms that embed reciprocity (Creative Commons, n.d.). In law, Lemley and Casey (2021) argue that training should generally be fair use; the Open Source Definition forbids field-of-use restrictions (Open Source Initiative, 2007); and the Ninth Circuit's September 2026 decision in *Doe v. GitHub* (read in full) affirmed dismissal of a DMCA claim on the "output" theory, holding that a code-generating tool creates new works and does not "remove or alter" copyright management information from copies, while declining to consider the training-stage "input" theory as forfeited; two breach-of-contract claims remain in the district court (*Doe v. GitHub, Inc.*, 2026). The case concerns code, and it does not decide whether training breaches an open licence's conditions.

The Foundation asks AI developers to credit contributions and to pay through Enterprise, in the language of a "virtuous cycle", but does not say that current reuse breaches the licence (Wikimedia Foundation, 2025d). Taken together, there is no evidence in this literature that licence compliance is lacking. The licence's steward concedes that the licence is the wrong instrument for the worry.

### 5.3 Tools for the moral claim

The philosophical vocabulary is available but has not been applied to this case, as far as the search found. Baier (1986), read in full, defines trust, "on this first approximation", as "accepted vulnerability to another's possible but not expected ill will (or lack of good will) toward one" (p. 235). She distinguishes it from mere dependence (Kant's neighbours who set their clocks by his walk may be disappointed but not betrayed), allows trust to extend to strangers and to be unacknowledged, and proposes as plausible conditions for proper trust that it survives the consciousness of both parties and that the trusted has had an opportunity to signify acceptance or rejection and to warn the trusting. She also argues that the contract model of morality strains when applied to relations between unequals. Holton (1994), read in full, locates the difference between trust and reliance in a participant stance: to trust is to rely on someone while being ready to feel betrayal if the reliance is disappointed and gratitude if it is upheld, whereas a machine's failure produces annoyance and not resentment. He treats trust as a three-place relation (one person trusts another to do certain things) and holds that one can decide to trust without believing the other will deliver. Hawley (2014), read in full, gives the most useful account for the essay. She holds that to trust someone to do something is to believe that they have a commitment to do it and to rely on them to meet it. Commitments in her sense are broad, "implicit or explicit", and she takes it that "mutual expectation and convention give rise to commitment unless we take steps to disown these"; commitments can also be acquired by allowing others to continue to rely on us. Two consequences matter for T2. First, on her account the wrong of betrayal lies in a failure to fulfil a commitment, so being trusted is neither necessary nor sufficient. The question for the essay is therefore whether AI developers hold a commitment, not only whether editors expected something. Second, expecting what nobody has committed to produces a feeling of betrayal without a betrayal: "we may feel betrayed, but we have not been betrayed, and the trustee may rightly complain of our moralising presumption". She also notes that when a commitment is made to a third party, the person betrayed is the one to whom it was made, which weakens the link between trust and betrayal for those who relied on someone else's commitment. For Wikipedia this separates the possible victims. The commitments that exist, such as the licence's attribution duty, the Foundation's own requests, and any public assurances AI developers have given (for example Stack Overflow, 2024), run to specific parties, and editors, readers and the Foundation are not in the same position. Whether open-knowledge convention creates a commitment of reciprocity, or whether it has been disowned, is left open by Hawley and by the literature found.

Thompson (1971) and Scott (1976/2017) supply a pattern of claims that are legal but illegitimate, grounded in a group's shared norms. Mauss (2024) supplies reciprocity as obligation, and the open licence is the hard case for it, since it is a deliberate gift to anyone with no named return. Hart (1955) and Rawls (1964) ground a fair-play duty on those who benefit from a cooperative scheme, but such duties usually presuppose participation. The Stanford Encyclopedia entry on exploitation notes that consensual and mutually beneficial exchange can still be unfair (Zwolinski et al., 2022). The data-as-labour literature (Arrieta-Ibarra et al., 2018) and data leverage (Vincent et al., 2021) recast contributors' claims as remuneration and collective bargaining. These philosophy items were checked mainly at the level of bibliographic records, so the arguments must be read before they are used.

## 6. Holes in the bastion

If the essay calls Wikipedia the last bastion of human knowledge, it must say whose knowledge and which defences. The critical literature is substantial.

- **The verification machinery thins the community.** Halfaker et al. (2013), read in full, report that the number of active English editors peaked in March 2007 at 56,400 and has declined since. They show that the proportion of promising newcomers has held steady while a shrinking share survive their first contributions, and they implicate the restrictiveness of the main quality-control mechanism and of algorithmic tools that reject contributions. They also find that the community's formal mechanisms for articulating norms calcified against change, especially change proposed by newer editors.
- **Geography.** Graham et al. (2014) find uneven, clustered coverage with little content about much of the world, and connectivity alone does not repair it.
- **Gender, notability and neutrality.** Wagner et al. (2016) find gender asymmetries in structure, content and language. Ford and Wajcman (2017), read in full, argue that Wikipedia's infrastructure produces less visible disparities beyond a masculine culture: being a Wikipedian requires sociotechnical expertise that is gender-coded as male, and those who master its "technocratic system of representation", with its emphasis on modular, verifiable facts, become its power brokers. They add that when search engines and fact boxes display Wikipedia's content, its facts fade into the background and are black-boxed. Tripodi (2023), read in full, combines ethnography with 38 months (January 2017 to February 2020) of deletion-nomination data on 22,174 biographies. The share of biographies about women rose from 16.83% to 18.25%, yet women were consistently over 25% of biographies nominated for deletion. Of the nominated biographies that were kept, about 25% were about women and 17% about men (19% on average), which she reads as women being more often miscategorised as non-notable; that reading is an inference from "keep" decisions. She also cites Adams et al. (2019), who found that women academics were not more likely to be deleted, so the evidence is not uniform. Tripodi does not use Fricker's framework. Menking and Rosenberg (2021), read in full, argue that the five pillars entrench the gender gap and that the first pillar gives priority to the product over the process of knowledge production; they propose pillars built on Code's epistemic responsibility and Longino's procedural objectivity, with the focus on the back-end process of aggregation. They write that Wikipedia "is more than just an encyclopedia", a community, which fits the essay's claim that the bastion is a community and a verification process, not a body of content. They also report that editors who challenged the pillars have been punished, citing topic bans in 2014. They considered Fricker's epistemic injustice and Harding's standpoint epistemology and set both aside as "less compatible with or sympathetic to the inherently conservative encyclopedic project" (p. 464).
- **Oral and indigenous knowledge.** Gallert and van der Velden (2014, an unedited preprint chapter) describe a catch-22: the reliable-sources rule excludes oral transmission, and knowledge keepers are judged too close to their subjects. Following the rules faithfully excludes the knowledge the project says it wants.
- **Language editions.** Miquel-Ribé and Laniado (2018) compared 40 editions and found that Vietnamese Wikipedia had the lowest share of culturally specific content, 2.5%, which they link to bot-generated translated content. This is the only Vietnamese-specific quantitative evidence found, and it needs checking against current data.

Defences and qualifications exist. Hill and Shaw (2013) estimate that the share of women among editors is higher than first reported (16.1% globally), though the gap persists. Shi et al. (2019) find that politically diverse editorial teams produce higher-quality articles, which implies that narrower demographics weaken the process. Jemielniak (2014) describes a governed bureaucracy with real power among administrators, neither anarchy nor a flat commons.

## 7. What the review implies for the essay

### 7.1 For T3 (relative legitimacy)

The literature supports a recurring pattern of panic and rehabilitation, a comparative case for Wikipedia's credibility as old as 2008, and the idea that Wikipedia is infrastructure for other platforms. It does not support a general collapse of Wikipedia, since scale and finances remain large and the academic estimates of decline are mixed. It does not support the claim that Reddit is dead. It treats the best-known empirical support for the "Google made us forget" narrative as contested. And the stronger claim that legitimacy is a function of the comparison class has no direct empirical test, so it would have to be a conceptual argument.

### 7.2 For T2 (reuse without reciprocity)

There is no evidence of a licence breach, and the licence's steward says the licence does not address machine reuse. The strongest evidence of an expectation of reciprocity is the Foundation's own request for credit and payment. On Hawley's (2014) account, betrayal requires a commitment held by the party said to have betrayed, which may be implicit, and expectations without a commitment produce only a feeling of betrayal. The open licence is a gift to anyone, which is the hardest case for reciprocity, and the commitments that do exist run to different parties. The comparison with Stack Overflow and Reddit shows a different route, in which a platform sells content its users produced, while the Wikipedia steward negotiates paid access for structured data. Who is wronged, editors or readers, remains unargued in the literature.

### 7.3 Gaps the essay could fill

1. No work connects social epistemology of testimony to LLM reuse of Wikipedia.
2. No empirical or conceptual study tests legitimacy as relative to a changing comparison class.
3. No paper applies the Fricker framework to Wikipedia directly. Menking and Rosenberg (2021) considered it and chose to set it aside for tactical reasons, so the application is an open choice and not an oversight; the empirical materials exist (Tripodi, 2023; Gallert & van der Velden, 2014).
4. No study tests whether Wikipedia editors hold a trust-based expectation of reciprocity from AI firms.
5. Vietnamese-language evidence is thin, and no peer-reviewed text argues that generative AI is good for the commons.

### 7.4 Premises in the working argument to revisit

- "Stack Overflow and Reddit are dead." True for Stack Overflow's question volume; not supported for Reddit.
- "Wikipedia is the only source AI can use." Its share of training data ranges from 0.14% to 4.5% depending on the metric, and its weight comes from curation.
- "Wikipedia is verified, so it is a source of truth." Its policy asks for verifiability, not truth, and its verification depends on outside sources.
- "Wikipedia must become a source of truth in the true sense." This needs a definition of the test (provenance, contestability, accountability), because the present policy does not promise truth.
