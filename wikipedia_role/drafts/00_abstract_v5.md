## Abstract

Wikipedia, once barred from classroom citation, is now invoked as a safeguard against machine-generated falsehood, even as its human readership falls and the licence under which firms train language models on its text says nothing about return. This essay reads that reversal as a lesson in how social norms change. It argues that Wikipedia's authority is a conditioned complex of credibility, contribution and reciprocity norms, whose function is to make testimony auditable through provenance, contestability and answerability; that several of the conditions which sustained the complex have shifted, so that its norms are lapsing in some places, being rewritten by the community in others, and being written down for the first time in requests for credit, paid access and preference signals; and that the sense of betrayal often voiced by contributors registers lapsing expectations without by itself showing a broken commitment. Using the Buddhist idea of impermanence as an interpretive lens, the essay distinguishes three stances toward such norms: eternalism, which seeks timeless grounds of obligation in fair play, natural duty or beneficence and finds that each fails at the premise that would bind reusers; annihilationism, which holds that nothing is owed; and a middle way on which obligations of reuse are conventional, real while their conditions hold, defeasible and open to deliberate reform. The task that follows is to hold the function and release the form. The most specific proposal is that the record of dispute behind Wikipedia's claims should be able to travel with them into the systems that reuse its text. The argument is conceptual, draws on public documents and the scholarly literature, and marks where the evidence is thin or comes from an interested party.

**Keywords:** Wikipedia; large language models; social norms; impermanence; auditability; knowledge commons
