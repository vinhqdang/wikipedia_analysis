# What is Wikipedia for in the era of language models? An auditable practice, not a source of truth

## Abstract

Wikipedia, once barred from classroom citation, is now invoked as a safeguard against machine-generated falsehood, even as its human readership falls and the licence under which firms train language models on its text says nothing about return. This essay asks what Wikipedia is for in the era of language models. It argues that Wikipedia's standing as a source of truth is relational, and that what deserves defence is a community-held practice of verification whose distinctive property is auditability: provenance, contestability and answerability, valued because they serve error-correction and calibrated trust. Language models draw heavily on its text and the licence is silent about that use, while whether the use strains the practice that produces the text is not established. Fair play, natural duty and beneficence each point toward, but none establishes, a duty on reusers; at most they give a light, defeasible reason to contribute to upkeep, which can take several forms. The practice's role depends more on what Wikipedia does itself, and the essay sets out changes, on provenance that travels with reused content, on newcomers, on what counts as knowledge, and on openness about reuse agreements, whose force does not depend on whether reusers contribute. The argument is conceptual, draws on public documents and the scholarly literature, and marks where the evidence is thin or comes from an interested party.

**Keywords:** Wikipedia; large language models; knowledge commons; social epistemology; auditability; stewardship

## 1. Introduction

In 2007 the history department at Middlebury College voted to bar its students from citing Wikipedia, after a historian traced near-identical errors in a set of examinations to Wikipedia entries (Jaschik, 2007; Waters, 2007). Two years earlier a *Nature* exercise had found that the encyclopedia's science entries were not much less accurate than Britannica's (Giles, 2005). Students were already using it, and so were some scientists (Head & Eisenberg, 2010; Giles, 2005).

Today the Wikimedia Foundation can publish a statement titled "In the AI era, Wikipedia has never been more valuable" (Wikimedia Foundation, 2025d). It says that almost all large language models train on Wikipedia datasets, and it asks their makers to credit the human contributors and to pay for access (Miller, 2025; Wikimedia Foundation, 2025d). In the same period human readership fell by roughly 8%, a fall the Foundation attributes in part to search engines and chatbots that answer directly, often from Wikipedia's own content (Miller, 2025). A source once scorned is now relied upon, and the people who maintain it are seeing fewer readers.

It is tempting to read this as one more case of a place for shared knowledge dying, like Stack Overflow, and to ask whether Wikipedia will follow. "Dying" needs a measure, because contributors, readers and business model do not move together. Stack Overflow lost its contributors: monthly new questions that are still visible fell from 207,171 in March 2014 to 5,262 in October 2025 (author's retrieval from the Stack Exchange data interface, October 2026), and activity fell by 25% within six months of ChatGPT's release relative to comparison forums where it was less available, which the authors treat as a lower bound (del Rio-Chanona et al., 2024). Reddit did not: its 2025 filings report revenue of $2.2 billion, up 69%, and 121.4 million daily active users in the fourth quarter, up 19% (Reddit, Inc., 2026a, 2026b). Wikipedia lost a modest share of attention, with falls of 6.7% and 7.9% in English-language views in 2025 and 2026 (author's retrieval from the public pageviews interface, October 2026), while keeping its scale and its finances; the academic evidence on whether it lost contributors is mixed (Reeves et al., 2025; Lyu et al., 2025). The data do not support the claim that Wikipedia shares the fate of the others. The question is what role a widely relied-upon practice, with fewer readers, can keep.

This essay asks what Wikipedia is for in the era of language models. It argues that Wikipedia's standing as a source of truth is relational, and that what deserves defence is an auditable practice of verification carried by a community (Section 2). Language models draw heavily on Wikipedia's text, the licence is silent about that use, and whether the use strains the practice that produces the text is not established (Section 3). On that evidence those who draw on it have at most a light, defeasible reason to contribute to its upkeep (Section 3.3), which can take several forms (Section 4). The practice's role depends more on what Wikipedia does itself. The essay's most specific practical claim is that what reuse takes is the output of the practice and not its audit properties, so that the record of dispute behind a claim should be made able to travel with it (Section 5.1); Section 5 sets out this and other changes, whose force does not depend on whether reusers contribute.

Three bodies of work frame the question. The epistemology of Wikipedia asks whether and when it can be trusted: Fallis (2008) defends it comparatively, Magnus (2009) argues that no single verdict is warranted and that readers must assess where it is likely to be reliable, and Tollefsen (2009) asks whether the community itself is the entity that testifies. Work on Wikipedia and language models raises concerns about data provenance, digital labour and sustainability from interviews with six experts (Vetter et al., 2025). And the commons literature treats shared knowledge as something kept up by rules and institutions (Hess & Ostrom, 2007; Benkler & Nissenbaum, 2006; Frischmann, 2012). The essay joins them by tying a specific property of the practice, auditability, to the question of what language-model reuse leaves it with, and by asking what follows, with the weight of the answer kept deliberately light.

The essay does not claim that Wikipedia is failing, that reuse of it is unlawful, that AI developers have betrayed its contributors, or that the harm is large. The argument is conceptual and rests on public documents and the scholarly literature. Empirical premises carry their dates and sources, and the text says where the evidence is thin or comes from an interested party. The focus is the English-language edition; Appendix A reports a check on a smaller edition.

## 2. What Wikipedia is for

*Provenance, not truth.* Calling Wikipedia a source of truth credits it with a claim it does not make. Its policy asks editors to base articles on "reliable, independent, published sources with a reputation for fact-checking and accuracy", and says that content "is determined by published information rather than editors' beliefs, experiences, or previously unpublished ideas" (Wikipedia contributors, n.d.). The slogan "verifiability, not truth" is now described as historical wording, and Garfinkel (2008) read it as an appeal to the authority of other publications. When Middlebury barred citation, the historian who proposed the rule reported that a Foundation representative agreed, because Wikipedia is, like a print encyclopedia, a tertiary source (Waters, 2007). What it promises is that a reader can check where a statement came from. "Source of truth" can also mean accurate or deferred to. The accuracy evidence is comparative and narrow: in the *Nature* exercise the average Wikipedia science entry had about four inaccuracies against Britannica's three, a difference "not particularly great" (Giles, 2005). Deference is conferred from outside, by search engines and fact boxes that put its statements in front of readers until, as Ford and Wajcman (2017) observe, the facts fade into the background and are black-boxed. The label is mostly a name for granted authority.

*Legitimacy by comparison.* The early defence was comparative from the start. Fallis (2008) argued that Wikipedia's effects were likely positive because its accuracy was comparable to traditional encyclopedias and better than other free sources, and a scientist quoted in the *Nature* report remarked that print encyclopaedias are "set up as the gold standards" against which "faster or cheaper resources" are judged (Giles, 2005, p. 901). Reputation then moved with the comparison class. The Middlebury historian objected to anonymity and to popularity as validation, a worry about accountability more than about accuracy, and granted that Wikipedia compared well in the sciences and less well in history (Waters, 2007). Today the alternative is a system that produces fluent text, sometimes with no visible chain of sources, and Wikipedia plays the opposite role: unreliable beside the expert-reviewed print reference, reliable beside a model that may fabricate. Credibility is a three-place relation, a source credible for some purpose relative to the alternatives open to the reader, and what changed was less the encyclopedia than the set it is compared with. No study tests this directly, so it is a conceptual claim that the cases illustrate and do not prove.

*A community that knows, with gaps.* If the claim is provenance and the standing is relational, what is worth defending? Hardwig (1985) argued that a chain of appeals to authority "must end somewhere", with "someone who possesses the necessary evidence" (p. 337), and that where no individual has all the evidence one may have to accept "knowledge that is known by the community, not by any individual knower" (p. 349). A Wikipedia article is the output of such distributed checking: editors verify sources, contest one another and apply rules, and the page records the outcome. Menking and Rosenberg (2021), drawing on Code's epistemic responsibility and Longino's procedural objectivity, argue that the project's pillars privilege the product over the process of aggregation, and that "there needs to be a deeper connection and more transparency between the design of the back end and the design of the front end" (p. 468); authority, they note, can assert itself through accuracy and reliability "precisely because authority can always be questioned" (p. 469). The practice has a carrying capacity, which is people. Halfaker et al. (2013) report that active English editors peaked at 56,400 in March 2007 and have declined since, and they trace this to quality-control machinery, including tools that reject contributions, that treats good-faith newcomers badly. The machinery that makes the practice reliable has thinned the community that carries it.

*Auditability.* Three properties describe what the community provides. They are a working vocabulary for this essay, not terms from the literature: *provenance* (a claim points to a published source), *contestability* (any claim can be challenged through a recorded process), and *answerability* (a community with rules answers for what stands). Together they are auditability, and the claim of this section is that auditability is what makes Wikipedia worth defending.

Auditability is not a rival to truth. It is valued in the way social epistemology values practices generally, by their tendency to promote true belief (Goldman, 1999), and it serves truth-tracking in two ways: it lets errors be found and corrected, and it lets readers calibrate how far to trust a claim. Hardwig's analysis sets its limits. Most readers cannot audit most claims, and Wikipedia compiles sources and does not produce the knowledge in them, so the dependence is second-order: from claim to source, and from source to those who produced it. The audit is therefore divided. Editors check the link from claim to source; the fields that produced the source answer for its content; and the reader can verify that a source exists, sample the check, and see whether a claim has been disputed. What the reader gets is not an audit but the assurance that a check is possible and has been attempted by people who answer for it. That is a thin good, and it matters most when the alternative is fluent text with no visible chain. Magnus (2009) describes the reader's side: Wikipedia varies a great deal in quality, so the reader must judge where it is likely to be reliable, using strategies that he argues Wikipedia frustrates to a greater or lesser degree. Provenance and a record of dispute are the cues such judgement needs, and answerability presupposes someone to answer. Tollefsen (2009) asks whether that someone is the individual editors or the community itself as a source of group testimony; the essay does not settle the question, and Hardwig's community-as-knower is one way of answering it.

Systems that retrieve and cite sources now offer a link from claim to source, so provenance in the narrow sense no longer separates Wikipedia from a generated answer. Whether the cited sources support the claims they accompany is an empirical question this essay does not settle. What a citation link does not carry are the other two properties: the record of dispute and a community with rules that answers for the result (Section 5.1). Traffic and content volume are therefore poor measures of the practice's health; whether newcomers can enter and whether claims can be contested are better.

*The walls have gaps.* Graham et al. (2014) find that despite millions of hours of volunteer labour the encyclopedia's geography is uneven and clustered, with little content about much of the world. Tripodi (2023), using 38 months of deletion data on 22,174 biographies, finds women consistently over 25% of the biographies nominated for deletion while they were under 19% of all biographies, and reads the higher rate at which such nominations were kept as evidence of mistaken nomination, an inference open to challenge. Ford and Wajcman (2017) argue that the expertise required to take part is coded as male. Gallert and van der Velden (2014) describe a catch-22 for oral knowledge: the reliable-sources rule excludes oral transmission, and keepers of knowledge are judged too close to their subjects. These gaps follow from the rules that give the practice its legitimacy: if knowledge must be published and its subjects notable by one community's standards, absences in the published record become absences in the encyclopedia. Fricker's (2007) framework of testimonial and hermeneutical injustice names how some knowers are discounted and how some experiences lack shared interpretive resources, and Menking and Rosenberg (2021) considered it and set it aside as less compatible with the encyclopedic project; the gaps above can be read through it, which this essay does not attempt. To call Wikipedia a last bastion of human knowledge would overstate the case. What is defended is published, verifiable knowledge as judged by a particular community. Diversity here is not an ornament: teams of editors balanced between political leanings produce higher-quality articles, most clearly on political topics (Shi et al., 2019), and contestability is what makes the gaps visible and repairable. A closed system's gaps cannot be audited in the same way.

## 3. Wikipedia and the language models

### 3.1 The licence is silent

Contributors license their text under CC BY-SA 4.0, and the Foundation's terms say that the licences "do allow commercial uses of your contributions, as long as such uses are compliant with the terms of the respective licenses" (Wikimedia Foundation, 2023). The licence grants a "worldwide, royalty-free, non-sublicensable, non-exclusive, irrevocable" right to reproduce and share the material and adapted material. Its attribution duty applies when the material is shared, and share-alike applies "if You Share Adapted Material You produce" (Creative Commons, 2013, sections 2(a)(1), 3(a)(1), 3(b)). It says nothing about training a model and nothing about returning anything to the source.

The steward of the licences reads their reach narrowly. Creative Commons writes that "AI training is often permitted by copyright", so that "the CC license conditions have limited application to machine reuse" (Pearson, 2025), and that its guidance "was developed for a world of reuse by people, a premise that doesn't fit as neatly in a world of widespread machine use" and that "licensing alone cannot address all of the challenges to sharing that AI presents" (Creative Commons, 2026). Courts have not filled the gap. *Doe v. GitHub* concerned code under open-source licences, not Wikipedia text: the Ninth Circuit affirmed dismissal of a Digital Millennium Copyright Act claim on the theory that Copilot's outputs remove copyright management information, treated the training-stage theory as forfeited, and left two contract claims pending in the district court (*Doe v. GitHub, Inc.*, 2026). Lemley and Casey (2021) argue that training should generally be fair use, in which case licence conditions are not reached at all. Nothing found here establishes that reuse of Wikipedia text breaches its licence, and the essay does not assume that it does.

What the licence does not regulate is what a commons might ask of those who draw on it: credit in outputs, a route back for readers, a share of the cost of keeping it running, and openness about use. The Foundation asks for exactly these. Attribution "means that generative AI gives credit to the human contributions that it uses to create its outputs"; access should go through Enterprise; and reuse should sustain "a virtuous cycle that continues those human contributions that create the training data that these new technologies rely on". It does not say that current reuse breaches the licence (Wikimedia Foundation, 2025d). The gap is not a violation. It is a set of expectations that no instrument binds anyone to meet.

### 3.2 What the models take, and what that costs

Much of the evidence in this subsection comes from the Foundation, which is an interested party, and is marked as such.

*Reliance.* The Foundation says almost all large language models train on its datasets (Miller, 2025). Its share is small in volume: Wikipedia and Wikibooks together, in their English and Simple editions, are about 0.14% of the tokens in one open corpus, and Wikipedia is 4.5% of the sampling mixture in another (Soldaini et al., 2024; Touvron et al., 2023), so any reliance is on curation and quality and not on bulk. Shumailov et al. (2024) show that training recursively on model output degrades models and that human-generated data gain value as synthetic text spreads. The dependence is contested: collapse can be avoided when real data are kept and synthetic data accumulate (Gerstgrasser et al., 2024). None of this shows that developers could not substitute other sources. The reliance premise rests on a self-report, a small token share that points to quality, and a contested literature.

*Strain.* Two kinds of cost must be kept apart. The first falls on infrastructure and is the best evidenced, though it is the Foundation's own report. At least 65% of the most resource-consuming traffic to the Foundation's data centres comes from bots, which account for about 35% of pageviews, and bandwidth used for multimedia has grown by 50% since January 2024, "largely" from scrapers feeding images to AI models (Mueller et al., 2025); not all bot traffic is linked to AI developers. Wikimedia Enterprise, its paid-access service, earned $8.3 million in fiscal 2024-25, 4.0% of Foundation revenue, from 13 commercial customers (Wikimedia Foundation, 2025a), and in January 2026 named further partners without terms (Wikimedia Enterprise, 2026). The second kind falls on the practice that produces the text, and the evidence is weaker. The Foundation reports human pageviews down roughly 8% against 2024 and attributes this to search engines "providing answers directly to searchers, often based on Wikipedia content" (Miller, 2025). The academic evidence is mixed: in twelve editions through early 2024 views and visitors did not fall and rose less where ChatGPT was available, while edits and editor numbers showed no fall (Reeves et al., 2025), against larger declines for new, popular articles overlapping with ChatGPT's output (Lyu et al., 2025). Reuse therefore plausibly burdens the infrastructure and may erode the readership from which new editors come, but the second effect is not established.

*What is taken.* What models take is the text, which is the output of the practice, and any reliance is on its curation and quality. They do not take the properties that make the practice auditable: provenance travels in the text's references, but the record of dispute and the community that answers for the result stay behind (Section 5.1). That is why the practice and the text must be kept apart in what follows. The evidence of reliance concerns the text; the case for caring about the practice rests on Section 2.

Readers lose something less visible. A generated answer may give no route to question a claim, and Section 2 argued that this route is what the practice provides. If the answer replaces the visit, the people who might have joined the practice never arrive.

### 3.3 A tentative reason to contribute to upkeep

Do those who draw on the practice have any reason to help keep it going? The answer defended here is modest: a *pro tanto* reason, one that can be outweighed and that gives no party a claim against reusers. Three accounts in moral and political philosophy point toward it, and none delivers a duty on its own terms.

*Fair play.* Hart (1955, p. 185) held that when persons conduct a joint enterprise according to rules and restrict their liberty, "those who have submitted to these restrictions when required have a right to a similar submission from those who have benefited by their submission", and Rawls (1964) made it a duty of fair play. Rawls's later statement requires that the institution be just and that one "has voluntarily accepted the benefits of the arrangement or taken advantage of the opportunities it offers to further one's interests" (Rawls, 1999, p. 96). Wikipedia is a joint enterprise whose participants accept restrictions such as sourcing rules and notability standards, and developers who choose it as training material have arguably taken advantage of its opportunities. The weak points are Nozick's (1974), that benefits nobody asked for cannot bind, and Simmons's (1979) reply that obligations arise only where benefits are accepted and recognised as others' cooperative product; Klosko (1992) asks instead that the goods be presumptively beneficial, later "indispensable for satisfactory lives", and a further variant drops voluntariness (all as reported in Dagger & Lefkowitz, 2021, section 4.2). Developers have not joined the scheme, since its restrictions fall on editors, and Wikipedia's text is hardly indispensable to firms that could use other data.

*Natural duty.* Rawls's (1999, p. 99) natural duty of justice "requires us to support and comply with just institutions that exist and apply to us" (as quoted in Dagger & Lefkowitz, 2021, section 4.4), and it also asks us to further just arrangements when that can be done without too great a cost to ourselves (Rawls, 1999, section 19). It rests on no voluntary act, so Nozick's objection does not touch it. But it concerns just institutions in a full political sense, so applying it to a voluntary association is an extension by analogy, and Simmons's particularity objection asks why one institution and not another.

*Beneficence.* Singer (1972) holds that "if it is in our power to prevent something bad from happening, without thereby sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, we ought, morally, to do it", and in a weaker version that this holds for something "very bad" and without sacrificing "anything morally significant". On Kant's account beneficence is an imperfect duty, "to sometimes and to some extent" aid others (Johnson & Cureton, 2022, section 5), which leaves latitude about form. The harm here is mild, so the weaker principle does not clearly apply. A cooperative account, on which each agent owes a fair share of what is needed to meet a problem given that others do their share (Murphy, 2000; Cullity, 2004), fits the case better than Singer's individual principle.

*What they share.* The accounts differ in why a duty would bind, and their weaknesses do not add up to one. They share a premise: those who knowingly benefit from a practice that others sustain, and who can help at small cost relative to their means, have a reason not to leave its upkeep to others. Strain is not part of the premise, since neither fair play nor natural duty requires that the benefit harm the scheme; it is a further consideration, so that use which burdens the infrastructure, as scraping does (Section 3.2), strengthens the reason and use that does not weakens it. Each account lacks a different condition: joining the scheme, the justice of the institution, or the badness of the harm. The reason is therefore weak. It is proportioned to benefit and capacity, it is stronger as the practice is fairer to those who join it, and its form is left to the one who has it, within limits. The step to an epistemic commons is this essay's own; the nearest work is Hess and Ostrom's (2007) point that shared resources survive through community rules and not licences alone, and Huang and Siddarth's (2023) proposal that developers contribute quality data back to the commons they draw on.

*To whom is the reason owed?* On Hart's formulation the right belongs to those who submitted to the restrictions, which would make editors, collectively, the right-holders. The essay does not claim that. It treats the reason as concerning the continued existence of a practice from which readers, reusers and editors all benefit, and it leaves open whether any party could press it. It does not claim that editors are owed payment or credit as a debt for past gifts, since a gift to anyone under an irrevocable royalty-free licence creates no such claim for individuals, whatever Mauss (2024) says of reciprocity in general. Nor does it call the reuse exploitation: exploitation can be mutually beneficial and consensual yet unfair (Zwolinski et al., 2022), but whether developers take unfair advantage depends on a theory of fair division that the essay does not supply. Viljoen (2020) argues that the value and harms of data are supra-individual and call for collective governance, and Benkler and Nissenbaum (2006) that commons-based peer production is also a setting for character formation, so that what thins with the practice includes the virtues it fosters. Cicero (1913, I.51) quotes Ennius on lighting another's lamp from one's own, "No less shines his, when he his friend's hath lit", which concerns the giver's generosity and shows why copying text costs nothing, not what the recipient owes. Two goods are in play: Wikipedia's text is non-rival, since copying does not diminish it, and Frischmann (2012) describes such infrastructure as an input into many downstream processes; the practice that produces the text is rival, kept going by labour and by newcomers who arrive. The reason concerns the second good, which is why it is compatible with free use of the first.

*A note on betrayal.* Reaction to this reuse is often phrased as betrayal, a word the argument does not need. On the commitment account (Hawley, 2014), betrayal requires a commitment that was broken, and "we may feel betrayed, but we have not been betrayed" where nobody committed to what was expected. The commitments that exist run to platforms and not to editors, as in Stack Overflow's partnership, where OpenAI will "provide attribution to the Stack Overflow community within ChatGPT" (Stack Overflow, 2024).

*What editors think.* This has not been measured systematically, and the argument does not assume an answer. In expert interviews one participant reported that the objection heard most often from Wikipedians is that "the situation doesn't seem fundamentally fair", since editors work unpaid under an open licence while large firms profit (Vetter et al., 2025); this is a second-hand report from six experts. Editors from regional-language communities quoted by a news outlet spoke of wanting models trained on quality content and of fixing knowledge gaps, and none of those named reported payment or credit (Bhattacharya, 2026). A survey of editors on the future of the project was being prepared by volunteer researchers and had no results on its project page (Gelauff, 2026).

## 4. What reusers can do

A form of support should run to the practice, be proportionate to reliance and capacity, be open to checking, and not damage what it protects. The last two matter because the practice is carried by people while the Foundation owns the servers and controls the budget for community programmes (Menking & Rosenberg, 2021), so money for the Foundation does not by itself reach the people who carry the practice.

Credit and a route back is cheap, runs to the practice, and, if readers follow the link, helps recruitment as well as traffic; the Foundation asks that platforms "elevate opportunities to visit and participate in those sources" (Miller, 2025) and proposes a reuse model that "sends value and future contributors back to Wikipedia — not just traffic away" (Wikimedia Foundation, 2026b). It is hard to enforce in law after *Doe v. GitHub*, and no study shows whether links bring readers into the practice. Paid structured access is proportionate in principle, and the Foundation caps Enterprise income at 30% of annual revenue to protect independence and says the content "will forever be free and open to anyone" (Becker, 2026); but its terms are not public and its revenue goes to the Foundation. Preference signals let a commons say what it expects without closing: Creative Commons describes its signals as "a flexible, commons-friendly framework for communicating expectations around AI use of content or data" and does not call them binding (Creative Commons, n.d.), and without such a channel sources close, as Longpre et al. (2024) document in the growth of crawl restrictions in 2023-24. Contribution back could take the form of help with the work that keeps the practice reliable, such as policing machine-generated text (Section 5.2), though that text is introduced by users of the tools and not by reuse of Wikipedia.

No form meets all four criteria. They work better together: a route back recruits editors, paid access funds infrastructure, signals state expectations. The weak point in most is the gap between those who receive support and those who carry the practice. A pooled arrangement run by the commons' own governance, with published terms and a published account of where money and effort go, would sit best with the criteria and would answer the problem that reliance varies across thousands of reusers. Proposals for multi-party governance of language data, organised among stakeholders, values and rights, offer a template for such an arrangement, though the one cited does not discuss Wikipedia (Jernite et al., 2022). Four requests follow, as proposals of this essay and not established norms: credit and a route back, acceptance of the terms the commons states for heavy use, disclosure of use, and support that reaches participation and not only infrastructure. Given the weakness of the reason in Section 3.3, they are requests and not demands.

## 5. What Wikipedia should change

This section does not depend on Section 3.3. The practice's role depends on its own openness and answerability, whether or not reusers contribute, and each recommendation below can be pursued on that ground alone. There is a further link. A claim on others' upkeep that rests on fair play or on a common good is only as strong as the practice is fair to those who join it; Rawls's principle of fairness binds only where the institution is itself "just (or fair)" (Rawls, 1999, p. 96). The changes below would therefore also strengthen such a claim, but they do not need it. The recommendations are the essay's own, drawn from the literature, and each is limited by how much of that literature this essay has reviewed. Only the community and its stewards can decide them.

### 5.1 Claim what it can deliver

Wikipedia's policy promises provenance and not truth (Wikipedia contributors, n.d.), and a claim to be a source of truth in a strong sense would conflict with it and invite the failure the policy avoids. The defensible claim is auditability, as defined in Section 2.

The claim is hollow if the audit trail stays at the back end. Menking and Rosenberg (2021) argue for a deeper connection between the back end and the front end, and Ford and Wajcman (2017) note that once facts are shown in search engines' fact boxes they fade into the background and are black-boxed. The Foundation's paid service already carries part of the trail. Its data dictionary lists parsed references and, for each revision, the editor's name, the edit comment and three machine-generated credibility signals, a revert risk, a reference risk and a reference need; for the previous version it gives only an identifier and a character count, and it lists no field for talk-page discussion or dispute history (Wikimedia Enterprise, n.d.). What travels with the content is therefore provenance and a measure of how risky an edit looks, and not the contest itself. Revision histories and talk pages are already public on the site, so the proposal concerns what travels in structured feeds and reuse products, not disclosure. The recommendation is that Wikipedia extend what travels to the record of contest, meaning the discussion, reverts and protection history behind a claim, and ask reusers to carry it, so that a reader of a generated answer can follow a claim back to its source and to the dispute about it.

### 5.2 Protect the way in

The practice is carried by people, and its binding constraint is who can join. Halfaker et al. (2013) report that desirable newcomers kept arriving but survived less often, and that the decline in editors follows from quality-control machinery, including tools that reject contributions, which treats good-faith newcomers badly. The Foundation's figure for the English edition is about 273,000 editors, 0.02% of its monthly readers (Wikimedia Foundation, 2026b); this counts editors differently from the 2007 peak of Halfaker et al. and from the active-user counts in Appendix A, and the figures are not comparable.

Experiments since then give a mixed picture. In a controlled trial on the English edition in 2014-15, newcomers sent an invitation to the Teahouse, a place to ask questions, delivered by a bot and signed by a named host, were more than 10% more likely than the control group to be editing three to four weeks after registering and to have made five or more edits two to six months later (Morgan & Halfaker, 2018); the analysis counts invitation and not participation, since only 331 invitees took part, and its authors treat the result as conservative. A newcomer homepage combining suggested tasks, a mentor module and help links was tested in 2021 on 27 non-English editions with 244,060 new accounts: it slightly raised early activation but had no significant effect on two-week retention, on productivity or on revert rates (Warncke-Wang et al., 2023). The first intervention was a personal welcome signed by a named host, and the trial cannot say whether the invitation, the welcome or the Teahouse itself did the work; the second did not raise retention. The two differ in design, edition and period, so the contrast is suggestive only. The evidence is thin, but it does not support the hope that better tooling alone fixes retention.

Machine-generated text raises the risk of the old pattern. The community has organised against it, with a cleanup project, a narrow speedy-deletion criterion, and in March 2026 a vote of 44 to 2 to prohibit LLM-generated or rewritten article content (Bansal, 2026; Froneman, 2026). In interviews with 16 editors who had used language models, Zhou et al. (2026) report that newcomers used the tools to fill gaps in knowledge, that fitting the output to community norms took tacit knowledge they lacked, and that other editors accepted such edits from experienced editors and called out and rejected them from newcomers. The sample is small and self-selected, but it is the mechanism that Halfaker et al. describe, now applied to a new class of contribution. The recommendation is that the community measure and publish newcomer survival alongside its measures of machine-generated text, and that it test pairing rejection with a personal, named explanation and invitation, as the Teahouse invitation was, so that the cost of the defence to the practice can be seen and limited. A personal response adds volunteer labour, and that cost belongs in the assessment. The text is introduced by users of the tools and not by reuse of Wikipedia, so the argument of Section 3.3 does not make developers answerable for the cost, but it gives a reason to ask what the makers of those tools should contribute toward the people who bear it.

### 5.3 Widen what counts as knowledge without loosening how it is checked

The same rules that give the practice its legitimacy produce its gaps (Section 2). Menking and Rosenberg (2021) propose reframing the pillars around the process of inquiry and report that editors who challenged the pillars have been punished, which suggests that the community's way of revising its norms is part of the problem.

Two recommendations follow. The community can publish how notability and deletion decisions fall across categories of subject, so that the disparity Tripodi (2023) infers can be tested and not only suspected. And it can develop explicit, documented procedures through which community-held knowledge may enter, such as a route through published research by those who record it, so that widening is done by procedure and not by lowering the standard. That route should be designed with the communities that hold the knowledge, since a requirement of publication by outsiders could reinscribe the rule that creates the gap (Gallert & van der Velden, 2014). The aim is to meet the demand for auditability by means other than print, and not to relax it, because a widened practice that cannot be audited would lose the property that makes it worth defending. Smaller editions raise the stakes, since the practice there is thinner (Appendix A).

### 5.4 Open its own books

The argument asks reusers for transparency and asks the same of the steward. The Foundation controls the budget for community programmes (Menking & Rosenberg, 2021) and in January 2026 announced partners for its paid service without giving terms (Wikimedia Enterprise, 2026). The recommendation is that it publish the terms of reuse agreements, or the principles that govern them, and an account of how income from reuse is divided between infrastructure and the support of participation, including newcomers. The Foundation states that Enterprise income is capped at 30% of annual revenue and that its content will "forever be free and open to anyone" (Becker, 2026). A published account would turn these from promises into commitments that the community and outside observers can check, which is the property the essay values.

### 5.5 Who would act, at what cost, and in what order

On the English edition the community decides through its own processes, such as requests for comment and topic projects; the Foundation's product, research and enterprise teams control the tooling, the data feeds and the agreements; and affiliates and language communities carry the same questions for smaller editions. The largest cost is the personal response in Section 5.2, which spends scarce volunteer attention. A sensible order is measurement first (published newcomer survival and the distribution of deletion outcomes), then tooling and policy proposals tested against it, then the terms of agreements and what travels with reused content. Categorising deletion outcomes by demographic traits of a subject, and republishing talk-page history in reuse feeds, both touch the identities and statements of volunteers, and aggregation and the existing licences should be reviewed first. These are proposals and not a plan, and none is a prediction that it will succeed.

## 6. Objections and conclusion

*AI answers may serve readers better, so who is wronged?* The essay does not claim that readers are harmed. Its claim concerns the role of a practice and the weak reason to help sustain it. Systems that cite sources already offer a link from claim to source, and where their citations are accurate that serves readers well (Section 2). What a link does not carry is the record of dispute and an answerable community, which is what Section 5.1 asks Wikipedia to make portable.

*A gift to anyone cannot be wronged.* This defeats a claim by editors to payment or credit as a debt for past gifts, which the essay does not make; the reason in Section 3.3 concerns the practice and is not owed to donors as repayment.

*The reason proves too much.* If knowing benefit and strain ground a reason, search engines, other model builders and readers have one too, in proportion. That the reason generalises is a feature, not an embarrassment: the Foundation's own account of its traffic loss points first to search engines and chatbots (Miller, 2025), and Wikipedia and search engines have long depended on each other, with the engine's direct display of content reducing visits while Wikipedia improved its results (McMahon et al., 2017).

*A light reason is too lenient.* A reason of this kind is not the only route. Regulation, contract terms, public funding of the commons and collective control over data are others that this essay does not assess, and a stronger case might be made through them for the actors with most leverage.

*Machine-generated text is entering Wikipedia.* Detectors flagged over 5% of newly created English articles in one study, given as a lower bound (Brooks et al., 2024), and another estimates LLM-related change of about 1% in some categories (Huang et al., 2025). If contamination grows, auditability matters more, since provenance and contestability are the tools for dealing with it, and the cost to volunteers rises with it.

The question was what Wikipedia is for in the era of language models. It is an auditable practice of verification carried by a community, not a source of truth, and its standing has risen and fallen with the alternatives against which it is compared. Language models draw heavily on its text, the licence is silent about that use, and whether the use strains the practice is not established. Those who draw on it have at most a light, defeasible reason to contribute to its upkeep. What decides whether the practice keeps its role is largely within Wikipedia: whether the record of dispute behind its claims can travel with them into the systems that reuse its text, how it lets newcomers in, what it counts as knowledge and how it checks it, and how openly it deals with those who reuse it.

Three limits should be kept in view. No study yet measures systematically whether editors hold an expectation of return from AI developers (Section 3.3 gives the indirect evidence), whether readers sent to a model fare worse than readers sent to the source, or how far the sources that Wikipedia cites are being contaminated by machine-generated text. The step from fair play, natural duty and beneficence to an epistemic commons is this essay's own and is open to the objections set out in Section 3.3. And several sources are used at the level of abstracts or encyclopedia summaries, most of the philosophical ones among them, and the empirical premises about reliance and strain come largely from the Foundation.

## Appendix A. A check on a smaller edition

The main text concerns the English edition. Smaller editions raise a different version of the problem, because the practice is thinner. In a comparison of 40 editions in 2018, culturally specific content averaged 23.2% of articles, and the Vietnamese edition, at 2.5%, was the third lowest after Cebuano and Waray (0.1% each), the three editions where, the authors note, bots made a major contribution to creation and translation (Miquel-Ribé & Laniado, 2018).

That figure came from a classifier combining several features and is not reproduced here. A simpler remeasurement counts articles whose Wikidata item has a country, citizenship, origin or sport-country property pointing to the language's territory, or sits inside an administrative unit of it. It is a lower bound on cultural-context content and not the 2018 measure, and it treats articles without a Wikidata item or with sparse properties as zero, so it may penalise editions with weaker Wikidata linkage; that was not tested. Applied uniformly in October 2026 it gave 1.8% for Vietnamese (23,410 of about 1.30 million articles), against 9.7% to 19.7% for 13 other editions, with a median of 13.5%. Only Cebuano (0.4%) and Waray (0.2%) were lower, as in 2018. Five editions (Korean, Polish, Czech, Japanese and Hindi) could not be queried and are omitted. The Vietnamese figure is close to the 2018 one and far from ordinary peers.

The scale of the practice can also be measured. In October 2026 the Vietnamese edition held about 1.30 million articles and 4,882 users active in the past 30 days, 267 articles per active user against 27 on the English edition (7,247,997 articles, 268,352 active users), and 117 editors made 100 or more edits in July 2026 against 4,230 on the English edition. Almost all new pages in 2026 are created by human accounts, 2,037 of 2,089 in July. A small group maintains a large stock, much of it created earlier by bots. Data are the author's retrievals from the Wikidata query service and the Wikimedia statistics interfaces, with queries and results kept with the project files.
