## Overall Assessment

### Recommendation: **Major Revision**

### Summary Assessment
The essay is well sourced, candid about the limits of its evidence, and its best idea is a good one: Wikipedia's epistemic standing is a matter of answerable, contestable provenance, and this is exactly what is lost when its text is reused without its dispute record. But the philosophical architecture does not yet carry the weight placed on it. The three "stances" are a rhetorical trichotomy: the "eternalist" grounds (fair play, natural duty, beneficence) are all explicitly conditional, so they are not eternalist in any sense the essay can defend, and the "middle way" is therefore not distinct from them. The "conventional reason" conclusion rests on a slide from a sociological fact (mutually held expectations) to a normative reason, without the bridging work that the conventions literature treats as the central problem; and its residual content ("a reason not to leave upkeep to others") is itself a non-conventional moral premise. The practical recommendations are, by the essay's own statement, independent of the obligation analysis, which leaves the obligation half of the essay idle. The Buddhist material is mostly accurately quoted but is used in a way that changes the referent of the key terms. Social-norm theory is competently summarised but conflates norms with conventions at the exact point where the distinction matters. The result is a good sourced narrative with a promising core thesis (auditability) and a weakly argued second thesis (obligation). I recommend major revision: either build the bridge or shrink the claim.

## Strengths

### S1: A defensible and specific functional thesis (auditability) with a concrete implication
The identification of provenance, contestability and answerability as the epistemically valuable features, and the observation that citation links carry only the first, is a real analytic contribution with a falsifiable practical upshot (the dispute record).
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §2.4 and §6.1 "What a citation link does not carry are the other two properties, the record of dispute"

### S2: Epistemic honesty about evidence and about the status of the lens
The essay marks interested-party sources, abstract-level readings, and concedes that "no source applying it to institutions was found". This is rare and should be kept.
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §5.1 "No source was found that applies the idea of impermanence to social institutions or technology"

### S3: The genetic account of betrayal-feelings is a good use of norm theory
Using Bicchieri's empirical/normative expectation distinction to explain why resentment can persist after the empirical expectation fails is apt, and the use of Hawley's commitment account to separate feeling from breach is the right literature.
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §4.1 "the empirical expectation fails first, and the normative expectation may persist as a sense that it ought to hold"

## Weaknesses

### W1: The "eternalist" stance is a straw position; fair play, natural duty and beneficence are not eternalist, so the trichotomy collapses
**Problem**: Eternalism is defined as the view that the obligation "is part of the nature of the situation, whatever conditions hold". No one in the cited literature holds that. Fair play is explicitly conditional on a cooperative scheme, accepted benefits and restrictions borne; Klosko's version on presumptively beneficial goods; natural duty on the existence of just institutions that apply to the agent; Singer and Cullity/Murphy on the capacity to help and the existence of need. The essay itself finds that "each fails at the premise that would bind reusers", i.e. at a condition. A principle whose application fails because a condition is unmet is the opposite of an unconditioned obligation. What these theories share with eternalism is only that the grounding principle is not itself a social convention (it is a moral principle, not a contingent practice). That is the realist/constructivist versus conventionalist contrast, which is not the same as the temporal contrast between permanent and passing.
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §5.2 "the obligation of those who draw on a practice is part of the nature of the situation, whatever conditions hold"
**Why it matters**: The paper's headline claim is that the obligation theories "fail", and that a middle way is needed. If the targets are conditional, the "middle way" (conventional, conditional, defeasible) is not a third position; it overlaps with fair play (conditional on the practice being in place) and with cooperative beneficence. The argument is therefore a dilemma with a missing horn rather than a trichotomy.
**Suggestion**: Drop the claim that these are eternalist grounds. Reframe as "conventional versus non-conventional grounds of obligation", and test the actual candidates (fair play, natural duty, beneficence, plus Scanlonian fidelity/reliance and Gilbertian joint commitment) on their own conditions. Report plainly that they fail at conditions that are contingent, and then ask what is left. Alternatively, restrict "eternalism" to a real position (e.g. a natural-rights or Lockean property view of authorship that holds irrespective of the licence or practice) and show that that position is the one refuted.
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 5 core expertise: political obligation, fair play

### W2: The "conventional reason" conclusion makes an unbridged move from social fact to normative reason and equivocates between "norm" and "convention"
**Problem**: §2.1 says "Lewis's conventions have the same structure" as Bicchieri's social norms. Bicchieri's own account (Grammar of Society, 2006) distinguishes them: conformity to a convention is conditional on empirical expectations only; a social norm adds normative expectations. Lewisian conventions are not by themselves normative at all (Lewis argued they are not obligations; Gilbert 1989 and Marmor 2009 argue convention requires an independent source of normativity). §5.4 then says norms "bind ... by making it reasonable to expect compliance and to resent its absence". Reasonable expectation is epistemic and predictive; a reason to comply is practical. That expectations are mutually held explains why people resent non-compliance (the sociological point of §4), but not why a reuser has a reason to comply. Without that bridge, "real as convention" means only "real as social fact", and the stronger "binds" is unsupported. The essay also assumes Hawley's implicit-commitment passage supports the contrary view (no commitment), while the quoted sentence ("mutual expectation and convention give rise to commitment unless we take steps to disown these") points toward a possible implicit commitment on the Foundation's side that the essay never examines.
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §5.4 "binds in the way conventional norms do, by making it reasonable to expect compliance and to resent its absence"
**Why it matters**: This is the load-bearing step for the paper's second thesis. A reader trained in this area will see it as the question-begging step.
**Suggestion**: (a) Separate norms from conventions in §2.1. (b) State which source of normativity the conventional reason has: Humean (artificial virtue arising from interest in the scheme), Rawlsian practice-rules, Hartian internal point of view and social pressure, Gilbertian joint commitment, Scanlonian assurance/reliance (Principle F), or Raz's voluntary obligations. (c) Say honestly which of these gives reusers, who are not participants in the editor community, a reason. The likely honest answer is that only Scanlonian assurance (the Foundation's public requests induce reliance) or a transferred fair-play premise does, and both need argument. (d) Revisit Hawley: examine whether public, repeated requests plus visible dependence create an implicit commitment that reusers have not "disowned".
**Severity**: Critical (second thesis does not stand without it; the first thesis survives, so the work as a whole is repairable, hence "Major Revision" overall)
**Confidence**: 5 core expertise: normativity of convention

### W3: The residual "thin reason" is an unconditioned moral premise in disguise; the middle way is parasitic on the position it rejects
**Problem**: §5.2 extracts a shared premise: "those who knowingly benefit from a practice that others sustain, and can help at small cost, have a reason not to leave its upkeep to others". §5.4 builds the "conventional reason" on it, "in proportion to their benefit and their capacity". Proportion to benefit and capacity is exactly the content of cooperative beneficence and fair play; this is a general moral principle, not a convention. The conventional layer adds only the conditions of its application. The essay therefore does not show that a conventional reason exists independently of moral principles; it shows that a moral principle applies weakly under certain conditions. Moreover, the principle cannot be derived from the failed accounts, since the essay says they "ground different duties" and fail; it is then used as a residue. Why does the residue survive when its parent principles failed? (Reason: it is weaker. But that is not an argument.)
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §5.2 "That is a reason and not a duty, and it is a reason whose force depends on whether the practice and its expectations are in place."
**Why it matters**: It exposes the middle way as a weak non-conventional principle plus conditions, which is a mainstream position (a contextualised duty of fair participation) requiring no Buddhist machinery.
**Suggestion**: Either (i) argue directly for a weak principle of reciprocity/non-free-riding on epistemic commons and show what makes it weak (cost, benefit, non-participation), or (ii) identify what the conventional layer adds that the principle alone lacks (determinacy of form: which actions count as reciprocating; e.g. Hume and Rawls on conventions as ways of making general principles specific). Option (ii) is the stronger contribution: conventions determine the form of a duty whose existence is independent. That also fits the "release the form" slogan.
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 4 core expertise: normative theory

### W4: The practical recommendations are, by the essay's own statement, independent of the obligation analysis
**Problem**: §6 says the recommendations "do not depend on whether reusers contribute", and the strongest recommendation (carry the dispute record) is derived from the function (auditability), not from §5. §7 says the guidance comes from "identify the function, ask which conditions supported it, ask what has changed, and judge reforms by whether they preserve the function." That is a functional-reform procedure which can be stated without the three stances. The three-stance structure and the conclusion about "what reusers owe" are thus orphaned from the practical outcome.
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §6 "They do not depend on whether reusers contribute."
**Why it matters**: The title, abstract and structure advertise an argument from impermanence and conventional obligation to a recommendation; the argument actually runs from function alone.
**Suggestion**: Either demote §5 to a bounded section ("what reusers owe, if anything") and lead the essay with auditability, or make the recommendations conditional on §5's result (e.g. a reasoned case why a reuser who benefits has a weak reason to carry the dispute record, and why this is the form the reason should take). The latter is philosophically better, since "carry the record" is exactly a determinate form of a weak reciprocity duty.
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 4 core expertise: argument structure

### W5: "Auditable testimony" is stipulated, not argued, and "audit" is the wrong notion for the structure described
**Problem**: Provenance, contestability and answerability are said to be "a working vocabulary for this essay and not terms from the literature". Nothing is argued about their jointly sufficient role. The essay then concedes (a) that most readers cannot audit and receive only "assurance that a check is possible", following Hardwig, whose point was that lay trust is rational despite inability to audit; (b) that the editors who check are the same community that answers, i.e. auditor and auditee coincide. Audit in its ordinary sense requires an independent auditor; what is described is accountability and answerability (O'Neill's "intelligent accountability", Fricker/Anderson on the questions a layperson can ask of a testifier, Sperber et al. on epistemic vigilance). Also, the claim that auditability "serves truth-tracking" is attributed to Goldman's veritism but not shown; whether a dispute record in fact improves reader calibration is an empirical question the essay does not address and the evidence base for it is absent. Finally, the essay's own concession that cited sources in generated answers match provenance, so that the difference rests on dispute record and an answerable community, concedes that Wikipedia's distinctive epistemic function is the part least likely to be carried or even used by readers.
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §2.4 "They are a working vocabulary for this essay and not terms from the literature"
**Why it matters**: The first thesis is the stronger one, and needs a defence against the obvious reply that this is an ordinary accountability notion described with a new word.
**Suggestion**: Rename or justify "auditable". Locate the three properties in the testimony literature (Coady 1992; Lackey 2008; Goldberg 2010 on reliance and monitoring; Faulkner 2011 on trust; Zagzebski 2012 on epistemic authority; O'Neill 2002), and state what reader behaviour the dispute record is supposed to enable (calibration, challenge, repair). Mark the truth-conduciveness claim as an empirical premise and cite evidence on whether exposure to edit/talk history changes reader judgement (e.g. work on credibility cues in Wikipedia; [UNVERIFIED] search lead: Kittur/Chi group on visualising revision history; Flanagin & Metzger).
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 4 core expertise: social epistemology of testimony

### W6: The Buddhist lens changes the referent of its terms; the extremes are applied by analogy and the analogy does less than §7 claims
**Problem (accuracy first)**. Quotations check out against the SEP: Hayes defines eternalism as "the doctrine that all things exist because of an eternal essence" and annihilationism as "the doctrine that things have essences while they exist but that these essences are annihilated just when the things themselves go out of existence" (confirmed); Westerhoff's sentences are as quoted, but see (c) below. The dependent-arising formula from Vélez de Cea is standard. The problems are in application:
(a) In the sources the essay cites, both extremes are about svabhāva (inherent nature). Eternalism is reification of essence; annihilationism is the claim that an essence exists and then ceases. They are not about duration or timelessness as such. The essay's "timeless obligations" versus "nothing is owed" is a different dichotomy (realism versus nihilism about obligation). It gets the Madhyamaka verdict "reifying" only in the sense that someone treats a moral ground as intrinsic, which, per W1, none of the named theories does.
(b) The more natural early-Buddhist mapping of annihilationism is the denial of karmic consequence (ethical nihilism, "no fruit of action"), and of eternalism the assertion of a persisting self. The essay's use of annihilationism for "nothing is owed because convention" is therefore not wrong in spirit but is under-justified, and the quoted SEP definition does not support it. The Madhyamaka locus classicus for the charge that emptiness destroys conventional practice and Nāgārjuna's reply (MMK ch. 24, including 24.18, emptiness = dependent designation = the middle way) is never cited; it is the passage that would actually justify the move from "conventional" to "still binding".
(c) The Westerhoff passage is partly misused. In context, the sentence that "practices need improvement from time to time" is attached to the observation that treating truth as more than conventional is advantageous for inquiry; the essay uses it as a licence to reform norms. The essay hedges with "one reading", but the particular sentence is not about normative reform. Also the fetched SEP text does not discuss MMK 24 at all, so the "conventional reality survives emptiness" point is not in the cited source.
(d) The dependent-arising use is selective: only diachronic causal dependence (this arises when that exists) is used. In Madhyamaka, dependence includes dependence on parts and on conceptual designation (prajñapti), which is the sense in which a convention exists. The latter is the version that would have connected the Lewis/Bicchieri account to the metaphysics: a norm exists only via mutual designation and expectation. The essay uses the weaker sense.
(e) Impermanence and "ordinary impermanence versus momentariness" is correct as a disclaimer (Siderits), but then the actual argument is about conditions changing. That is simply contingency of social institutions; no Buddhist claim is needed.
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §5.1 "In the original context the extremes concern persons and selves. Applying them to norms is an analogy"
**Why it matters**: §7 states "The test is whether each borrowed concept does analytic work" and then says that if the distinctions "could be drawn without the vocabulary, the vocabulary would be dispensable". They can: Hume's circumstances of justice, Rawls's practice conception, constructivist metaethics, and institutional-change theory already supply conditioned obligations, and realism/error-theory versus conventionalism is the standard trichotomy. As written, the lens is largely decorative, and the title promises more.
**Suggestion**: Make the analogy honest and narrow. (1) State that the extremes are being re-purposed from essence-claims to claims about the ground of obligation, and say what stays and what changes. (2) Cite MMK 24 via a source that discusses it (e.g. Garfield's translation/commentary; Siderits' Buddhism as Philosophy; Westerhoff's Nāgārjuna's Madhyamaka) and use two truths plus dependent designation to explain why conventional norms are not "nothing". (3) Correct the Westerhoff use. (4) If the lens cannot show a distinction the secular literature does not, retitle and reposition: "conditioned" can stay as a plain term, with Buddhism as a brief heuristic, or add what is distinctive (for instance the soteriological dimension of non-attachment to form, "release the form", which the essay never develops and which may be the lens's real contribution).
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 3 adjacent field: Buddhist philosophy, checked against SEP text and standard secondary literature

### W7: The "function" criterion for reforming norms is under-theorised
**Problem**: §5.4: "A convention is better or worse according to whether it serves the function that gave it point." The function is identified by the essay (auditable testimony), not by Bicchieri/Lewis/Young, for whom conventions solve coordination problems and need not have a unique function. Why is auditable testimony the function, rather than free knowledge production, a volunteer social good, or reader access? The norm of "contribution" has the function of sustaining a commons; "reciprocity in use" has the function of sustaining supply. Choosing the function determines the verdict about what to hold and what to release; this selective choice is the main normative decision of the essay and is not argued. A further problem is aetiological versus current function: the norms arose for other reasons, and "gave it point" is a historical claim not supported by the cited histories (Forte & Bruckman; Butler et al.; Halfaker et al.).
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §5.4 "A convention is better or worse according to whether it serves the function that gave it point."
**Suggestion**: Argue for the function (from the Wikipedia policies' own statements of purpose, the Foundation's mission, or a general account of epistemic commons) and say whether the criterion is current function, selected function or normative function (ought-function). Discuss alternative candidate functions and show where they would give different verdicts, for example on newcomer protection (§6.2) or widening (§6.3).
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 4 core expertise: functional explanation and normative criticism of conventions

### W8: Norm-theory applications are partly inaccurate or oversold
**Problem**:
(a) Centola et al. (2018) give a tipping fraction of about 25% in a naming-game coordination setting. The essay's inference ("a few visible reusers adopting a norm of credit ... that others then follow") does not follow: a quarter of a population is not "a few". If the relevant population is a handful of frontier developers, the dynamics are bargaining or collective action among few players (Ostrom-type or contract-type), not norm cascade. The essay half-acknowledges the coordination/norm gap but still uses the result as support.
(b) Bicchieri and Mercier's trendsetter point is real but concerns changes in beliefs about what others do; Bicchieri's later work (Norms in the Wild, 2017) on norm-change interventions, pluralistic ignorance and schemas/scripts is the proper source and is not cited. Pluralistic ignorance is directly relevant to the editors' stance (§4.2 observes that some editors still hold the expectation and others do not: a textbook setting for it).
(c) §2.1 says norms "can lapse without anyone changing their values". Correct for Bicchieri's account, but the essay then slides to "lapsing" as a unitary phenomenon, mixing (i) loss of empirical expectation in reusers, (ii) loss of normative expectation in editors, and (iii) shift in the alternative sources. These require different diagnoses; norm erosion in the reuser population is not the same as the norm never having bound them. In fact the third strand (reciprocity in use) was never a norm that reusers held; it was an expectation of the contributors. A Bicchieri norm requires that the reference network (here the reusers) hold both expectations. The reuser population never did. The essay's phrase "lapsing" therefore presupposes a prior norm that its own evidence does not show.
(d) The sentence "norm change is typically not smooth" from Young concerns stochastic evolution of conventions in interacting populations; the essay does not use it, and no data on Wikipedia's timeline is presented as showing a burst. Currently it is ornamental.
(e) The share-alike clause of CC BY-SA is itself a reciprocity rule encoded in the licence (copyleft). §2.2 says "The licence supplies an attribution duty ... the rest was expectation". The third strand's reciprocity is partly encoded in ShareAlike (Wikipedia moved from GFDL to CC BY-SA in 2009 for this reason). Whether model weights or outputs are "adapted material" is a legal question, but the philosophical point (reciprocity was partly codified, and the codification is what is now contested) is lost.
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §4.3 "a few visible reusers adopting a norm of credit, route back and carried provenance that others then follow"
**Why it matters**: The empirical-normative core of the lapse story (§§2-4) depends on a norm existing in the right reference group.
**Suggestion**: Specify the reference network for each strand; show that the reuser population did hold the relevant expectations (or acknowledge it did not and that "lapsing" applies only to the editors' expectation); correct the Centola reading; add Bicchieri (2017) and pluralistic-ignorance work; add ShareAlike to §2.2.
**Severity**: Major
**Confidence**: 4 core expertise: social norms

### W9: Strawson and the reactive attitudes are misdescribed, and the genetic/justificatory distinction is blurred
**Problem**: §4.1 writes "Resentment is the emotional form of an unmet normative expectation." In Strawson, resentment responds to the quality of will (ill will or indifference) shown in an action; it presupposes a demand, but it is not the generic form of any unmet expectation, and holding someone to the demand presupposes that the demand is warranted. Wallace (1994) is the better source for the expectation-based reading. The essay needs the distinction between explaining why contributors feel resentment (genetic) and whether the feeling is warranted (justificatory), and it moves between the two: "the feeling registers lapsing expectations". Also the targets of the attitude (large firms; model developers; corporate agents) raise issues about the apt object of reactive attitudes that are not discussed.
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §4.1 "Resentment is the emotional form of an unmet normative expectation."
**Suggestion**: Use Wallace and Watson; separate "is the resentment intelligible" from "is it justified". Flag that on the essay's own account intelligibility is established and justification is not.
**Severity**: Minor
**Confidence**: 4 core expertise: moral psychology of responsibility

### W10: Craig and Hardwig are used beyond what they support
**Problem**: Craig's genealogy of the concept of knowledge is used to claim that "what counts as a good informant is a social standard ... it can shift when the available informants do". Craig does allow variation in the standards for good informants but argues for an objectivisation process that fixes the concept; the essay's relativist reading is much stronger than the source warrants. Hardwig (1985) supports rational dependence, but he does not argue that dependence is justified by auditability; his later "The Role of Trust in Knowledge" (1991) argues that trust in expert character is unavoidable and cannot be reduced to checking. **Evidence Anchor**: text: §2.2 "What counts as a good informant is then a social standard answering to a social need, and it can shift"
**Suggestion**: State the limits of each reading, or cite alternative sources (e.g. Fricker 2007 ch. on Craig's state-of-nature story, which uses it for a normative critique of informant standards).

## Detailed Comments

### Is the central thesis a philosophical argument or a narrative with philosophical vocabulary?
It is two theses. The first (authority as a conditioned complex; function as answerable/contestable provenance) is a genuine, if modest, philosophical thesis and is supported by good literature; it needs the retitling and justification in W5. The second (a three-stance argument ending in a conventional reason) is, in its present form, closer to a narrative with philosophical vocabulary: its stances are not exclusive (W1), its conclusion rests on a bridge it does not build (W2), and its practical output is independent of it (W4). The essay is most convincing where it is a careful sociological and institutional analysis (§§3, 4.2, 6) and least where it claims that the lens yields a verdict on what reusers owe.

### Social-norm theory
Bicchieri, Young and Centola are correctly cited at the level of the SEP summaries, but the essay relies on encyclopedia entries rather than the primary texts and so misses the norm/convention distinction (§2.1), the reference-network requirement and the question of whether the reusers ever belonged to the norm (W8c). Sunstein's norm entrepreneurs and cascades are fine, but the mechanism requires a population large enough for reputational incentives to cascade, which is not the situation of a few developers. Lewis is correctly cited for the definition but not for the point that conventions are not obligations. Not sufficient for the obligation conclusion; adequate for the descriptive story with the corrections above.

### Fair play, natural duty, beneficence
The summaries of Hart, Rawls, Nozick, Simmons and Klosko via Dagger and Lefkowitz are accurate; the application to developers is reasonable (they have not joined the scheme; the benefit is not indispensable). The problem is the label and the use of the results (W1, W3). Missing: Klosko's own extension to non-state schemes; Arneson and Wellman on fair play; the "free-rider on a public good" form of the argument, which is closer to what is at issue than the political-obligation framing; and Wikipedia's licence-based openness as a feature of the scheme that arguably removes the "restrictions borne" premise (editors accept no restriction vis-à-vis reusers; they gave irrevocably). The essay is right that the premises fail, but it should say that for fair play the failure is structural: a scheme in which benefits are open and unrestricted by design cannot ground a fairness claim against those who take them, unless the scheme's terms include reciprocity (ShareAlike). That point strengthens the essay and ties back to W8e.

### The conventionalist conclusion
A conventionalist account can carry a descriptive claim (what people expect and resent) and, with the added assumptions in W2-W3, a weak and conditional reason. It cannot by itself carry "what reusers owe". If the essay wants to retain the conclusion in the abstract ("obligations of reuse are conventional, real while their conditions hold, defeasible and open to deliberate reform"), the shortest repair is the Humean/Rawlsian one: a general moral principle (non-free-riding on cooperative commons) whose form is fixed by conventions. This also gives "release the form" a clear sense: the form is conventional, the principle is not.

### Buddhist accuracy: summary
Accurate: the Hayes definitions; the IEP dependent-arising formula; the ordinary versus momentary impermanence disclaimer (Siderits); the claim that applying the extremes to norms is the author's own. Imprecise or misleading: (i) eternalism/annihilationism are about svabhāva/essence, not timelessness or non-ownership; (ii) the early-Buddhist ethical sense of annihilationism (denying karmic fruit) is available and is a better fit than the essay's; (iii) the Westerhoff "improvement" sentence is about inquiry; (iv) no MMK 24 or dependent designation. Legitimacy of the extension: as heuristic analogy yes; as an argument (§7 "does analytic work") no, until it shows a distinction the secular literature cannot (W6).
