## Manuscript Information
- **Title**: Conditioned authority: impermanence and the fate of Wikipedia's norms in the era of language models
- **File reviewed**: essay_full_draft_v5.md (read in full, read-only; no other project file consulted)
- **Review round**: 3 (simulated, blind)
- **Venue configuration**: Associate editor, *Philosophy & Technology* (Journal-Fit); methodology reviewer for a theoretical/conceptual paper with empirical premises

## Overall Assessment

**Recommendation (both seats): Major Revision.**
**Confidence**: Journal fit 4/5 (core fit judgement is mine; length limits quoted from memory and should be checked against current author guidelines). Methodology 4/5 (conceptual argument analysis is in my competence; Buddhist-studies points are adjacent, 3/5).

### Summary Assessment

The essay argues that Wikipedia's authority is a conditioned complex of credibility, contribution and reciprocity norms whose function is to make testimony "auditable" (provenance, contestability, answerability). It argues that several enabling conditions have changed, so the norms lapse, are rewritten, or are codified for the first time. It argues that contributors' "betrayal" registers lapsing expectations and does not show a breach. Using impermanence as a lens, it sorts positions on the obligations of reuse into eternalism (fair play, natural duty, beneficence), annihilationism (nothing is owed), and a middle way (conventional, defeasible, reformable reasons). It ends with four practical recommendations, the most specific being that the dispute record should travel with Wikipedia text into reuse systems.

The essay is carefully hedged, unusually candid about its limits, and well sourced. The citation hygiene is better than average, and most of the quotations I could check are accurate. Its weaknesses are philosophical rather than editorial. The new philosophical content is thin relative to the apparatus: the Buddhist lens is conceded to be an analogy that carries no premise, the "middle way" is close to a standard conventionalist view of norms, and the recommendations in Section 6 are declared independent of the obligation argument. The empirical premises about lapse are acknowledged to be unmeasured, yet the abstract and conclusion assert lapse. The thesis is restated close to verbatim at least five times, and Appendix A is disconnected from the argument. With a tighter claim, a defended bridge from conventional truth to conventional obligation, and cuts, this could be a competent P&T paper. It is not yet one.

## PART 1. Journal-Fit Review

### 1.1 Fit
- **Scope**: Good. The paper sits at social epistemology, philosophy of information and AI/data governance, and the ethics of the digital commons, all in P&T's remit. The Wikipedia/LLM case is timely.
- **Readership**: P&T readers will accept the SEP-mediated use of sources less readily than a specialist journal would. Several load-bearing philosophical positions (Madhyamaka, Rawls, Hart, Simmons, Klosko, Strawson) are reached through encyclopedia entries, and the essay says so itself (Section 7, "Three limits"). This is acceptable for orientation but weak for a claim to philosophical contribution.
- **Alternative venues if rejected**: *Ethics and Information Technology*, *Social Epistemology*, *Philosophy East and West* (if the Buddhist argument is deepened), *Big Data & Society*.

### 1.2 Originality: philosophical contribution or reframing?
My judgement is **mostly a reframing, with one defensible original proposal**.

- **Reframing**: The core account (norms as expectation-dependent, so they can lapse without change of values; resentment without breach; the reuse obligation as a thin, defeasible reason) is a recombination of Bicchieri (conditional preferences), Lewis (convention), Hawley (betrayal needs commitment), Holton (reactive attitudes) and the Hart/Rawls fairness literature. The Buddhist vocabulary is explicitly "interpretive" and "the essay does not rest any premise on Buddhist doctrine being true" (Section 5.1; Section 7). Under the essay's own test, if a distinction "could be drawn without the vocabulary, the vocabulary would be dispensable" (Section 7). I think it can be: Sections 2.1, 4.1 and 5.4 run on Bicchieri and Lewis alone. The essay does not show otherwise.
- **Defensible original element**: the functional analysis of Wikipedia's authority as "auditable testimony" (Section 2.4) and the recommendation that the dispute record should travel with reused text (Section 6.1, supported by a reading of the Enterprise data dictionary). This is concrete and arguable. But Section 6 states the recommendations "do not depend on whether reusers contribute", so they also do not depend on the norm-lapse thesis or on the impermanence lens. The practical payoff is therefore detached from the philosophical frame.
- **Gap**: Nothing engages the philosophical literature on commons governance and data stewardship beyond Hess & Ostrom (2007), Ostrom (2010) and Huang & Siddarth (2023), or on the ethics of exploitation of free labour or digital labour beyond a single SEP entry. Nothing engages the standard conventionalist treatment of obligation (Hume, Gilbert's joint commitment, Brennan et al. on normative expectations), which is the nearest competitor to the "middle way". Nothing engages the testimony-and-trust literature for group sources beyond Tollefsen (2009), which is expressly left open.

### 1.3 Significance
Moderate. The case matters, and the point that credibility is a three-place relation relative to alternatives (Section 2.2) is useful. The wider claim, that obligations of reuse are thin conventional reasons, is modest by design, which is honest, but it limits significance. The paper needs to say what follows for an actor who would otherwise have believed the obligation to be stronger or absent, and why the middle way yields different practical conclusions from the annihilationist.

## PART 4. Methodology Review: Argument Structure

Paradigm: theoretical/conceptual analysis with empirical premises. Focus: validity of inferences, premise strength, counterexamples, use of sources, falsifiability, repetition.

### Strengths
- **S1. Disciplined hedging and source-marking.** Evidence Anchor: text: Section 3.2 "Much of the evidence in this subsection comes from the Foundation, which is an interested party, and is marked as such." The essay flags provenance and weakness of evidence throughout and avoids claiming unlawfulness or large harm (Section 1).
- **S2. A useful, plain functional definition.** Evidence Anchor: text: Section 2.4 "provenance... contestability... answerability... Together they are auditability." The three properties are disclaimed as the essay's own vocabulary, not the literature's, which is honest and gives the recommendations a clear target.
- **S3. Anticipates objections.** Evidence Anchor: text: Section 7 "The lens is decoration" and "A light reason is too lenient." The objection section is serious, though some replies concede more than they answer (see W1, W3).
- **S4. Careful treatment of the AI-citation point.** Evidence Anchor: text: Section 2.4 "Whether the cited sources support the claims they accompany is an empirical question this essay does not settle."

### Weaknesses

**W3. The bridge from "conventional truth" to "conventional obligation" is unsupported.**
Severity: Major | Evidence Anchor: text: Section 5.4 "Madhyamaka thought supplies the structure" and "a conventional and defeasible reason to take part in re-instituting norms" | Confidence: 4 (conceptual analysis, core; Madhyamaka, adjacent).
The Westerhoff passage is about truth: there is no ultimate truth, there is conventional truth, and truth-practices improve. The step to "norms are real as conventions and bind within their conditions" is a separate claim about normativity. Westerhoff himself presents the conventionalist reading as one among several, as the essay notes; Garfield's own problem (in what sense conventional truth is truth and not falsehood) is cited and not answered. The "improvement" sentence concerns scientific practice. The defensible content (norms are expectation-dependent) comes from Bicchieri, not from Nāgārjuna. Suggestion: state the inference explicitly (premises, conclusion), argue it from the social-norms literature, and treat Madhyamaka as an illustration unless a real argument is added.

**W4. The three-stance taxonomy is partly a constructed contrast.**
Severity: Major | Evidence Anchor: text: Section 5.1 "In the original context the extremes concern persons and selves. Applying them to norms is an analogy" | Confidence: 4.
"Eternalism" is defined as the search for timeless grounds of obligation, and "annihilationism" as "nothing is owed". Neither matches the cited SEP definitions (eternal essence; essences annihilated at destruction). The first is stipulated and the second is a position no author holds in the text; the Open Source remark ("refusal of restrictions on fields of endeavour expresses the same culture", Section 5.3) is an assertion without a citation. Fair play and beneficence are not "timeless" in the sense used: Hart-Rawls fairness is explicitly conditional on a scheme existing and benefits accepted, which is a condition-dependent, "conventional-like" account. So the first contrast is partly a straw man. Suggestion: name the actual opponents (for example a Lockean or desert-based claim on the commons versus a pure licence-positivism) and quote them.

**W5. Premise stronger than evidence: "the norms are lapsing".**
Severity: Major | Evidence Anchor: text: Abstract "its norms are lapsing in some places" versus Section 4.2 "What editors themselves think of this reuse has not been measured systematically" and Section 2.5 "These conditions are the essay's reconstruction and not an established causal history. Each has now changed." | Confidence: 5.
Three levels of overreach:
(a) Of the four named conditions, two (cost of reuse high enough that copiers also credited and visited; a licence culture with shared expectation of acknowledgement) have no cited evidence that they ever held. "Each has now changed" is asserted for all four, while Section 3.2 shows the reader-to-editor path evidence is mixed (Reeves et al. versus Lyu et al.), and Section 3.3 shows Wikipedia keeping its scale and finances.
(b) The resentment-without-breach argument (Section 4.1) needs the premise that contributors once held a mutual expectation of "acknowledgement and a visit in return". Section 2.2 cites Algan, Hergueux and Dang Nguyen for norms of return and recognition, but those are read "at abstract level" and, as the essay says, show motives, not a mutual expectation about reusers. The licence (irrevocable, commercial use explicitly allowed) points the other way.
(c) The explanatory work in Section 4.2 is unfalsifiable as written: "A norm that is lapsing should show up exactly so: some members still hold the expectation and feel its breach, and others have already stopped holding it." The same pattern would appear if the expectation never existed, if it is stable but contested, or if it is growing. No observation would count against the lapse hypothesis. State what pattern would disconfirm it (for example, stable or rising endorsement of expectation of credit among editors).
Suggestion: soften the abstract and conclusion to "may be lapsing", define observable markers, or present the lapse as a conditional ("if the expectations were mutually held, then...").

**W6. Appendix A and Section 3.3 are weakly connected to the argument and methodologically thin.**
Severity: Major (Appendix A) / Minor (Section 3.3) | Evidence Anchor: text: Appendix A "It is a lower bound on cultural-context content and not the 2018 measure" and "Five editions (Korean, Polish, Czech, Japanese and Hindi) could not be queried." | Confidence: 4.
The Appendix compares a Wikidata-property proxy (2026) with a different published measure (2018), treats missing Wikidata items as zero, admits it "may penalise editions with weaker Wikidata linkage; that was not tested", and cannot query five editions. The headline contrast (1.8% against 9.7% to 19.7%) therefore reflects measurement artefacts as plausibly as practice. The text uses the result nowhere in Sections 2 to 7 and offers no inferential link to "thinner practice"; the sentence "A small group maintains a large stock" is descriptive. Data and queries are "kept with the project files", so a reader cannot reproduce them. Suggestion: drop the Appendix, or put it in a data supplement with queries and a validity check (compare the proxy to the 2018 measure on editions where both exist).
Section 3.3's Stack Overflow figures are the author's retrieval with a one-year gap and a comparison with Reddit (growth in revenue and users) which does not bear on norm change; these paragraphs argue that "a loss of norms need not be a loss of the institution" but give no direct evidence about norms at either site.

**W7. The "weak reason" survives only by reintroducing a non-conventional premise.**
Severity: Major | Evidence Anchor: text: Section 5.2 "What they share is a premise... have a reason not to leave its upkeep to others. That is a reason and not a duty... This is already the move to the third stance." | Confidence: 4.
The thin residual reason (those who knowingly benefit and can help at small cost have a reason not to free-ride) is exactly the shared core of the fairness and cooperative-beneficence accounts. It is not conditional on conventions holding, yet Section 5.4 says that when expectations lapse "the standing of the reason" lapses and "the reason that remains is the thin one of Section 5.2". So the middle way is a mix: a conventional layer, plus the non-conventional minimum that the eternalist accounts were said to fail to supply. Either the thin reason is conventional (then why does it survive lapse?) or it is not (then the eternalist critique is overstated, since a non-conventional reason exists). Resolve which. Related: the claim that the stronger grounds "failed at a premise that would bind reusers" depends on contestable moves (for instance "Developers have not joined the scheme", though they take its benefits, which is the Simmons acceptance condition; and "Wikipedia's text is hardly indispensable", asserted without support in a section that elsewhere stresses its quality-value to model builders).

**W8. Section 6 recommendations do not follow from the thesis and are under-supported.**
Severity: Major | Evidence Anchor: text: Section 6 "They do not depend on whether reusers contribute." and Section 6.1 "This is the specific practical claim that follows from locating the function in auditability" | Confidence: 4.
They follow, if at all, from the auditability analysis in Section 2.4, a claim whose definition is stipulated and for which the essay offers no test. Sections 6.2 to 6.4 (newcomer retention, geographic and gender gaps, financial transparency) are standard governance proposals tied to the norms thesis only by the phrase "hold the function". The one specific proposal, carrying the dispute record, is not assessed for feasibility, cost, or what a downstream user would do with it; whether talk pages and revert histories improve a reader's calibration (Section 2.4's premise) is not argued. Suggestion: make 6.1 the central contribution, give a short worked example, and prune the rest or shorten it.

**W9. Repeated material.**
Severity: Minor | Evidence Anchor: text: Abstract, Section 1 roadmap paragraph, Section 5.4 "The view has three parts" and the Section 7 conclusion state "conventional, real while their conditions hold, defeasible, and open to deliberate reform" in almost the same words | Confidence: 5.
The formula appears at least five times. The limits of the lens are stated in the abstract, Section 1, Section 5.1, Section 7 (first objection) and the final limits paragraph. The point that a citation link does not carry the dispute record appears in Sections 2.4, 6.1 and 7. Pruning these would free 600 to 800 words.

**W10. Overreliance on abstract-level reading of empirical literature as premises.**
Severity: Minor | Evidence Anchor: text: Section 2.2 "All three are abstract-level readings" and Section 3.4 "both are preprints read at abstract level" | Confidence: 4.
The essay is open about it, but premises resting on abstracts (Algan et al., Hergueux et al., Dang Nguyen et al., Hora & Robbes, Afroz et al.) carry the claim that contribution rested on "norms of return and recognition and not on pure gift". A philosophy venue will accept this less than a social-science one. Read the studies or weaken the claim.

**W11. Selective use of Foundation sources.**
Severity: Minor | Evidence Anchor: text: Section 1 "a fall the Foundation attributes in part to search engines and chatbots that answer directly" | Confidence: 3.
The cited Diff post also reports revised bot detection and generational shifts in how people consume information. Giving only the AI-related cause tilts the premise of the narrative. State the other causes.

### Methodological fallacies checked
- Equivocation: "norm" is used for social norms (Bicchieri), conventions (Lewis) and policy rules (Wikipedia contributors, Butler et al.) in Sections 2.1 to 2.3 and 4.3 without distinguishing them. Policies in Wikipedia are formal rules, not Bicchieri-type social norms.
- Genetic argument: the history of Middlebury's ban is used to infer that credibility is relative, which is a fair point, but the same history also shows a rule driven by one historian's experience, not a lapsing norm.
- Possible circularity: auditability is defined by what Wikipedia provides, then used as the test of what is worth keeping.
- Counterexamples: the essay handles AI-answers-may-serve-readers (Section 7). It does not consider that if a norm of credit is rebuilt through "trendsetters" (Section 4.3), this undercuts the "lapse" language, since the process described is norm formation on a new object, not lapse of an old one.

### Questions for Authors
1. What observation about editors' attitudes would count as evidence that the credit-and-return expectation never existed, or has not lapsed?
2. Can the argument of Sections 4 and 5.4 be stated with Bicchieri and Lewis alone? If so, what does the impermanence lens add that survives your own dispensability test?
3. Is the thin reason of Section 5.2 conventional or not? If conventional, why does it survive the lapse of expectations?
4. Why is the dispute record, rather than provenance with an accuracy audit, the right target for travel, and what evidence is there that readers use or would use it?
5. What is Appendix A for in the argument?

### Minor Issues

- Section 5.3: the Open Source Definition remark needs a citation.
- Section 4.3: the sentence "A loss of norms need not be a loss of the institution, and the reverse" (Section 3.3) is cryptic; rewrite.
- Section 6.4: "forever be free and open to anyone" is quoted from a Foundation blog post that is also the source of the 30% cap; mark as self-report.
- Title: "impermanence" appears in the title but the argument turns on conventionality and conditionality; consider retitling.

## Recommendation Signal

**Major Revision** (both seats). Justification: the topic fits and the manuscript is honest, well-sourced and accurate in the quotations I could check. But the philosophical argument does not yet carry what the abstract promises: the Buddhist frame is admittedly dispensable, the key bridging inference (W3) and the residual-reason problem (W7) are unresolved, and the lapse premise (W5) rests on unmeasured expectations, with an unfalsifiable reading in Section 4.2. All of this is repairable without new data: restate the thesis as conditional, argue the conventionalism from the social-norms literature, resolve the status of the thin reason, cut repetition and Appendix A, and centre the paper on the auditability analysis and the dispute-record proposal. After such revision, re-review is needed. I would not reject: no finding is fatal, and the core idea (credibility and reciprocity as expectation-dependent, with a concrete proposal for what should travel) is worth publishing in a leaner form.
