# Lit 1: Social epistemology of encyclopedias and Wikipedia

## A. Epistemic dependence and testimony

### 1. Hardwig (1985)
**Citation:** Hardwig, J. (1985). Epistemic dependence. *The Journal of Philosophy, 82*(7), 335-349. https://doi.org/10.2307/2026523
**Type:** peer-reviewed article
**Verification:** Crossref record confirms author, year, title, journal, volume, issue (the record lists first page 335; end page 349 from my memory of the standard citation, not confirmed on a page I opened).
**Annotation:** I saw only the bibliographic record, not the text. The title and venue establish it as the standard source on rational reliance on others' expertise; I make no claim here about its detailed argument beyond that.
**Bearing:** Frames the baseline for T3: relying on an unseen source is not irrational per se, so the question is what grounds trust in anonymous collaborative output. Check the argument against the text before quoting.
**Keywords:** epistemic dependence, expertise, testimony, trust
- **Full-text update (3 Oct 2026, scanned copy supplied by the author, read from page images):** pages 335-349 confirmed. Hardwig argues (1) that one can have good reasons to believe p if one has good reasons to believe that others have good reasons for p, a reason that is not evidence for p itself; (2) that because the layman is the epistemic inferior of the expert, rationality sometimes consists in refusing to think for oneself; (3) that the expert-layman relationship is essential to scholarly knowledge. The chain of appeals to authority "must end somewhere" with someone who has the evidence (p. 337). Footnotes in research are appeals to authority, and a case of knowledge distributed over A, B, C, D and E leads him to say that p may be known by the community and not by any individual ("We know that p"), with Peirce's community of inquirers as primary knower (p. 349). The earlier record-only annotation is superseded.

### 2. Goldman (2001)
**Citation:** Goldman, A. I. (2001). Experts: Which ones should you trust? *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research, 63*(1), 85-110. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1933-1592.2001.tb00093.x
**Type:** peer-reviewed article
**Verification:** Crossref record (title, author, year, volume, issue, pages) plus a Wiley search listing with the same DOI. Full text not read.

### 3. Coady (1992)
**Citation:** Coady, C. A. J. (1992). *Testimony: A philosophical study*. Clarendon Press. https://doi.org/10.1093/0198235518.001.0001
**Type:** book
**Verification:** Oxford Academic book page (title) and Crossref record (author, publisher; online edition dated 1994, print 1992 per OUP and the Lowe review listing in search).
**Annotation:** Per the Crossref abstract: reliance on others' word plays a central role in the economy of knowledge, previous justifications of testimony rest on a reductive individualism, and linguistic communication itself depends on testimonial reliability. **Bearing:** Supports the idea that trusting a collective source is ordinary, and that a commons is a chain of testimony; relevant to the T2 trust framing.
**Keywords:** testimony, anti-reductionism, transmission chains

### 4. Fricker (2007)
**Citation:** Fricker, M. (2007). *Epistemic injustice: Power and the ethics of knowing*. Oxford University Press. https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780198237907.001.0001

## B. Epistemology of Wikipedia

### 5. Fallis (2008)
**Citation:** Fallis, D. (2008). Toward an epistemology of Wikipedia. *Journal of the American Society for Information Science and Technology, 59*(10), 1662-1674. https://doi.org/10.1002/asi.20870
**Type:** peer-reviewed article
**Verification:** Crossref record with abstract; PhilPapers and Wiley listings appeared in search.
**Annotation:** Abstract only: surveys worries about Wikipedia's reliability and argues its epistemic consequences are likely positive, since accuracy is comparable to traditional encyclopedias and better than free alternatives, with extra virtues (accessibility, speed, comprehensiveness). Calls for clarifying the underlying epistemology.
**Bearing:** The main pro-Wikipedia philosophical case, framed in 2008 as a comparative judgment. This supports T3's point that legitimacy is relative to the alternatives.
**Keywords:** Wikipedia, reliability, veritistic epistemology, comparative

### 6. Magnus (2009)
**Citation:** Magnus, P. D. (2009). On trusting Wikipedia. *Episteme, 6*(1), 74-90. https://doi.org/10.3366/E1742360008000555
**Type:** peer-reviewed article
**Verification:** Cambridge Core abstract page and Crossref record agree on author, title, volume, pages, DOI.
**Annotation:** Abstract only: Wikipedia articles are sometimes quite good but vary a great deal; five strategies we use to assess claims from other sources are, to varying degrees, frustrated by Wikipedia's anonymous collaborative model. Concludes that responsible use needs new epistemic approaches.
**Bearing:** Complicates a pro-Wikipedia reading: the trust is not warranted in the usual ways (author credentials, track record). Useful for T3's claim that legitimacy is conferred by context, not intrinsic.
**Keywords:** trust, anonymity, assessment strategies, Wikipedia

### 7. Sanger (2009)
**Citation:** Sanger, L. M. (2009). The fate of expertise after Wikipedia. *Episteme, 6*(1), 52-73. https://doi.org/10.3366/E1742360008000543
**Type:** peer-reviewed article
**Verification:** Cambridge Core page and Crossref record agree.
**Annotation:** From the page summary: Wikipedia succeeds despite open, bottom-up processes that disclaim expert authority; quality depends on knowledgeable people to whom deference is voluntarily paid, yet anonymity and aggressive contributors drive qualified editors away. Argues expert roles are compatible with open communities. Sanger co-founded Wikipedia and later left, so this is an insider critic.
**Bearing:** Sceptical or opposing source: expert attrition and the anti-elitist culture undercut the "bastion" narrative. Also relevant to T2 (who is driven away from the commons).
**Keywords:** expertise, deference, anonymity, Citizendium

### 8. Wikipedia:Verifiability (policy page)
**Citation:** Wikipedia contributors. (n.d.). Wikipedia:Verifiability. In *Wikipedia*. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Verifiability (retrieved 3 October 2026)
**Type:** primary document (policy page; not peer-reviewed)
**Verification:** Opened the page. Also searched for the legacy slogan page Wikipedia:Verifiability, not truth (appeared in results; not opened).
**Annotation:** The policy states that even if you are sure something is true it must have been published in a reliable source before it can be added, and that verifiability means readers can check that material has been published by a reliable source. The "verifiability, not truth" slogan is described as the historical wording, since superseded.
**Bearing:** Shows what Wikipedia claims to be: a tertiary compiler of published sources, not a source of truth. This sets up T3: the "source of truth" label is an external projection. Cite the revision date you rely on, since the policy text changes.
**Keywords:** verifiability, reliable sources, no original research

### 9. Garfinkel (2008)
**Citation:** Garfinkel, S. L. (2008, October 20). Wikipedia and the meaning of truth. *MIT Technology Review.* https://www.technologyreview.com/2008/10/20/218162/wikipedia-and-the-meaning-of-truth/
**Type:** news-commentary
**Verification:** Opened the MIT Technology Review page (author and date as shown; the print issue was Nov/Dec 2008).
**Annotation:** Argues Wikipedia's standard of verifiability, "not truth", is an appeal to the authority of other publications, not of truth. Illustrates that something counts as established if published in a reputable outlet, and that the system can propagate unchecked sources. Notes Wikipedia's search dominance gives this epistemology reach.
**Bearing:** Sceptical or opposing source for T3: Wikipedia's authority is derivative of the sources it cites, so contamination of those sources (LLM-generated web content) feeds back.
**Keywords:** verifiability, truth, secondary authority, search

## C. Reliability and accuracy studies

### 10. Giles (2005)
**Citation:** Giles, J. (2005). Internet encyclopaedias go head to head. *Nature, 438*(7070), 900-901. https://doi.org/10.1038/438900a
**Type:** peer-reviewed news feature (Nature News)
**Verification:** Crossref record confirms title, author, year, volume, pages. Nature page redirected to a login, so I did not read the text. Figures below come from the Wikipedia project page on the study (secondary), flagged accordingly.
**Annotation:** Per a secondary summary (Wikipedia:External peer review/Nature December 2005): experts reviewed 42 science entries from both encyclopedias; 38 Wikipedia and 40 Britannica entries had at least one error; the headline conclusion was that the difference in accuracy was not particularly great. Treat the numbers as unverified against the original.
**Bearing:** The reference point for a reversal in academic standing: a Nature news feature finding rough parity. Limits: small sample, science topics only, not a peer-reviewed study in the strict sense.
**Keywords:** accuracy, Britannica, expert review, comparison
- **Full-text update (3 Oct 2026, copy supplied by the author):** read in full (pp. 900-901). It is a Nature news-team exercise with expert reviewers blind to source. 42 usable reviews of 50; about four inaccuracies per Wikipedia entry versus about three for Britannica; eight serious errors in total (four each); 162 versus 123 factual errors, omissions or misleading statements; the difference was "not particularly great". Reviewers criticised the structure of several Wikipedia articles. A separate survey of over 1,000 Nature authors: over 70% had heard of Wikipedia, 17% of those consulted it weekly, under 10% helped update it. **The earlier figures above (38 and 40 entries with at least one error) came from a secondary page and do not appear in the report; do not use them.**

### 12. Kräenbring et al. (2014)
**Citation:** Kräenbring, J., Monzon Penza, T., Gutmann, J., Muehlich, S., Zolk, O., Wojnowski, L., Maas, R., Engelhardt, S., & Sarikas, A. (2014). Accuracy and completeness of drug information in Wikipedia: A comparison with standard textbooks of pharmacology. *PLoS ONE, 9*(9), e106930. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0106930
**Type:** peer-reviewed article
**Verification:** Opened the PLOS ONE article page and Crossref record.
**Annotation:** Compared 100 drugs across five categories with pharmacology textbooks: factual accuracy 99.7% (+/-0.2), completeness 83.8% for German and 93.1% for English Wikipedia; pharmacokinetics lowest (~68%). Authors flag limits: textbooks as the gold standard, small sample, undergraduate-level scope.
**Bearing:** A later, narrower accuracy result consistent with T3's narrative of rising credibility, but domain-specific; does not generalise to contested topics.
**Keywords:** accuracy, medicine, completeness, textbooks

### 14. Okoli, Mehdi, Mesgari, Nielsen & Lanamäki (2014)
**Citation:** Okoli, C., Mehdi, M., Mesgari, M., Nielsen, F. Å., & Lanamäki, A. (2014). ### 16. Waters (2007)
**Citation:** Waters, N. L. (2007). Why you can't cite Wikipedia in my class. *Communications of the ACM, 50*(9), 15-17. https://doi.org/10.1145/1284621.1284635
**Type:** news-commentary (CACM Viewpoint)
**Verification:** Crossref record (author, affiliation Middlebury College, year, volume, pages) and ACM/CACM listing in search; CACM page itself returned 403.
**Annotation:** From the Crossref summary: argues Wikipedia's method of compiling information risks conflating facts with popular opinion. Waters is a historian who bars citation of Wikipedia in his class (per the search description).
**Bearing:** Sceptical or opposing source and a primary exhibit of the academic prohibition that T3 says later eased. Note its argument is about citation norms, not accuracy.
**Keywords:** citation policy, academia, popular opinion
- **Full-text update (3 Oct 2026, copy supplied by the author):** read in full (CACM 50(9), 15-17). Waters, a historian at Middlebury College, proposed the department rule after noticing near-identical errors in student examinations that traced to Wikipedia entries. The rule: students are responsible for the accuracy of what they write and cannot point to Wikipedia; Wikipedia is not an acceptable citation though it may lead to a citable source. He argues that scholarship requires accountability, that readers never know whether the last editor was careful or careless, that popularity-driven policing and Google ranking create a self-reinforcing cycle, and that democratising access is conflated with democratising the information itself. He says Wikipedia compares favourably with other tertiary sources in the sciences and is spottier on history (citing Rosenzweig, 2006), that a Foundation representative agreed with the department because Wikipedia is a tertiary source, and that making it citable is impossible for any general tertiary source, including Britannica. His remedy is editor accountability (real names) and more rigorous fact-checking. The earlier summary-only annotation is superseded.

## Gaps and disputes
Philosophical work on Wikipedia clusters in the 2008-2010 Episteme special issue and little has followed on its role as a training corpus; I found nothing here connecting social epistemology to LLM reuse. Accuracy studies are mostly 2005-2015 and domain-specific (science, medicine); I found no recent, broad accuracy comparison. Giles/Britannica remain disputed, and I could read neither original text. "Verifiability, not truth" is discussed mostly in commentary and Wikipedia's own pages, and the slogan has been reworded. The history of academic scepticism rests on faculty surveys and opinion pieces, not a systematic history.
