### Strongest Counter-Argument

The revision has moved the essay from "redescription" to "classification", and the classification is where it now fails. The essay sorts grounds by whether they depend on a cooperative scheme, finds that those that survive are scheme-dependent, and concludes that the best-supported reason is weak and conditional. But "best-supported" is never argued; it is the residue of two screens applied beforehand. Natural duty and beneficence are screened out because "the harm is mild", a premise supported only by Foundation-sourced figures and an author-computed pageview decline of 6.7% and 7.9% that the essay itself says does not show editor erosion. Labour-desert and gratitude are screened out because contributors "gave under a licence of their own choosing", which treats consent to an irrevocable licence as consent to a use (model training) that the essay elsewhere says the licence never contemplated. What is left is fair play, which the essay concedes binds only where a scheme exists, and for the one party the argument is about, AI developers, Section 5.2 says the scheme may never have formed. The conclusion for developers is therefore not "a weak reason" but "no reason of this kind", and the essay then says the reason "asks" developers to build the scheme (6.4). A reason that depends on a scheme's existence cannot also require the scheme's creation; that is a different ground, the very unconditional duty (beneficence or natural duty) screened out earlier. Meanwhile the only specific proposal, carrying the dispute record, is justified by a function the author stipulated, and it addresses retrieval and feeds, whereas the essay's own figures say bulk training share is tiny. The essay has produced a careful taxonomy whose output does not bear on its recommendation.

### 2. Attack on the revised core

**2.1 Conditionality classification.** (a) The axis is single: scheme-dependence. A ground's strength, applicability and contestedness are different axes, and "best-supported" quietly merges them. Fair play is the most contested of the listed grounds (Nozick, Simmons's acceptance requirement, Klosko's indispensability), so the "best-supported" label is applied to a contested ground by default after the others were removed. (b) Section 6.3 says all conditional grounds "share a premise: those who knowingly benefit from a practice that others sustain... have a reason not to leave its upkeep to others." Stated that way the premise is not scheme-dependent; it is a general reciprocity principle, which is what the "No ground" category was supposed to exclude. Either the shared premise is the real ground and the classification is cosmetic, or it is scheme-bound and the sentence overstates. (c) "Mild harm" is the load-bearing screen for the unconditional grounds, yet Section 4.2 calls the infrastructure cost "better evidenced" and the editor-supply cost "not established". The screen is applied to the second and ignored for the first. (d) The Hume/Rawls circumstances-of-justice condition includes moderate scarcity; the essay itself says the text is non-rival. On the essay's own premises the Humean route yields nothing about the text, and the essay moves to the rival "practice" to rescue it. That rescue is reasonable but should be presented as a reconstruction, not as what Hume and Rawls entail.

**2.2 "Best-supported reason is weak and conditional".** The claim has three parts: best-supported, weak, conditional. Only "conditional" is shown by the classification. "Weak" is asserted from thinness of expectations (Section 8: "the weakness here follows the thinness of the expectations in question"), which is the descriptive account again, so the normative strength is read off the sociological fact. That is the C1 problem returning one level up: strength of a reason is made to track strength of expectation, though a reason can be strong where expectations are thin (a newly recognised injustice).

**2.3 Lapse vs non-formation.** The distinction is neat but the evidence for the non-formation side is absent and there is counter-evidence in the essay. (a) The large developers include firms that were search engines and reusers of Wikipedia for years (the essay cites McMahon et al. on the Wikipedia-Google interdependence). Calling developers a population that "never belonged to the reference network" is not shown and is doubtful for exactly those firms. (b) OpenAI's own announced commitment to "provide attribution" (Stack Overflow partnership, Section 4.3) shows developers can hold, at least bargaining-wise, the credit norm; Section 5.1 reads this as a commitment to platforms only, which is possible but is a choice. (c) Hawley's disowning clause cuts both ways, as the essay admits, so the test of non-formation should be "did developers disown", yet the Section 5.5 test is "policies show no prior commitment", which tests absence of a written commitment and cannot separate non-formation from lapse or from tacit-norm-without-writing (the very condition the essay says tacit norms had). (d) The distinction changes what can "fairly be demanded", but Section 6.3 says the essay makes no demand, so the work the distinction does downstream is small.

**2.4 Falsifiability (5.5).** Improvement: yes, four conditions are named. Real exposure: limited. (i) Test 1 (resentment tracks prior expectation of acknowledgement, not lost readership) has no operationalisation of "prior expectation", no direction-of-causation control (resentful people recall higher prior expectation) and does not discriminate from the rival I raised before, status or loss aversion, which also predicts that long-standing, recognition-valuing contributors resent most. A "pass" would not favour the lapse reading over its simplest rival. (ii) Test 2 (adoption grows after codification) has no baseline, window, denominator or threshold; "growing" and "flat or falling" can be met by almost any data series. (iii) Test 3 is conceded in its own sentence to be ambiguous ("codification also occurs in good health"). It reduces to "timing and concentration" with no criterion. (iv) Test 4 is an absence check on documents. (v) "None of these is tested here" is acceptable for an essay, but it means the section expresses intent, not exposure. (vi) No test targets the parts that carry the argument: the classification (6.2), the "weak reason" claim (6.3) or the function claim (3). They are philosophical, and a stricter statement would say which observations would make the author withdraw the recommendation in 7.1 (for example, evidence that readers of generated answers with dispute links do not use them, or that such links raise abuse of editors). As drafted, the account is falsifiable only in its descriptive periphery.

**2.5 Function claim and recommendations.** (a) The function is chosen by the author and the criterion for improving a norm, "that it serves the function that gave it point", is the author's own (6.5). The function is read off the norms, then used to judge them: circular unless an independent warrant is offered. Section 3 gives epistemological support for accountability's value, but not for its being the function, as opposed to free access, coverage, or neutral summary. (b) Magnus (2009), cited as support, argues that Wikipedia frustrates the strategies readers use with other sources; Menking and Rosenberg (2021) is a critique arguing the pillars privilege product over process. Both are used as supports for an account that treats the process as the function. That use deserves a sentence of acknowledgement. (c) Section 7 states the recommendations "do not depend on whether reusers contribute". Then they do not follow from Section 6, the philosophical core; they follow from Section 3 and the conditions of 2.4. The essay thus has two arguments with different conclusions and a connector sentence. (d) 7.2 (newcomer retention) and 7.3 (gender, geography, oral knowledge) are about Wikipedia's internal governance. The link to LLM reuse is through "the first condition" only; 7.3 follows from epistemic justice, not accountable testimony, and the essay disclaims applying Fricker. These are good recommendations in search of this argument. (e) 7.1 is the one specific recommendation, and it has unaddressed problems: privacy and harassment risks from shipping editor names and talk-page disputes in feeds (editors are pseudonymous, talk pages hold personal attacks); the proposal works on retrieval and structured feeds, not pretraining (the essay's own Dolma figure is 0.14%); and nothing shows a generated answer can summarise a dispute faithfully, or that a reader will follow it. Wikimedia Enterprise could ship such a field unilaterally, so the "ask reusers" part is not required by the reason of Section 6. (f) Unexamined premise stays open: Section 7.1 admits "the community that answers for the result" stays behind, and Section 3 defines the third property as a community answering. A transported record preserves provenance and contestability's artefact, not answerability. "Hold the function and release the form" therefore describes a transfer that the essay's own definitions say cannot occur.

**2.6 Buddhist and Humean framing.** The demotion in 6.5 is honest and the right repair. Remaining problems: (a) the title ("Conditioned norms: impermanence...") and the abstract's penultimate sentence still advertise a framing the body says carries no premise, which a reader will take as a promise; (b) Section 8 says "Hume's conventionalism could carry the argument alone", but Hume's artificial virtues are strongly binding once the convention exists, via approbation, so Hume gives no support for "weak"; the weakness is the essay's gloss; (c) the eternalism/annihilationism mapping is described as "loose" and then used to organise 6.2; if loose, drop it, and if used, defend it; (d) the SEP-Madhyamaka "conventional truth" step to "conventional reason" is acknowledged as not derived, so Section 6.5 contributes no support and about 250 words. Delete or move to a footnote.

### Ignored Alternative Explanations/Paths
1. Strength of reason tracks justification, not expectation: a reason can be strong where expectations are thin (newly recognised injustice), so "weak because expectations are thin" is a non sequitur.
2. Labour and rights view: contributors consented to a licence, not to every later use; the licence silence is the grievance. Dispatched in one sentence.
3. Status and loss-aversion explanation of resentment, which makes the same prediction as Test 1.
4. Developers as long-standing reusers (search, Google/Microsoft) who held the credit norm, so the case is lapse, not non-formation.
5. The dispute-record proposal as a Wikimedia product decision, needing no obligation on reusers.
